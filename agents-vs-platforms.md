# Agents Versus Our Platforms — the In-the-Room Narrative

This covers Trip.com, Full Truck Alliance, KE Holdings, MercadoLibre, Sea, Tencent, and TOTVS. The framework is the "Friction Test," plus the eight theories of agentic disruption.

## The narrative — say this

The twenty-second answer. Agents attack interfaces and bundles — but both are just wrappers that sit above the real asset. The businesses we own aren't wrappers; their value is the substrate underneath — live inventory, liquidity, trust, and settlement that an agent can't manufacture. An agent can take our screen or pipe around our API and still has to call that substrate to complete the booking, the freight match, or the home sale. Agents eat wrappers; we own substrate. The market has applied an indiscriminate "AI discount" to all of them, and we're using it to add.

Reframe before you defend. The eight-theory disruption framework was written for software-as-a-service — software sitting on top of a workflow. Run our names through it and six of the eight simply don't bite: unbundling, the zero-dollar minimum viable product, compound engineering, and "the end of the seat" only kill things you can clone cheaply. You can vibe-code a Trip.com look-alike in a weekend; you cannot vibe-code 1.7 million accommodation listings, 4.6 million verified truckers, or about 550,000 property agents. The one theory actually in our share prices is the "Uncertainty Tax" — sound businesses de-rated because the market can't model year five. That is opportunity, not impairment.

## How agents capture value — the two-by-two

Two things get attacked — interfaces, where the value is in the screen; and bundles, where the value is in the packaging — by two mechanisms: replace it, or reduce it to a pipe. Every example here is a business whose value lived in the wrapper, which is exactly what an agent, being a better wrapper, can take. Picture a two-by-two grid.

Take the interface, where value is the screen. Replaced outright: Chegg — ChatGPT just does the homework, so the wrapper loses its reason to exist. Reduced to a pipe: mobile carriers — WhatsApp and iMessage ride on top; Verizon and AT&T still carry every message but become commodity data pipes.

Now the bundle, where value is the packaging. Replaced outright: Adobe — AI lets you make just the one piece you need instead of renting the whole Creative Cloud suite. Reduced to a pipe: Microsoft Office — an assistant writes the doc, builds the slides, and updates the sheet by driving the apps underneath; you stop opening them.

Three of these are already visible — Chegg, mobile carriers, Adobe; the Office case is emerging — and it's Microsoft's own stated worry, so it's credible, not speculative.

The turn: our holdings sit in none of these boxes, because their value was never in a wrapper. It's in the substrate underneath. An agent can replace Trip.com's screen or pipe around its API and still has to call the 1.7 million listings, the 4.6 million truckers, the roughly 550,000-agent network — plus the trust and settlement rails — to actually transact. Nothing of value moves.

## The Friction Test — the pivot

The question is not "can an agent imitate the front-end?" It is: does taking the user out of the platform make the task cheaper, easier, or more reliable — or does it just relocate the friction? For our holdings it relocates it, because discovery is the product — users refine as they go: dates, price, reviews, availability — and because the value sits below the checkout button: inventory, logistics, payments, credit, trust. And our companies aren't standing still — they build their own agents, like Trip.com's Genie and Tencent's, so where an agent layer emerges, they tend to own it. The likely outcome is adaptation, not disintermediation.

## The triage — how to place any platform in six steps

First, the commoditisation test. Can a model just do the core task from its own training data, with no proprietary input? If yes, it faces direct-replacement risk, like Chegg. If it needs the platform's live or proprietary asset, it's safe from this vector.

Second, the strip-the-screen test. Remove the interface — what's left that's hard to rebuild? If nothing but the screen, it's exposed. If live inventory, liquidity, or governed data or a network, the moat is intact.

Third, the discovery test. Is intent fixed at the outset, or is discovery part of the product? A fixed, bounded instruction is agent-friendly; an iterative, comparison-led journey is not — the journey is the transaction.

Fourth, the below-the-button test. After the decision, is there heavy fulfilment — inventory, payments, credit, logistics, trust, settlement? A thin hand-off is exposed. A thick one means value sits below and the agent must call it.

Fifth, the cold-start test. Is it a two-sided network? Can an agent rebuild both sides at once? If liquidity is the asset, an agent can't conjure it, so it's safe.

Sixth, the ownership and adaptation test. Does the incumbent own its own agent, control the API terms, and deploy AI itself? If yes, it captures the upside rather than ceding it.

## Where the book lands, on AI-disruption risk

Trip.com, a travel online travel agent — rated green. Discovery is the journey; 1.7 million listings, cross-border payments, twenty-four-seven service; it owns its agent, Genie. Real watch-item: top-of-funnel.

Full Truck Alliance, a freight network — green. Its value is the liquidity of about 4.6 million truckers and 3.1 million shippers, with deterministic trust rails. An agent can't conjure a truck. Watch-item: take-rate and regulation.

KE Holdings, property transactions — green. Physical, trust-maximal; the housing dictionary plus the Agent Cooperation Network of 61,000 stores and about 550,000 agents. An agent front-ends the easy 5 percent. Watch-item: the property cycle.

MercadoLibre, Latin American e-commerce — green. Marketplace plus Envios logistics plus Pago payments and credit. Not a checkout button; discovery-led. Watch-item: top-of-funnel.

Sea, which owns Shopee, in Southeast-Asian e-commerce — amber. Shopee logistics plus SeaMoney; value below the button. Fragmented geography resists convergence. Watch-item: competition and margin.

Tencent, a super-app — green. Weixin is the identity and payments substrate others plug into. It's building its own agents. Watch-item: the AI ad and search mix.

TOTVS, embedded ERP — green. It's a system of record — payroll, tax, compliance. AI means more work through it; seats become outcomes. Watch-item: the monetisation shift.

Note: every name is green or amber on AI-disintermediation. The ambers and the watch-items are conventional risks — competition, macro, monetisation — not agents taking the value.

## The platforms, one by one

### Trip.com — travel, an online travel agent, a discovery marketplace

The bear case: "you'll just tell an agent to book the trip" — a superagent eats the online travel agent.

The friction verdict: discovery is the journey — users weigh dates, price, reviews, and availability in real time. You can't commoditise a hotel room; 1.7 million accommodation listings, over 600 airlines across more than 3,400 airports, multi-currency payments, and twenty-four-seven multilingual service all sit below the button.

The killer line: eighteen months after capable agents shipped, OpenAI's Instant Checkout has unwound — Walmart pulled out in March 2026 for wrong items in carts, Etsy left for lack of sales. Meanwhile Trip.com built Genie and owns its own agent layer.

The watch-item: top-of-funnel discovery and brand queries migrating to large language models — that's traffic sourcing, not the transaction.

### Full Truck Alliance — digital freight matching, a two-sided liquidity network — the strongest case

The bear case: an agent matches loads to trucks and disintermediates the platform.

The friction verdict: the asset is liquidity, not an app — 236 million orders fulfilled in 2025 across about 3.1 million monthly active shippers and about 4.6 million active truckers. Match quality is a function of network density; a perfect interface can't summon a truck that isn't on the network. Verified capacity, credit, settlement, insurance, and dispute resolution are deterministic, trust-heavy rails.

The killer line: to disintermediate, an agent must rebuild both sides of the network at once — a cold-start problem AI does nothing to solve. Full Truck Alliance is itself the natural home to deploy matching AI.

The watch-item: take-rate and regulation — not agent risk.

### KE Holdings, also known as Beike — property transactions, a physical and governed-data network

The bear case: an agent handles the home search and cuts out the platform.

The friction verdict: the highest-friction, most physical transaction there is — viewings, verification, financing, escrow, closing, where tacit judgement dominates. The moat is a system of record, not a screen: the "housing dictionary" of standardised property data, plus the Agent Cooperation Network linking about 61,000 stores and about 550,000 agents.

The killer line: an agent can help shortlist; it can't tour the flat, verify title, arrange the mortgage, or hold the escrow. It front-ends the easy 5 percent and leaves KE owning the hard, valuable 95 percent.

The watch-item: the China property cycle — macro, not AI.

### MercadoLibre — Latin American e-commerce, an integrated ecosystem

The bear case: agentic checkout reduces the marketplace to a fulfilment layer.

The friction verdict: not a thin shopping interface — an operating system of marketplace plus Mercado Envios logistics plus Mercado Pago payments and credit. Discovery is led inside the platform, and proprietary data underwrites credit in ways banks struggle to match. A large part of the value sits below discovery.

The killer line: you can't reduce MercadoLibre to the checkout button — payments, credit, and logistics deepen engagement and monetisation even if discovery evolves at the margin.

The watch-item: top-of-funnel discovery, plus conventional competition and margin — not AI disintermediation.

### Sea — Shopee and SeaMoney — Southeast-Asian e-commerce, an integrated ecosystem

The bear case: the same e-commerce disintermediation story as MercadoLibre.

The friction verdict: the same pattern — Shopee's logistics network plus SeaMoney payments, credit, and financial services across the ecosystem. Southeast Asia is structurally hard: fragmented merchants, local logistics, execution-heavy — which resists the brutal one-winner convergence China conditioned investors to expect.

The killer line: the relevant question is whether today's spend is offensive — deepening logistics and habit while weaker rivals struggle — not whether an agent removes the platform.

The watch-item: competition keeping margins lower for longer — conventional, not AI. That's why it's amber overall.

### Tencent — the Weixin ecosystem, games, ads, cloud — a super-app substrate

The bear case: agents route around the app layer and skim the interface.

The friction verdict: Weixin is the identity, distribution, and payments substrate that other services must plug into — not an interface you can bypass without losing the ecosystem. Tencent is deploying its own agents inside the ecosystem, adapting from a position of ownership of both the graph and the agent layer.

The killer line: the agent that tries to go around Tencent still needs Weixin's users, payments, and mini-program rails to actually do anything at scale.

The watch-item: how AI reshapes search and ads within the ecosystem — mostly upside.

### TOTVS — Brazilian ERP — an embedded system of record, the software case

The bear case: AI lets customers build and orchestrate workflows themselves, so seats — and the vendor — fall away.

The friction verdict: not a generic interface — it's embedded in accounting, payroll, tax, inventory, and compliance, where data is structured, actions have consequences, and reliability beats elegance. AI changes how users interact, but not the need for a governed system of record holding permissions, audit trails, and compliance logic.

The killer line: if AI lets a customer run the same business with fewer people, more work flows through the system, not less — value migrates to the workflow, and pricing moves from seats toward usage and outcomes — "Task as a Service."

The watch-item: the seats-to-usage monetisation transition won't be frictionless — execution risk, not structural decline.

## Close — land it

Distinguish businesses where AI genuinely alters industry structure from those where the effect is being extrapolated too broadly. Ours keep growing free cash flow, keep buying back stock, and hold competitive positions that are intact — in some cases stronger — yet they've de-rated. The setup doesn't need a re-rating to work; it needs these businesses to keep doing what they're doing. Agents eat wrappers; we own substrate. Tough times never last, but tough companies do.
