# Qualitas — The Seven Questions

Qualitas is the twenty-fourth-largest position in the GEM portfolio at one-and-a-half percent, with a market cap of around four to five billion US dollars. Here is Qualitas in seven questions.

---

## Cold open

Qualitas just printed its first-quarter 2026 results. Written premiums grew fifteen-point-three percent year-on-year to twenty-one-point-seven billion pesos. The combined ratio was ninety-point-two percent — meaningfully below the company's long-term target of ninety-two to ninety-four percent. The quarterly return on equity was twenty-three-point-seven percent, and the trailing twelve-month return on equity was sixteen-point-eight percent. Solvency stood at three hundred and fourteen percent of the regulatory minimum. Market share in Mexican auto insurance held at thirty-four percent. A dividend of nine pesos per share representing a seventy-one percent payout ratio, and an eight-hundred-million-peso share buyback fund, are being proposed to the April shareholders' meeting. And over the past three years, more than ten-point-nine billion pesos of dividends have been distributed to shareholders.

This is the dominant Mexican auto insurer, compounding through a Mexican macro cycle that is actively destabilising lesser operators.

Seven questions.

---

## Question One: What is it?

Qualitas is the number-one auto insurance company in Mexico by written premiums, with a thirty-four percent market share. It is a monoline insurer — meaning it writes almost exclusively automobile insurance — across multiple segments, plus a growing Latin American subsidiary footprint.

The segment mix is deliberate. The traditional retail segment represents sixty-six percent of written premiums and grew thirteen percent in the first quarter. The fleet segment and the financial institution segment each contribute roughly thirty percent of written premiums, and both grew over twenty-five percent year-on-year. The individual segment grew nearly four percent. Foreign subsidiaries — including Latin American operations in countries like Costa Rica, Peru, and El Salvador — declined modestly in peso terms but grew forty-two percent in US-dollar terms excluding foreign-exchange effects. The US business, by contrast, declined seventy-seven percent as Qualitas deliberately exited unprofitable US programmes to focus on profitability.

The revenue structure is straightforward insurance mechanics. Earned premiums were nineteen billion pesos in the quarter, up twelve percent. Claims ratio was sixty-three percent overall, with Mexico at sixty-one percent. Acquisition costs were just under five billion pesos, with an acquisition ratio of twenty-three percent. Comprehensive financial income — investment returns on the technical reserves float — was one-point-two billion pesos, down twenty-three percent on lower Mexican interest rates. Technical reserves reached sixty-nine-and-a-half billion pesos, up eight billion year-on-year.

The thing to internalise: Qualitas is not a diversified insurer. It is the most disciplined auto insurance underwriter in the Mexican market, with two-and-a-half decades of concentrated underwriting track record, a thirty-four-percent market share that gives pricing data advantages no competitor can match, and a capital structure that funds aggressive shareholder returns.

## Question Two: Can we trust them?

Yes — and the capital-allocation discipline through the recent VAT regulatory change plus the United States exit is the clearest evidence.

José Antonio Correa is the Chief Executive Officer. The founding Hermano family has a long-standing and well-aligned ownership position. Disclosure quality is high and consistent.

The past-stress case that matters is the 2024-to-2025 Mexican VAT regulatory transition. A change in value-added-tax rules on auto repair parts materially raised claim-cost inflation for every Mexican auto insurer. Lesser operators absorbed the pressure by either under-reserving, cutting prices irrationally, or pulling back from loss-making segments late. Qualitas implemented disciplined price increases to offset the VAT impact, protected the combined ratio, and used the disruption to deepen its market-share advantage. The first quarter 2026 combined ratio at ninety-point-two — well below the long-term target — is the proof that the pricing discipline translated to margin protection despite the underlying cost-inflation pressure.

The capital discipline signals from the latest print are straightforward. Combined ratio below target at ninety-point-two percent. Trailing twelve-month return on equity at sixteen-point-eight percent. Solvency ratio at three hundred and fourteen percent — deeply over-capitalised. Dividend proposal of nine pesos per share representing a seventy-one percent payout ratio, meaning the majority of profit is returned to shareholders. New share buyback fund for eight hundred million pesos being proposed. Share buyback fund already held seven hundred million pesos at quarter-end with six-point-one million shares in treasury. Over ten-point-nine billion pesos of dividends distributed over the past three years. And the deliberate US exit is a quality signal — management chose profitability over scale when the US book was underperforming.

The watch item is succession depth. The management team has been stable for years; explicit succession planning at the CEO level and depth of leadership below are worth tracking.

## Question Three: Why does value compound?

Three engines, plus a kicker.

The first engine is Mexican auto insurance penetration. Mexico remains significantly under-insured by global standards. New vehicle sales in Mexico grew two percent year-on-year, with light vehicles up and heavy vehicles facing headwinds. Every additional registered vehicle is a potential Qualitas policy, and Qualitas has the sales and service infrastructure to capture a disproportionate share of new registrations. Traditional segment growth of thirteen percent well exceeds nominal GDP growth.

The second engine is the specialty and institutional segments. Fleet and financial-institution-channel premiums each grew more than twenty-five percent in the quarter. These are higher-margin segments that require deep underwriting capability, balance-sheet strength, and servicing infrastructure — characteristics that favour the scale leader and compound over time as the market matures.

The third engine is the investment float on the technical reserves. Technical reserves rose from sixty-two billion to sixty-nine-and-a-half billion pesos year-on-year. Even with Mexican interest rates below their peak, the float generates meaningful investment income that compounds alongside the underwriting profit. Mexican rates, while declining from highs, remain attractive in global terms; any rate stability or re-acceleration adds to the compounding engine.

The kicker is the capital return. A seventy-one-percent dividend payout ratio plus an ongoing share buyback fund means the company is returning the majority of its cash generation to shareholders. On a mid-teens-percent return on equity, with compounding share-count reduction, per-share book value growth is structurally higher than reported earnings growth suggests. The ten-point-nine billion pesos of dividends distributed over three years is the tangible manifestation of that capital-return discipline.

## Question Four: What proves us right, and what to track?

Six specific metrics.

First, combined ratio — ninety-point-two percent in the first quarter, guidance upper end of ninety-two to ninety-four for the year; maintenance below ninety-four confirms the underwriting discipline. Second, written-premium growth — fifteen percent in the quarter, guidance high-single to low-double digit for the year; sustained double-digit confirms share gain. Third, trailing twelve-month return on equity — sixteen-point-eight percent today, target around twenty percent for the year; movement toward twenty is the quality signal. Fourth, solvency ratio — three hundred and fourteen percent; maintenance above two hundred and fifty confirms capital strength. Fifth, dividend and buyback execution — the nine-peso dividend and eight-hundred-million-peso buyback fund through 2026; continuation signals ongoing capital-return commitment. Sixth, Mexican market share — thirty-four percent; continued share gain confirms competitive advantage.

## Question Five: What's it worth?

The honest framing. Full-year expected earnings depend on execution, but the first-quarter run-rate implies a trailing non-GAAP net income in the range of six-and-a-half to seven billion pesos, or roughly three hundred and fifty million US dollars. Market cap is around four to five billion US dollars. That implies a trailing price-to-earnings in the low-to-mid teens on current-quarter earnings.

The simple framing: a dominant auto insurance monoline with thirty-four percent market share, twenty-percent-target return on equity, seventy-one-percent dividend payout, and a share buyback fund, trading in the low-teens earnings multiple. Mexican insurance peers trade at similar-or-higher multiples on weaker market positions. Global auto insurance comparables — Progressive in the US, Geico via Berkshire — trade at substantially higher multiples for similar returns on equity and broader geographic diversification.

The key sensitivity is Mexican macro and FX. A sharper Mexican recession or significant peso weakness compresses US-dollar reported earnings. Second sensitivity is VAT and regulatory evolution — further tax or regulatory change affecting claims costs would test underwriting discipline.

## Question Six: Where is the market wrong?

Two variant perception angles.

First: Qualitas is priced as a Mexican small-cap insurer with Mexico-macro-dependent revenue, when the operating track record through the 2024-to-2025 VAT cycle proves the company has genuine pricing power and underwriting discipline that compound independent of macro. Global insurance compounders with similar return on equity and similar market-leadership positions trade at materially higher multiples.

Second: the capital-return programme is priced as an income yield rather than as a compounding lever. Ten-point-nine billion pesos of dividends over three years on a market cap that moves with Mexican equity sentiment means compounding share-count reduction and per-share book value growth that are not fully reflected in the headline multiple.

The honest counter: Mexican equity markets carry persistent illiquidity and governance discounts that do not close easily. The VAT regulatory cycle demonstrates that regulatory risk is real. And the US exit, while a discipline signal, also represents lost diversification optionality.

## Question Seven: What could kill it, and when do we sell?

The real risks, ranked.

One: Mexican macro recession. A sharper slowdown in new vehicle sales or broader consumer credit would compress premium growth. Two: regulatory or tax change. Further VAT or regulatory evolution raising claims costs without price offset. Three: competitive escalation. GNP, MetLife Mexico, or other peers pushing aggressively on pricing to challenge Qualitas's share. Four: investment-income compression. Deeper Banxico rate cuts compressing the float-return engine. Five: peso volatility affecting foreign subsidiaries and US-dollar reported returns. Six: succession. Stable management team but explicit succession planning below Correa is a multi-year watch item.

Specific sell triggers. Combined ratio breaking above ninety-six percent for two consecutive quarters. Return on equity falling below fifteen percent for two consecutive quarters without a clear one-off. Market share declining meaningfully for two consecutive quarters. Dividend payout ratio cut below fifty percent without a clear capital-allocation rationale. A material adverse regulatory event in Mexican auto insurance. José Antonio Correa stepping back without a named credible successor.
