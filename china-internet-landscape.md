The China Internet Landscape — A Listener's Map

Welcome. The goal of this briefing is simple. By the end, you should be able to picture the whole Chinese internet on a single mental map, and know where Alibaba sits on it. Don't try to memorise numbers. Just hold onto the shape.

Here is the shape, in one sentence. The Chinese internet is two old empires being attacked by three insurgents, across five battlegrounds. The two empires are Alibaba and Tencent. The three insurgents are Pinduoduo, Meituan, and ByteDance. And Alibaba, as we'll see, is fighting on nearly every battleground at once.

Let's meet the two empires first.

The first empire is Alibaba. Think of Alibaba as the original e-commerce giant. Its heart is two enormous shopping marketplaces, Taobao and Tmall, where hundreds of millions of Chinese consumers have shopped for over a decade. But Alibaba is more than shopping. It also runs the largest cloud computing business in China — the data centres and computing power that other companies rent. And it is now betting heavily on artificial intelligence, with its own family of A.I. models called Qwen, as the next engine of growth. So when you picture Alibaba, picture a sprawling empire: shopping at its core, cloud computing as its growth story, and a web of other businesses around the edges — logistics, food delivery, maps, and overseas shopping apps.

The second empire is Tencent. Now, here's the thing to understand about Tencent: it is not really a shop. Tencent's crown jewel is WeChat — a single app that more than a billion Chinese people live inside every day. WeChat is messaging, social media, mini-apps, and payments all in one. On top of WeChat, Tencent runs the largest video games business on Earth, and it owns one half of China's payments system, called WeChat Pay. What's clever about Tencent is that it rarely builds new businesses from scratch. Instead, it invests. Tencent owns big stakes in many of the other players we'll discuss — including two of the three insurgents. So picture Tencent as the empire that owns the town square everyone gathers in, and quietly holds shares in half the shops around it.

Now the three insurgents — the younger, hungrier attackers.

The first insurgent is Pinduoduo. Pinduoduo is the cheap-and-cheerful challenger. It grew explosively by offering rock-bottom prices and group buying, and in doing so it stole e-commerce market share away from Alibaba on its home turf. But the bigger story today is Pinduoduo's overseas app, called Temu — that's spelled T, E, M, U. Temu has exploded across the United States and Europe, selling bargain goods shipped direct from China. So Pinduoduo is two things at once: a share-taker at home, and a fast-growing disruptor abroad.

The second insurgent is Meituan. Meituan is the king of what's called local services. If something happens in your neighbourhood — ordering a meal, booking a restaurant, reserving a hotel room — Meituan wants that order. It dominates food delivery in China. Think of Meituan as the company that owns the everyday, physical, around-the-corner economy, the way Alibaba owns the parcel-in-the-post economy.

The third insurgent is ByteDance. This is the most important name that most people outside China under-rate. ByteDance is the parent of TikTok, and inside China it runs TikTok's sister app, called Douyin. Now, ByteDance is private — it is not listed on any stock market — but it is pivotal. Why? Because Douyin's short videos now sell goods and food directly inside the feed. People watch a video, and buy without ever visiting a shopping app. That makes ByteDance an attacker on two fronts at once — it threatens Alibaba's shopping business and Meituan's local-services business simultaneously. It is the wildcard on the board.

So those are the five players. Two empires, Alibaba and Tencent. Three insurgents, Pinduoduo, Meituan, and ByteDance. Now let's lay down the five battlegrounds where they collide.

Battleground one is e-commerce — online shopping. This is Alibaba's home turf, and it's now a four-way brawl. The deep shift to understand is this: people used to search for products, typing what they wanted into a search box. That behaviour favoured Alibaba. But increasingly, people discover products — they stumble on them in a video feed or chase a bargain. That shift favours ByteDance and Pinduoduo. So Alibaba is defending its oldest, most profitable territory against newer ways of shopping.

Battleground two is local services and food delivery. Here Meituan is the clear leader. Alibaba is the number two, through its food-delivery arm. And once again, ByteDance is muscling in with video. So Alibaba is fighting here too — as a challenger this time, not the leader.

Battleground three is payments. This one is a stable two-way split. Almost every transaction in China runs through one of two systems: Alipay, which belongs to Alibaba's affiliate, or WeChat Pay, which belongs to Tencent. A comfortable duopoly, hard for anyone else to break into.

Battleground four is cloud computing and artificial intelligence. This is Alibaba's great hope. Alibaba's cloud business leads in China, and the race to build the best A.I. models — Alibaba's Qwen, alongside rivals like DeepSeek and Tencent's models — is the hottest growth story in the whole sector. This is where Alibaba wants the conversation to be.

Battleground five is going global. Almost everyone is pushing overseas, because the home market is saturated and tightly regulated. Pinduoduo has Temu. ByteDance has TikTok Shop. Alibaba has its overseas apps like AliExpress and Lazada. Even Meituan is expanding abroad. Growth abroad is the shared obsession.

So step back and look at the map. Of those five battlegrounds, Alibaba is fighting on four of them. That is the single most important thing to carry into your meeting. Alibaba is not a focused company attacking one prize — it is an empire defending many borders at once. And defending borders costs money.

That leads to the tensions worth probing on Thursday. First: defending its core shopping business against Pinduoduo and ByteDance costs a great deal — is that spending actually working, or just slowing the bleeding? Second: how real is the cloud and A.I. growth story, and how much are they spending to chase it? Third: cash returns — Alibaba has been buying back a lot of its own stock, and that's central to the case for owning it. Fourth: the backdrop of a cautious Chinese consumer hangs over everything. And fifth, quietly in the room: the memory of the government crackdown on big tech a few years ago, which seems to have eased, but never quite leaves.

That's the map. Two empires, three insurgents, five battlegrounds, and an Alibaba fighting almost everywhere. Hold that shape in your head, and every fact you learn over the next two days will have somewhere to hang. Good luck on Thursday.
