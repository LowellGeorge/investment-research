# Asia Pitch Book, August 2026

Seventy-two lines, six mechanisms. A pitch book: what each company is, why it is here, and what would make us wrong. Weights are from the workbook of the twenty-seventh of August, 2026.

Seventy-two lines, of which fifty-three carry weight and nineteen are stubs — positions displayed at zero percent, the scraps of things sold down or the first bricks of things being built. The fifty-three are organised here into six groups, each one a single mechanism its members share, so that remembering one name pulls the others along behind it. The transcribed weights sum to 99.7 percent and the remainder is rounding.

The same two ideas underneath as always. Businesses run by people who own them — founders, families, and the occasional exemplary professional house — because alignment is the cheapest form of governance. And Morris Chang's rule, which describes most of the winners here: connect harder with the customer in the downturn, and never lord it over them in the boom.

## The six groups

Each group is one mechanism its members share. Remember the mechanism and the names follow.

## The silicon spine, 31.1 percent

TSMC, Samsung, MediaTek, SK Hynix, Realtek, Silergy, and the ASMPT stub. One press — TSMC — and the chips that pass through it: the two Korean memory houses fighting over what sits next to the GPU, and three Taiwanese fabless designers living off the press next door. The shared mechanism is a priced queue — when AI demand exceeds wafer, packaging and memory capacity, whoever controls the queue takes the margin. The shared risk is the cycle, which can reverse pricing inside a quarter, and nearly a third of the portfolio stands on it.

## The electrified workshop, 16.5 percent

Lite-On, Sunny Optical, XCMG, Techtronic, Mindray, Tripod, CATL, AirTAC, Delta, Inovance, Voltronic, Quanta, Fuyao, Hongfa, Luxshare, Advantech, and the ELGi stub. The factory floor of the energy transition and the AI datacentre: power supplies, servers, circuit boards, batteries, relays, pneumatics, motor drives, glass and optics. None of these is a household name; all of them sell the physical components without which the famous companies cannot ship. The shared mechanism is the specialist's catalogue — thousands of parts, qualified into customer designs, hard to displace once in. The shared risk is that they sit mid-supply-chain, taking volume from above and price pressure from below. Mindray is filed with the Chinese consumer group below; it belongs to both rooms.

## Banking a billion savers, 22.4 percent

ICICI Bank, AIA, OCBC, Kotak, HDFC Bank, Bank Central Asia, KB Financial, Bank of the Philippine Islands, Kasikorn, Bajaj Finance, Niva Bupa, DBS and ICICI Lombard. Asian households are moving money out of gold, property and the mattress into deposits, policies and funds, and every name here is a toll booth on that flow: Indian private banks taking share from the state, Southeast Asia's deposit franchises, Korea's shareholder-return story, and AIA writing life policies across the whole region. The shared mechanism is a trusted licence compounding a low-cost deposit or float. The shared risk is credit — the cycle arrives eventually in every one of these markets, usually just after everyone has stopped pricing for it.

## The Chinese consumer, second act, 18.7 percent

Tencent, Midea, H World, NetEase, Mindray, ANTA, Trip.com, PDD, Full Truck Alliance, and the KE Holdings and Atour stubs. China's consumer economy in its cautious middle age: the defaults a fragmented market consolidated onto — Tencent, PDD, Trip.com — the branded manufacturers that survived the shakeout — Midea, ANTA, Mindray — and the asset-light service networks, H World and Full Truck. The shared mechanism is scale rewarding whoever becomes the default; the improvement since 2021 is that the owners now return the cash — buybacks and dividends are the norm across this group. The shared risk remains the regulator's view of what a default may charge.

## Houses and handovers, 7.2 percent

LG Corp, Sony, Astra, Mahindra and Mahindra, Jardine Matheson, Nintendo, plus the Bajaj Auto and Jardine Cycle and Carriage stubs and the consumer stubs — DFI, Unilever Indonesia, Colgate India, Century Pacific, Philippine Seven, Selamat Sempurna and Mobile World. Family and founder houses in the middle of generational handovers — Korean holding companies closing their discounts, Japanese creators monetising their archives, and the Jardine and Tata-adjacent style conglomerates of Southeast Asia simplifying after decades of sprawl. The shared mechanism is a discount to the sum of parts plus an owner newly motivated to close it. The shared risk is that handovers can stall, and a discount with no catalyst is just a cheap thing that stays cheap.

## The Anzac wing, 3.3 percent

ResMed, Fisher and Paykel Healthcare, BHP and CSL. Four Australasian listings: three healthcare device and plasma franchises selling into the world's ageing lungs and immune systems, and one iron-ore major that is effectively a bet on Asian construction run from Melbourne. The mechanism they share is a listed-in-the-Pacific claim on global demand with governance you can sleep through. The risk is that none of them is cheap, and BHP answers to the Chinese property cycle.

## Country views

What to remember about each place, in one paragraph.

Taiwan, 26.5 percent. Eleven names — the deepest country exposure and the most concentrated skill base on earth: the leading-edge press, TSMC; the designers next door, MediaTek, Realtek and Silergy; and the workshop tier of power, boards and servers — Delta, Lite-On, Voltronic, Tripod, Quanta, Advantech and AirTAC. The macro is benign; the political risk is the one everyone names and nobody can price, so it is managed by sizing rather than forecast. What to remember: Taiwan is the ground the AI trade stands on, and its best company's humility with customers is why they do not try to leave.

China, including the Hong Kong listings, 27.2 percent. Seventeen names and none of them property developers or state banks. Our China splits into the consumer defaults — Tencent, PDD, Trip.com, H World, ANTA, NetEase — and the industrial champions the electrification decade produced: CATL, Midea, Inovance, XCMG, Fuyao, Sunny, Hongfa, Luxshare. The economy is deflationary and the consumer cautious, which favours value formats and exporters of engineered goods. Capital returns have improved beyond recognition since 2021. What to remember: we own China's defaults and its best factories, and we trust the owners because they have started handing back the cash.

South Korea, 16 percent. Memory — Samsung and Hynix — plus the corporate-governance thaw, LG Corp and KB Financial. Korea gave markets their most violent summer moves — Hynix down by a third in won as leveraged retail was carried out — and their most interesting slow story: the Value-Up programme pushing holding companies and banks to shrink discounts, lift payouts and retire treasury shares. What to remember: Korea is two trades in one country — the most cyclical product in technology, and the cheapest well-run financial assets in Asia slowly being allowed to pay their owners.

India, 11.7 percent. Seven names, five of them financial. The engine is household formalisation — savings moving from gold into deposits, insurance and funds — and the banks, insurers and lenders here are toll booths on that flow. Mahindra adds the capex and rural-demand side: tractors and SUVs. Indian quality is never cheap and the market's dollar returns sagged this summer, which is when adding gets interesting. What to remember: buy the toll booth rather than the road, and keep sizes moderate because the tolls were priced early.

Hong Kong's regional platforms, 4.4 percent. AIA and Jardine Matheson — two colonial-era institutions that outgrew the colony. AIA writes life insurance from Shanghai to Jakarta with the agency force as the moat; Jardines is mid-simplification, collapsing cross-holdings and selling the periphery. What to remember: both are claims on Asian household wealth accumulation wearing Hong Kong listings.

Singapore, 3.2 percent. OCBC and DBS — the region's safest balance sheets, paying out generously as rates normalise, with wealth-management franchises gathering the money that Asia's rich keep moving to Singapore. What to remember: Singapore banks are the utility layer of Asian wealth, dull by design.

Indonesia, 3.1 percent. Bank Central Asia and Astra: the country's best deposit franchise and its dominant car-and-everything conglomerate. A young population, an under-banked economy, and a market that punishes governance lapses harshly — which is why we hold the two cleanest houses. What to remember: Indonesia is the demographic argument, expressed through its two most conservative institutions.

Japan, 2.1 percent. Sony and Nintendo — two archives of beloved intellectual property learning, at different speeds, to charge for them properly. Governance reform has made Japanese managers spend buybacks like they mean it. What to remember: Japan here is content, not macro.

Thailand and the Philippines, 1.9 percent. Kasikorn and Bank of the Philippine Islands: bank franchises in two under-penetrated credit markets at opposite points of the cycle — Thailand working through household debt, the Philippines still early in formalisation with the demographics of India a generation ago. What to remember: small weights, long runways.

Australia and New Zealand, 3.3 percent. ResMed, Fisher and Paykel, CSL and BHP. Three healthcare franchises with global reach and one resources major cabled into Chinese steel demand. What to remember: the Anzac wing is the portfolio's claim on ageing lungs, plasma and iron ore, with Pacific governance.

Vietnam and the residual stubs, 0.3 percent. FPT, Mobile World, Sea, KE Holdings, Atour, Cognizant and a dozen others sit at zero percent — scraps of exits or the first bricks of entries. They are listed at the back for completeness; none moves the portfolio.

## The pitches

Two to three minutes each: the line, the business, the reason, the worry. Ordered by group, then weight. First, the silicon spine — chips, 31.1 percent of the portfolio.

## Taiwan Semiconductor

9.6 percent. Taiwan. Everyone else in the AI trade is arguing about who wins. TSMC is the ground they are all standing on.

Picture a printing press so precise it lays down features a few dozen atoms wide, and a queue at the door that includes Nvidia, Apple, AMD, MediaTek and, on bad days, Intel. TSMC does not design chips. It makes other people's designs real, and at the leading edge it is now effectively the only shop open. Samsung and Intel keep announcing they will catch up; customers keep sending wafers to Hsinchu.

The moat has two layers. The first is the physics: each node costs more to develop than the last, and only enormous volume amortises it. The second is the culture Morris Chang built — humility toward customers, measured price rises, connect harder in the downturn. Customers keep coming back rather than plotting their escape.

Advanced packaging — the CoWoS process — has become a second moat: AI accelerators are assembled by stacking dies and memory onto interposers, and TSMC controls the capacity the whole industry queues for.

What would worry us? Geopolitics cannot be timed or priced, so we manage it with sizing rather than forecasts. The useful worry is cyclical: capital intensity keeps rising, and if AI capex pauses, the depreciation doesn't.

## Samsung Electronics, preference shares

8.5 percent. South Korea. The giant that lost the high-bandwidth memory race and is now paid to be the second source.

Samsung is the only company making memory, logic foundry, phones, screens and appliances under one roof. For a decade the breadth looked like strength; then high-bandwidth memory — HBM — arrived, and the profit engine found itself behind a much smaller neighbour on the one product that mattered, its bonding process cracking at twelve layers while qualification with Nvidia dragged.

The case now is the second source. No customer wants one supplier of a component this critical, and the sixteen-layer HBM4 generation is a fresh start where Samsung's scale and cash matter. The foundry business remains a call option that has so far been mostly cost.

We hold the preference shares — same cash flows, no vote, cheaper ticket — and the position is the largest Korea exposure in the portfolio because the downside is protected by a fortress balance sheet and a valuation that assumes little.

What would worry us? Governance: the controlling family's priorities have not always matched minorities'. And a third straight HBM generation lost to Hynix would turn the second-source thesis into a permanent silver medal.

## MediaTek

5.5 percent. Taiwan. The chip designer for the phone in the other five billion pockets — and now a back door into AI silicon.

Qualcomm designs the processor for the phone you probably own; MediaTek designs it for the phone most of the world owns, shipping more smartphone chips than anyone, plus the quiet businesses in Wi-Fi, TVs and power management.

The second act is the reason for the size. Hyperscalers want custom AI accelerators and lack the engineers, so they hire an ASIC partner: Broadcom got there first, MediaTek is the credible second, with the TSMC relationship, the interconnect intellectual property, and a Hsinchu cost base. Google has already used them.

Fabless, net cash, generous dividend, founder still running the place.

What would worry us? Smartphones are mature and Chinese handset makers are brutal customers. The ASIC pipeline is real and lumpy — one delayed programme moves the shares.

## SK Hynix

4.1 percent. South Korea. The underdog that won the memory race by gluing chips together better than the giant next door.

In 2011 Chey Tae-won bought a debt-ridden memory maker nobody wanted, flew to Taiwan to ask Morris Chang whether he should do foundry, and was told, in effect, to relax and run the business he had. Fifteen years later that business makes the high-bandwidth memory next to every Nvidia GPU, and in June its market value passed Samsung Electronics.

The physical story is stacking: high-bandwidth memory is a tower of DRAM dies twelve or sixteen high, and the hard part is holding the tower together without warping. Hynix's answer, a process called MR-MUF, floods the stack with an epoxy from Namics on an exclusive deal — luck as much as foresight — and it delivered yield while Samsung's approach was still cracking. The union-won bonus formula, a share of operating profit, aligns the workforce to the cycle in both directions.

What would worry us? The most cyclical product in technology, and the summer showed it — down roughly a third in won from the peak, with Korean retail margin-called on the way. Sizing here is half the GEM weight for a reason: this book is closer to the blast radius.

## Realtek

2 percent. Taiwan. The crab logo on the chip that connects almost everything to almost everything else.

Open a laptop, a router, a TV or a car dashboard and the odds are good a Realtek chip handles the networking or the audio. It is Taiwan's connectivity house: Ethernet controllers, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth combos, codecs, switch chips — a catalogue business with thousands of parts designed into other people's products for years at a time.

The kicker is speed migration: every step from one-gigabit to 2.5 and five-gigabit Ethernet, in PCs, switches and increasingly cars, resets the price per port in Realtek's favour. Fabless, cash-rich, and priced like the unglamorous business it is.

What would worry us? Broadcom above, Chinese entrants below; consumer electronics volumes decide the year.

## Silergy

1.5 percent. Taiwan. China's answer to Texas Instruments, listed in Taipei, run by two Silicon Valley engineers.

Every device needs chips that convert, regulate and protect power. It is unglamorous analogue design where the value is the catalogue, and Silergy — founded by two ex-Silicon Valley engineers — built the largest Chinese one just as trade politics made a domestic supplier prudent for every Chinese customer. The automotive-grade catalogue is the current build-out.

Founders own a large stake and have now run through a full inventory cycle, which is when we added.

What would worry us? Chinese analogue is crowded with subsidised rivals, and the destocking cycles are violent.

## The electrified workshop pitches

The workshop, 16.5 percent of the portfolio.

## Lite-On Technology

2.5 percent. Taiwan. The power supplies feeding the AI datacentre, from a company everyone stopped watching a decade ago.

Lite-On spent decades as a diversified maker of power supplies, LEDs and PC components — a fine business slowly commoditising. Then the datacentre changed shape: AI racks draw multiples of the power of the old ones, and the power-supply and power-management layer became a bottleneck with real engineering content. Lite-On's server power business, qualified into the hyperscaler chains, is now the growth engine, with margins the old optical-drive company never saw.

Management pruned the commodity lines for years before the AI wave arrived, which is why the mix shifted so fast when it did.

What would worry us? Power supplies attract competition precisely when margins get interesting — Delta is the reigning heavyweight in the same room.

## Sunny Optical

1.4 percent. China. The lenses through which Chinese phones and, increasingly, cars see the world.

Sunny grinds and assembles the camera modules and lenses in a large share of the world's smartphones, and its second act is vehicle lenses — the eyes of driver-assistance systems, where it is the global leader and the content per car keeps climbing.

Founded in a Zhejiang village and still run with an engineer's frugality; the employee shareholding scheme is one of the older and better alignment structures in Chinese manufacturing.

What would worry us? Smartphone camera specs have plateaued, and auto lenses are a price-down business every year the way phone lenses were.

## XCMG

1.4 percent. China. Chinese cranes and diggers going global, just as the domestic construction site goes quiet.

XCMG is one of China's big three construction-machinery makers — cranes above all — and the thesis is the export cycle: Chinese machinery now matches Japanese quality at two-thirds of the price, and Belt-and-Road, Middle Eastern and Latin American buyers have noticed. Exports have grown into roughly half of revenue while domestic construction scrapes along a bottom.

A mixed-ownership reform improved incentives, and the electric line-up — battery loaders and cranes — is where Chinese makers lead outright.

What would worry us? A state-adjacent balance sheet with receivables to match, and the domestic cycle can stay on the floor longer than exports can carry it.

## Techtronic Industries

1.4 percent. Hong Kong listed, with US demand. Milwaukee power tools: a Hong Kong company that conquered the American building site battery by battery.

Techtronic owns Milwaukee and Ryobi, and its insight was the battery platform: sell the tradesman one battery system and every subsequent tool purchase is locked in. Milwaukee out-innovated Stanley Black and Decker for a decade straight — brushless motors, one new tool launch after another — and took the professional channel through Home Depot.

Founder-chaired, cash-generative, with manufacturing now spread across Vietnam and the US as well as China.

What would worry us? It is a claim on US housing and the Home Depot relationship; tariffs and a soft renovation cycle both bite.

## Tripod Technology

1.3 percent. Taiwan. The printed circuit boards under the servers, made by the most conservative family in the business.

Tripod makes printed circuit boards — the fibreglass real estate every chip is soldered to — with a mix tilting toward servers, networking and automotive. It is a family-run Taiwanese manufacturer of the classic type: net cash, high dividends, no empire-building, capacity added a step behind demand rather than ahead of it.

AI servers need more layers and better materials per board, which lifts the price of exactly what Tripod sells.

What would worry us? Circuit boards are a cyclical commodity at the low end, and the discipline that protects returns also caps growth.

## CATL

1.3 percent. China. One in three electric cars on earth runs on its batteries.

Contemporary Amperex is the world's largest electric-vehicle battery maker, supplying nearly every major carmaker, with a research budget larger than most rivals' revenue. Scale gives it the cost curve; chemistry breadth — iron-phosphate through to sodium-ion and condensed-matter cells — gives it the next product whichever way the market turns; and the licensing model now exports the technology into US and European plants it does not own.

Founder Robin Zeng still runs it, and the Hong Kong listing added a governance-friendly currency.

What would worry us? Battery prices deflate structurally, carmakers dual-source on principle, and Western industrial policy is aimed squarely at it.

## AirTAC

1.2 percent. Taiwan. The pneumatic fingers of the Chinese factory, taking share from Japan's SMC one cylinder at a time.

AirTAC makes pneumatic components — cylinders, valves, fittings — the compressed-air muscles of automated production lines. It is the Chinese-market challenger to Japan's SMC, winning on delivery speed and price with a direct sales force that calls on the factory floor, and it has been grinding share upward for fifteen years. The linear-guide business is the second product line, aimed at another Japanese-dominated niche.

Founder-run, vertically integrated, margins that embarrass most machinery companies.

What would worry us? It moves with Chinese factory capex, which has been flat on its back; share gains have to outrun the cycle.

## Delta Electronics

1.1 percent. Taiwan. The tax collector on every watt that enters a datacentre, a factory or an electric vehicle.

Delta is the world's largest maker of switching power supplies and a leader in industrial automation, EV charging and datacentre cooling. Its founding family has spent five decades on one idea — power efficiency — and the AI datacentre, which is above all a power problem, has made that idea the centre of the industry.

It is the premium name in the room and priced like it; we own the workshop tier around it partly because Delta proved the market exists.

What would worry us? Valuation carries the AI power story fully; any pause shows up here first.

## Shenzhen Inovance

1 percent. China. The motor drives and controls replacing Siemens on the Chinese factory floor.

Inovance grew out of Huawei's electrical division and makes the inverters, servo motors and programmable logic controllers that run automated production lines — the localisation winner in industrial controls, plus a large electric-vehicle powertrain business supplying Chinese carmakers. Engineer-founded, R&D-obsessed, and the default domestic choice as factories replace imported German and Japanese kit.

What would worry us? Factory capex is cyclical and the powertrain business earns thin margins in a brutal customer market.

## Voltronic Power

0.9 percent. Taiwan. The no-name behind the names: uninterruptible power supplies and solar inverters for whoever puts their brand on the box.

Voltronic manufactures purely as an original-design manufacturer — it makes the product, hundreds of brand customers sell it. Refusing to compete with its own customers is the moat: brands trust it precisely because it has no retail ambitions. Emerging-market power grids are unreliable and solar-plus-storage is booming, which is Voltronic's whole demand picture.

Founder-run, extraordinary returns on capital, dividends most of it back.

What would worry us? Customer concentration by channel, and Chinese inverter makers moving up-market.

## Quanta Computer

0.9 percent. Taiwan. The company that actually builds the AI servers everyone else takes credit for.

Quanta assembles notebooks and servers as an original-design manufacturer, and its server arm builds AI racks directly for the hyperscalers. The notebook business is mature ballast; the server business is riding the same capex wave as Nvidia, with the assembler's thin margin but enormous volume, and Quanta got into the AI rack business earlier than its Taiwanese rivals.

What would worry us? Assembly margins are thin by design and the customer list is four names long.

## Fuyao Glass

0.8 percent. China. One in three car windscreens on earth, made by a devout founder who wrote the book on cost.

Fuyao makes automotive glass and almost nothing else: a third of the world market, plants in China, the US — the one in the Netflix documentary — and Europe, and a founder, Cao Dewang, who has spent fifty years taking cost out of a product everyone else treated as a commodity. Electric vehicles and driver-assistance add content per car — bigger roofs, head-up displays, embedded antennas — so the product is quietly premiumising.

What would worry us? Global auto volumes are flat and energy is the biggest input cost.

## Hongfa Technology

0.7 percent. China. The relay in the meter, the car and the solar inverter — the world's largest maker of a component nobody thinks about.

A relay is an electrically operated switch, and Hongfa makes more of them than anyone: for smart meters, appliances, electric vehicles and photovoltaics. High-voltage direct-current relays for electric cars are the growth product, where Hongfa displaced the Japanese incumbents. It is a one-product specialist with the cost position that comes from making billions of units.

What would worry us? Component price deflation every year, forever; growth must come from volume and mix.

## Luxshare Precision

0.6 percent. China. From connector maker to Apple's other assembler, run by a woman who started on the Foxconn line.

Grace Wang worked on a Foxconn production line before founding Luxshare, which climbed from cables and connectors to assembling AirPods, Apple Watch and now iPhones — the only serious mainland challenger to Foxconn — with automotive harnesses and communications interconnects as the diversification.

What would worry us? Apple concentration, Apple's own de-China programme, and assembly margins.

## Advantech

0.3 percent. Taiwan. Industrial PCs for everywhere a normal computer would die.

Advantech makes the ruggedised computers and edge-AI boxes inside factory lines, hospital equipment and transport systems — the global leader in a fragmented niche, with software-like margins for hardware. A residual weight after reductions on valuation.

What would worry us? Valuation, and industrial capex cycles.

## The financials pitches

Banking a billion savers, 22.4 percent of the portfolio.

## ICICI Bank

4 percent. India. The bank that nearly blew up in 2018 and is now the best-run large lender in India.

ICICI spent the 2010s with a corporate book full of infrastructure loans gone bad and a chief executive who left under a cloud. Under Sandeep Bakhshi it has been rebuilt as a retail and small-business bank with a technology platform rivals copy, a granular deposit base, and credit costs that stayed low through the whole post-COVID cycle.

Indian banking is a growth business because credit-to-GDP is low and formalisation keeps pulling savings out of gold and into deposits. ICICI is taking share within that, with returns on equity at the top of the private sector — which is why it is the largest financial in this book and the largest India position.

What would worry us? Valuation reflects all of it, and rate cuts compress margins near-term.

## AIA Group

3.4 percent. Hong Kong, pan-Asia. A million agents selling the first life policy an Asian family ever buys.

AIA writes life and health insurance across eighteen Asian markets, and the machine is the tied agency force — career agents selling protection policies face to face, a distribution asset that took a century to build and cannot be bought. The product is the first serious savings and protection contract of a household entering the middle class, and the embedded value compounds as each cohort renews.

Mainland China is the prize: AIA is the only foreign life insurer with a wholly-owned mainland business, expanding province by province, and the wealthy mainland customer buying in Hong Kong is a second, high-margin channel that reopened with the border.

What would worry us? Chinese household caution suppresses new business; falling bond yields squeeze the investment margin the products assume.

## OCBC

2.6 percent. Singapore. Singapore's quiet compounder: a bank, a private bank and an insurer under one conservative roof.

OCBC is the second of Singapore's three banks, with Bank of Singapore as its private-banking arm and a majority of the insurer Great Eastern. The franchise gathers the money Asia's wealthy keep moving to Singapore, lends it carefully across ASEAN and Greater China, and pays most of the profit out. Capital is thick even by Singapore standards, which is saying something.

What would worry us? Rate cuts compress the margin that made the last three years so good, and the Great Eastern minority saga drags on.

## Kotak Mahindra Bank

2.4 percent. India. Uday Kotak's bank, the most conservative balance sheet in India, now run by someone other than Uday Kotak.

Kotak began as a bill-discounting firm and became a bank in 2003. The founder's obsession with capital and credit quality produced the sector's highest capital ratios and lowest credit losses, plus asset management, broking and insurance around the bank. Succession was forced by the regulator's tenure rules; the new chief executive is an outsider.

We own it for the balance sheet, and because a regulatory freeze on digital onboarding — since lifted — knocked the valuation to where the conservatism is finally being paid for.

What would worry us? Growth has lagged peers and the succession is untested in a bad cycle.

## HDFC Bank

2.2 percent. India. The franchise that defined Indian private banking, digesting the merger that made it too big to grow fast.

HDFC Bank compounded at twenty percent for twenty-five years by lending carefully to retail customers and never having a bad credit cycle. Absorbing its mortgage parent in 2023 brought a huge loan book funded with wholesale money, and the years since have been about rebuilding deposits to match. The franchise underneath — best cost of funds, biggest private branch network — is intact, at a valuation below its own history.

What would worry us? The deposit rebuild takes longer than promised and growth stays below system for years.

## Bank Central Asia

1.8 percent. Indonesia. Indonesia's best bank, owned by the Hartono family, with the cheapest deposits in Asia.

BCA is the transaction bank of Indonesia — the account every business and salaried employee keeps for payments — which gives it near-free current-account deposits no rival can match. It lends conservatively, holds excess capital, and earns the highest return on assets of any large Asian bank. The Hartonos have owned it since the crisis-era restructuring and run it like the family silver it is.

What would worry us? Always the valuation; occasionally the rupiah.

## KB Financial

1.7 percent. South Korea. Korea's biggest bank, learning to pay its owners — the cleanest expression of the Value-Up trade.

KB is Korea's largest financial group: the national bank franchise plus insurance, cards and brokerage. For decades Korean banks traded at fractions of book because profits were hoarded or directed. The government's Value-Up programme flipped the incentives — KB now targets returning half its profit through dividends and buybacks, retiring shares every year, and the discount has been closing from a very long way out.

What would worry us? Korean household leverage is world-class in the wrong way, and political appetite for bank profits changes with administrations.

## Bank of the Philippine Islands

1.1 percent. Philippines. The Ayala family's bank in the demographic sweet spot of Asia.

BPI is the oldest bank in Southeast Asia, controlled by the Ayala group, in a country whose median age is in the mid-twenties and where most people are only now getting their first bank account, card and loan. Credit-to-GDP is where India's was a generation ago; BPI's conservative book and low-cost deposits make it the safest way to hold that runway.

What would worry us? Philippine credit cycles are young and untested at today's scale; family-group related lending needs watching.

## Kasikornbank

0.8 percent. Thailand. Thailand's small-business bank, priced for the household-debt hangover it is slowly working through.

Kasikorn is the lender to Thai small business and the country's leading digital bank by users. Thailand's problem is household debt and a sluggish decade; the bank has spent years provisioning through it, and the shares price a permanent malaise. We own it for the franchise at a fraction of book while the clean-up compounds quietly.

What would worry us? The Thai macro can stay poor longer than the provisioning cycle stays kind.

## Bajaj Finance

0.8 percent. India. The consumer lender that taught India to pay in instalments.

Bajaj Finance financed the fridges and phones of the Indian middle class and built the country's largest consumer-lending franchise on the data that threw off — tens of millions of customers, cross-sold from durable loans into personal loans, cards and deposits. Growth has run at rates banks cannot match, with credit costs that so far have stayed honest.

What would worry us? Unsecured consumer credit growing this fast has a cycle in it somewhere; the regulator thinks so too.

## Niva Bupa Health Insurance

0.7 percent. India. Health cover for a country that pays for hospitals out of its own pocket.

Most Indian medical bills are still paid in cash at the hospital desk, and retail health insurance is growing faster than any other line as the middle class buys protection. Niva Bupa — the Bupa joint venture, recently listed — is the fastest-growing of the standalone health insurers, with a retail-heavy book and claims discipline learned from its British parent.

What would worry us? Medical inflation runs ahead of pricing in bad years, and the listed history is short.

## DBS

0.6 percent. Singapore. The best-run bank in Southeast Asia, held small because everyone knows it.

DBS turned itself from a stodgy state-linked lender into Asia's most digitally capable bank, with wealth management gathering the region's migrating money and a payout policy that treats shareholders as the point. A small position: quality this recognised rarely comes cheap.

What would worry us? Rate normalisation takes back what the hiking cycle gave.

## ICICI Lombard General Insurance

0.5 percent. India. India's largest private non-life insurer, in a country where most cars are still uninsured.

Motor, health and commercial cover in a market where non-life penetration is a fraction of developed levels. Best underwriting record among the private players, float invested through a rising-rate decade, distribution through the ICICI parent. A small position rebuilt from the GEM book's larger one.

What would worry us? Regulated motor pricing and persistent health-claims inflation.

## The Chinese consumer pitches

The Chinese consumer, second act, 18.7 percent of the portfolio.

## Tencent

6.3 percent. China. A billion people wake up and open WeChat before they open their eyes. Tencent charges rent on the day.

WeChat is messaging, payments, mini-apps and the identity layer of Chinese daily life, which gives Tencent something Google and Meta never got: a payment relationship with every user. Around it sit the world's largest games business, video, music, cloud, and an investment portfolio that has quietly been one of the great venture funds of the era.

Capital allocation is what distinguishes it. Since 2021 Tencent has bought back stock aggressively, paid dividends in kind, and treated its own equity as the best available investment. Pony Ma's team behaves like owners.

The AI angle is domestic: Tencent's models inside WeChat are the most plausible consumer AI distribution in China.

What would worry us? The regulator has frozen game approvals for a year before and can again. Prosus's steady selling is a permanent overhang.

## Midea Group

2.6 percent. China. The world's largest appliance maker, run like a private company that happens to be listed.

Midea sells more air conditioners, washing machines and kitchen appliances than anyone on earth, owns the German robot maker Kuka, and runs an industrial-automation and building-technology arm underneath the consumer brands. The founder handed the company to professional managers decades ago and kept them incentivised like owners — the cleanest governance story in Chinese manufacturing.

The thesis is operational excellence in a consolidating industry plus export share: Midea's own brands and contract-manufacturing lines supply the world, and the Hong Kong listing added a buyback currency it has used.

What would worry us? Chinese property completions drive appliance demand, and the industry's price wars never fully end.

## H World

2.6 percent. China. The hotel chain that is franchising China's road trip.

H World — Huazhu — runs Hanting, Ji and a stable of mid-scale brands: on the order of ten thousand hotels, most franchised. The model is Marriott's: own the brand and booking system, let someone else own the building. Chain penetration of Chinese hotels is still low, and every conversion adds a fee stream with almost no capital. Founder Ji Qi built it from one hotel; the loyalty base books direct, which is where the margin lives.

What would worry us? Revenue per available room is cyclical, supply in lower-tier cities is brisk, and the German legacy business remains a distraction.

## NetEase

2.1 percent. China. A games studio run by a founder who would rather make a great game than a big one.

William Ding still owns nearly half of the company he started as an email provider in 1997. China's second games publisher builds rather than buys: Fantasy Westward Journey has run for twenty years, and the Blizzard partnership survived a public divorce and reconciliation. Games throw off cash, the balance sheet holds a mountain of it, and the payout keeps rising.

What would worry us? Hit-driven, licence-dependent, and overseas expansion has been patchy.

## Shenzhen Mindray

1.4 percent. China. China's medical device champion, selling monitors and ultrasound to hospitals that used to buy GE and Philips.

Patient monitors, ventilators, ultrasound and lab analysers: Mindray reverse-engineered its way up the quality ladder and now takes share from the multinationals inside China, where procurement favours local, and across emerging markets, where price decides. Founders own a majority and reinvest in research at a rate few Chinese industrials match; overseas revenue approaches half the total.

What would worry us? Hospital anti-corruption campaigns freeze procurement without warning; volume-based procurement squeezes price.

## ANTA Sports

1.3 percent. China. The Chinese sportswear house that bought Fila's China rights and made more money from them than Fila ever did.

China's largest sportswear group, ahead of Nike locally: ANTA for the mass market, Fila for premium, Descente for outdoor, and control of Amer Sports — Arc'teryx, Salomon, Wilson. The Ding family's skill is brand management — each run separately with its own retail — in a category where Chinese consumers keep spending even as they cut elsewhere.

What would worry us? Fila is mature and needs a successor growth brand.

## Trip.com

1.1 percent. China. Booking.com for the largest travelling population on earth, with the hard years already behind it.

Trip.com survived three years of zero-COVID with revenue near zero, cut its cost base, and emerged with more share and better margins than it entered with. Outbound travel is still climbing back toward 2019 levels, which is the runway, and the international brand plus Skyscanner gives it Asia outside China.

What would worry us? Meituan and Douyin want the hotel business; outbound is hostage to visas and airline seats.

## PDD Holdings

1 percent. China. The company that taught Chinese shoppers to expect a lower price, then taught the rest of the world through Temu.

Pinduoduo became the third leg of Chinese e-commerce by being cheaper and simpler — no inventory, factory-direct merchants, an algorithm that pushes deals — and Temu exported the model to the West in three years flat. Margins are extraordinary because PDD owns no goods; disclosure is thin because the founding team prefers it that way. Sized for the governance, owned for the value mood of the Chinese consumer.

What would worry us? Opaque governance and Temu's tariff exposure; both are why the weight is a third of Tencent's.

## Full Truck Alliance

0.4 percent. China. The Uber for Chinese lorries, matching millions of truckers to the loads that used to sit waiting.

Shippers post loads, drivers find them, and once both sides are on the platform neither can leave. Take rates remain low relative to what the matching is worth, which is the growth story; the balance sheet is mostly cash. A small position against the GEM book's larger one.

What would worry us? Regulatory sensitivity around driver pay and data, plus the American depositary receipt structure.

## The houses pitches

Houses and handovers, 7.2 percent of the portfolio.

## LG Corp

1.7 percent. South Korea. The politest chaebol, trading at half the value of the things it owns.

LG Corp is the holding company of the LG group — stakes in LG Chem, LG Electronics, LG Household and Health Care and the rest — historically valued at a cavernous discount to those listed pieces. The family split of 2021 simplified the register, the Value-Up programme supplies the pressure, and the holding company has begun buybacks and better payouts. The thesis is arithmetic: the discount narrows, or the assets grow, and preferably both.

What would worry us? Holding-company discounts can hibernate for years, and the group's underlying businesses face brutal Chinese competition.

## Sony Group

1.5 percent. Japan. A century of beloved things — PlayStation, Spider-Man, half the world's camera sensors — finally priced as one company.

Sony's transformation from consumer-electronics also-ran to entertainment house is complete: games, with PlayStation's installed base and subscriptions; music, the world's largest publisher; pictures; and the image-sensor semiconductors inside most premium phone cameras. Management under Yoshida and Totoki has allocated capital like investors — buying music catalogues and anime distribution, buying back stock — and the conglomerate discount has been closing as the segments get reported honestly.

What would worry us? Games is a hit business between console cycles, and sensors follow the smartphone market.

## Astra International

1.3 percent. Indonesia. Indonesia's economy with a ticker: cars, motorbikes, palm oil, banks and heavy equipment under Jardine discipline.

Astra distributes Toyota and Honda in Indonesia, finances the purchases, sells the Komatsu diggers to the miners and runs plantations and toll roads besides. It is the bluest chip in Jakarta, majority-owned by Jardines, and it compounds with Indonesian consumption while paying out generously. The electric-vehicle transition is the live question — Chinese brands are arriving — and Astra's distribution and financing network is its hand to play.

What would worry us? Chinese electric-vehicle entrants attack the Toyota franchise that anchors the profit pool.

## Mahindra and Mahindra

1.2 percent. India. India's tractor king and its most improved carmaker, run by a capital-allocation convert.

Mahindra dominates Indian tractors — rural India's capital good — and its SUV line-up has gone from also-ran to segment leader on the strength of genuinely good product. The bigger change is discipline: the group spent a decade funding vanity subsidiaries, then publicly adopted return-on-capital religion, exited the losers, and the stock has behaved accordingly.

What would worry us? Monsoons decide tractor years, and the electric transition in SUVs is capital-hungry.

## Jardine Matheson

1.1 percent. Hong Kong, Singapore listed. The taipan's conglomerate, simplifying itself after 190 years of accumulating.

Jardines controls Hongkong Land, DFI Retail, Astra, Mandarin Oriental and more — a colonial trading house that became a holding company trading far below the sum of its parts. The current generation has collapsed the circular cross-holding, sold peripheral assets and started buybacks. Owning it alongside Astra doubles the best asset and gets the rest at a discount.

What would worry us? Hongkong Land's Central office exposure is a long convalescence, and family control caps how fast simplification goes.

## Nintendo

0.5 percent. Japan. Mario, Zelda and Pokémon — the most under-monetised character stable on earth, one console generation at a time.

Nintendo owns characters three generations of humans love, and has finally begun exploiting them beyond the console: films, theme parks, a museum, mobile. The Switch 2 extends the installed base the first Switch built. Kyoto conservatism cuts both ways — the balance sheet is a vault, and the pace is glacial. A small position for the intellectual-property value.

What would worry us? Between console cycles the earnings breathe out, and the film pipeline is young.

## The Anzac pitches

The Anzac wing, 3.3 percent of the portfolio.

## ResMed

1.3 percent. USA, Australia listed. The machines that stop the world's snorers suffocating, in a duopoly its main rival gifted it.

ResMed makes CPAP machines and masks for sleep apnoea — a chronic condition, massively under-diagnosed, treated nightly for life with consumables attached. Philips, the other half of the duopoly, recalled its devices in 2021 and spent years in regulatory purgatory, handing ResMed share it has kept. The weight-loss drugs that were supposed to kill the category turned out to bring more diagnosed patients into it.

What would worry us? The GLP-1 drugs remain the long-run question, and reimbursement decides margins.

## Fisher and Paykel Healthcare

0.8 percent. New Zealand. Humidified air, patented: the Auckland company inside hospital respiratory care worldwide.

Fisher and Paykel Healthcare makes humidification systems for hospital breathing support and masks for home care — nasal high-flow therapy is its franchise, growing as clinical practice adopts it ward by ward. Research-led, founder-cultured, and a business where the installed base pulls decades of consumables behind it.

What would worry us? Priced as the quality it is; hospital capital budgets set the tempo.

## BHP

0.7 percent. Australia. The lowest-cost iron ore on earth, plus the copper the electrified world keeps needing.

BHP digs iron ore in the Pilbara at costs no rival matches and copper in Chile and Australia where the grade story favours incumbents. It is the portfolio's one resources position: a claim on Asian construction and global electrification, run with post-2015 capital discipline and paid out as dividends.

What would worry us? Chinese steel demand has probably peaked; copper optionality has to carry the next decade.

## CSL

0.5 percent. Australia. Plasma collected in American strip malls, fractionated into medicines nobody else can make at scale.

CSL runs the world's largest plasma-collection network and fractionates it into immunoglobulins and specialty therapies — a business where scale in collection is the moat and demand grows with diagnosis. A small position while the post-COVID margin recovery grinds through.

What would worry us? Collection costs, and the flu-vaccine arm dilutes the crown jewel.
