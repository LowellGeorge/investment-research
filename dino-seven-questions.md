# Dino Polska — The Seven Questions

Dino Polska is the twenty-fifth-largest position in the GEM portfolio at one-point-seven percent, with a market cap of around nine to ten billion US dollars. Here is Dino Polska in seven questions.

---

## Cold open

Dino Polska just closed 2025 with revenue of thirty-three-point-six billion zloty, up fifteen percent year-on-year. That is the continuation of a multi-year compound annual growth rate above nineteen percent. The company opened three hundred and forty-five new stores in the year, taking the network to three thousand and thirty-three locations and twelve distribution centres. Net sales area expanded thirteen percent. Like-for-like sales grew four-point-four percent. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation was two-and-a-half billion zloty. Operating cash flow covered one hundred and twenty-two percent of capital expenditure. Net debt to earnings was negative — meaning the company sits on a small net cash position despite opening nearly a store a day.

This is the disciplined Polish grocery discounter that has been compounding at European-retail-best-in-class rates for a decade, with the founder still controlling the company and the store-opening engine still firing.

Seven questions.

---

## Question One: What is it?

Dino Polska is the largest and fastest-growing grocery retail chain in Poland by store count, specialising in proximity-discount supermarkets — mid-sized stores of around four hundred square metres, located predominantly in small towns and rural areas, focused on fresh food, everyday groceries, and a limited non-food assortment.

The model is deliberate. Fresh food represents forty-one percent of 2025 revenue. Other food categories — dry groceries, beverages, household staples — account for forty-seven percent. Non-food is just twelve percent. This high-fresh-food mix drives daily visits and repeat traffic, making each store a local hub rather than a weekly-shop destination. The store format is standardised: consistent layout, consistent assortment, consistent supplier relationships — which enables rapid rollout without execution risk.

The geographic footprint is still expanding. The company opened three hundred and forty-five new stores in 2025 alone, taking the network to three thousand and thirty-three stores across Poland. The supporting logistics infrastructure is twelve distribution centres. Store density is rising in established regions while entirely new regions continue to be entered. Management's explicit view is that Poland has significant room for further Dino store density — the total addressable store count is well above the current footprint.

The operating-economics structure is unusual. Like-for-like sales grew four-point-four percent in 2025, outpacing Polish food inflation of three-point-six percent. That is real volume growth. EBITDA margin came down slightly — thirty basis points — reflecting investment in new stores and assortment, but cash generation was strong enough to fund all capital expenditure plus absorption of working capital with headroom.

The thing to internalise: Dino is not a supermarket chain. It is a disciplined real-estate-and-logistics compounder in an under-served category — rural and small-town Polish groceries — where the combination of store format, fresh-food focus, and owner-operator rural proximity produces unit economics that the big international chains cannot replicate.

## Question Two: Can we trust them?

Yes — and the fifteen-year track record of consistent store rollout plus disciplined capital allocation is the evidence.

Tomasz Biernacki is the founder and controlling shareholder, with roughly fifty-one percent ownership. He built the business from a single-store operation in the 1990s to the Polish national leader. Management remains stable and long-tenured.

The past-stress case that matters is 2022 to 2023. Polish consumer-goods inflation spiked above fifteen percent during the Ukraine-war energy shock. Consumers traded down; labour costs rose; supply-chain costs compressed margins across European food retail. Dino absorbed the pressure, continued opening new stores at pace, protected the combined EBITDA margin through cost discipline, and used the market dislocation to take share from weaker regional competitors. By 2025, with Polish food inflation moderating to three-point-six percent, like-for-like sales grew four-point-four percent — meaningful real volume growth post-stress. That is the track record that matters.

The capital discipline signals from the 2025 print are striking for a retailer. Operating cash flow covered one hundred and twenty-two percent of capital expenditure. Net debt to EBITDA was negative at minus zero-point-zero-eight — meaning the company sits on a small net cash position despite aggressive organic expansion. Interest coverage ratio was over sixteen times. Working capital is structurally negative, meaning suppliers finance inventory — a disciplined retail feature. The photovoltaic installation programme now covers two thousand eight hundred and fifty-six stores, generating over one hundred gigawatt-hours in 2025 and reducing carbon-dioxide emissions by about fifty-four thousand tons. This is capital-discipline applied to ESG rather than ESG marketing.

The watch item is Biernacki's succession. He is still active and the firm is still his. There is no dividend policy — retained earnings are fully reinvested into store openings. Any change to this discipline would be a signal worth parsing carefully.

## Question Three: Why does value compound?

Three engines, plus a kicker.

The first engine is the store rollout machine. Three hundred and forty-five new stores in a single year, at net sales area growth of thirteen percent, on a standardised format with known unit economics. Each new store carries a predictable payback profile and begins contributing to EBITDA in its second year. This is the core compounder: organic unit-count expansion on funded-by-cash-flow basis.

The second engine is like-for-like sales growth from the existing base. Four-point-four percent LFL growth in 2025 tells you that mature stores are still gaining traffic and basket. This is the compounder underneath the compounder — earnings power that grows without additional capital.

The third engine is Polish grocery market-share consolidation. Smaller regional chains, traditional independent grocers, and even some international operators are losing share in small-town and rural Poland to Dino's model. Market-share gain on top of Polish food inflation plus real volume compounds into nominal revenue growth in the mid-teens.

The kicker is the margin optimisation runway. As store density rises and logistics routes become more efficient, operating leverage on the fixed logistics and headquarters cost base improves. The photovoltaic energy programme is reducing energy costs at scale. Private-label penetration continues to expand. Each of these levers adds basis points to EBITDA margin over time without requiring top-line acceleration.

## Question Four: What proves us right, and what to track?

Six specific metrics.

First, store count growth — plus three hundred and forty-five in 2025; continued two-hundred-plus new stores per year confirms the rollout engine. Second, like-for-like sales growth — four-point-four percent; maintenance above three percent confirms the mature-store compounder. Third, EBITDA margin — marginal compression in 2025; stabilisation or expansion from here confirms operating leverage. Fourth, operating cash flow versus capital expenditure — one hundred and twenty-two percent coverage in 2025; staying self-funding confirms capital discipline. Fifth, net debt to EBITDA — slightly negative today; remaining near zero or negative confirms balance-sheet discipline. Sixth, geographic expansion cadence — entry into new regions versus density in existing regions; continued both confirms the runway is real.

## Question Five: What's it worth?

The honest framing. Full-year 2025 revenue was thirty-three-point-six billion zloty. EBITDA was two-and-a-half billion zloty. Operating cash flow comfortably exceeded capital expenditure. Market cap is around nine to ten billion US dollars — roughly thirty-five to forty billion zloty.

On trading multiples, Dino trades at roughly twenty to twenty-five times trailing earnings and around fifteen times EBITDA. For a fifteen-percent-revenue-growth retailer with a fifteen-year track record, negative net debt, and a still-running organic rollout, that is in line with European grocery compounders like Ahold Delhaize or Jeronimo Martins and below global best-in-class discounters like Costco.

The simple framing: a disciplined Polish grocery compounder with multi-decade store-rollout runway, at a multiple that reflects European retail pricing rather than emerging-market compounder pricing. The right comparable set is Costco, B&M European Value Retail, and German hard discounters — all of which trade at similar or higher multiples for lower growth rates.

The key sensitivity is Polish consumer inflation and wage dynamics. Labour costs in Poland have been rising faster than food inflation; sustained wage pressure compresses margins. Second sensitivity is competitive entry. Biedronka — the Jerónimo Martins-owned market leader — and Lidl both operate in Poland and could respond more aggressively.

## Question Six: Where is the market wrong?

Two variant perception angles.

First: consensus prices Dino as a Polish regional retailer, when the operating economics are more comparable to the best global discount retailers — Costco, Aldi, Lidl — on return on invested capital and like-for-like growth. The geographic restriction to Poland is the reason the multiple is not higher, but the operating discipline is best-in-class global-retailer quality.

Second: the photovoltaic energy programme and other operational optimisations are priced as ESG features rather than as margin levers. In practice, renewable-energy self-generation at two thousand eight hundred and fifty-six stores is a meaningful cost advantage versus peers dependent on grid energy at European prices. This is operational alpha that compounds.

The honest counter: Polish equity markets carry persistent geographic discounts. Currency risk between Polish zloty and US or euro affects international investors. Competitive intensity from Biedronka and Lidl is real and could inflect. The founder concentration creates a single-person quality risk if Biernacki steps back. Position sizing should reflect these structural headwinds to multiple expansion.

## Question Seven: What could kill it, and when do we sell?

The real risks, ranked.

One: Polish consumer stagnation. A sharper Polish economic slowdown or wage-inflation squeeze compresses like-for-like growth and margins simultaneously. Two: competitive escalation. Biedronka or Lidl accelerating investment in small-town Poland would directly compete for Dino's core markets. Three: store-rollout saturation. At some point, the best Polish small-town locations become harder to find; the rollout curve flattens. Four: labour cost pressure. Polish minimum-wage increases or labour shortages in retail could persistently compress EBITDA margin. Five: regulatory change. Polish retail regulation — Sunday trading restrictions, tax on large-format retail, specific tariffs — has been unstable. Six: succession. Biernacki is the fifty-one-percent owner and the strategic anchor; any change in ownership or executive leadership is a potential volatility event.

Specific sell triggers. Store openings decelerating below two hundred per year without explicit strategic pivot explanation. Like-for-like growth declining below two percent for two consecutive years. EBITDA margin compressing more than one hundred basis points for two consecutive years. Net debt to EBITDA expanding above one times without a clear capital-project justification. Biedronka or Lidl announcing specific targeted moves against Dino's core markets. Tomasz Biernacki selling material stock or stepping back without a credible succession plan.
