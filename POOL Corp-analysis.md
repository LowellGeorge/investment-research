# POOL Corporation — Investment Analysis

**Date:** 16 March 2026
**Disclaimer:** This analysis is based on training data through mid-2025. Figures should be verified against the most recent filings.

## 1. Business History

Pool Corporation traces its origins to a 1993 leveraged buyout of South Central Pool Supply, a regional distributor based in Covington, Louisiana. The newly formed entity, initially named SCP Pool Corporation, was backed by private equity and led by a management team that recognized a fundamental inefficiency in the swimming pool supply chain: the industry was deeply fragmented, served by dozens of small regional distributors with limited purchasing power, inconsistent service levels, and no scale advantages. The thesis was straightforward — consolidate these distributors into a single national platform, leverage purchasing scale to extract better vendor terms, and build route density that would make the economics of delivery virtually unassailable.

SCP Pool went public in 1995, using the IPO proceeds and access to public capital markets to fund an aggressive roll-up strategy. Through the late 1990s and into the 2000s, the company acquired regional distributors at a rapid clip, absorbing competitors across the Sun Belt and beyond while retaining their local customer relationships and folding their operations into an increasingly efficient centralized infrastructure. Each acquisition added sales centers, deepened route density, and strengthened vendor negotiating leverage in a self-reinforcing cycle. The company formally changed its name to Pool Corporation in 2001, reflecting both its market dominance and its ambitions beyond the original SCP footprint.

The architect of much of this transformation was Manuel Perez de la Mesa, who became CEO in 1999 and led the company for two decades until his retirement in 2019. Perez de la Mesa instilled a culture of distribution excellence — relentless focus on inventory turns, fill rates, delivery reliability, and intimate customer service for the roughly 120,000 contractor, builder, and retail customers the company serves. Under his leadership, Pool Corp expanded internationally into Canada, Europe, and Australia, and diversified adjacently through the 2007 acquisition of Horizon Group, which brought irrigation and landscape supply distribution onto the platform, and the 2008 purchase of National Pool Tile, adding hardscape and tile to the product portfolio. Dozens of smaller tuck-in acquisitions continued throughout.

Peter Arvan, who succeeded Perez de la Mesa as CEO, inherited a business with a structural moat rooted in the installed base of approximately 5.7 million in-ground swimming pools in the United States. Each pool requires $1,000 to $2,000 or more annually in chemicals, replacement parts, and equipment — expenditures that are largely non-discretionary, recession-resistant, and recurring. This maintenance annuity, combined with Pool Corp's unmatched network of over 420 sales centers and its roughly 40 percent share of the wholesale distribution market, creates a flywheel that competitors find extraordinarily difficult to replicate.

The COVID-19 pandemic proved to be an unexpected accelerant. As homeowners invested heavily in backyard living during 2020 and 2021, new pool construction surged roughly 25 percent above historical norms, the existing installed base expanded meaningfully, and Pool Corp's revenues jumped from $3.2 billion in 2019 to $5.4 billion by 2022. While new construction has since normalized, the pools built during that boom permanently enlarged the recurring maintenance revenue base, embedding a lasting step-change in the company's earnings power.

## 2. Management & Incentives

POOL Corporation benefits from a seasoned leadership team with deep distribution expertise, though the organization has navigated a meaningful generational transition at the top in recent years.

CEO Peter Arvan assumed the role in January 2019 after a career spanning HD Supply and Hubbell Incorporated, where he held senior operational and general management positions. Arvan brought an outsider's perspective to a company long defined by its prior leader, Manuel Perez de la Mesa, who served as CEO for nearly two decades (2001–2018) and is widely credited with transforming POOL from a regional distributor into the dominant national platform. Perez de la Mesa transitioned to Executive Chairman upon Arvan's appointment and remains on the board, providing continuity and institutional memory. This arrangement has been largely seamless — Arvan has maintained the disciplined operating philosophy while layering on technology investments (POOL360 platform, digital selling tools) and accelerating the private-label strategy.

CFO Melanie Hart took over the finance function in 2022, succeeding Mark Joslin, who had held the role for over a decade. Hart had been with the company in senior finance roles prior to her promotion, ensuring a smooth handoff.

Compensation structure aligns well with shareholder interests. Base salaries are moderate relative to peers. Annual cash incentives are tied primarily to operating income and EPS growth targets, while long-term equity awards — a mix of performance-based restricted stock units and stock options — are linked to multi-year EPS growth and return on invested capital (ROIC) thresholds. This ROIC linkage is particularly important given the capital-light distribution model and the company's history of tuck-in acquisitions; it discourages value-destructive deal-making.

Insider ownership is meaningful but not controlling. Perez de la Mesa has historically been the largest individual holder, with a position worth several hundred million dollars. Arvan and other named executives hold more modest stakes supplemented by ongoing equity grants.

Capital allocation has been a hallmark strength. Management has executed aggressive share repurchases — reducing the diluted share count by roughly 25–30% over the past decade — while simultaneously growing the dividend at a double-digit compound rate. The company has maintained a track record of small, disciplined tuck-in acquisitions at sensible valuations, avoiding the large transformational deals that often destroy value. Leverage has generally been kept within a prudent range of 1.5–2.5x net debt/EBITDA.

Board composition skews experienced and independent, with directors drawn from retail, distribution, and building products backgrounds.

Guidance credibility is high. POOL has a strong track record of meeting or exceeding annual guidance, with management tending toward conservatism in initial outlooks. The COVID-era demand surge and subsequent normalization tested this credibility — importantly, management communicated the normalization clearly rather than clinging to unsustainable growth expectations, which reinforced trust with the investment community.

## 3. Competitive Landscape

POOL Corporation operates in a structurally attractive position within the North American swimming pool supply distribution market. With an estimated 40% share of the roughly $18 billion wholesale distribution market, POOL is roughly eight times the size of its next largest competitor, which holds approximately 5% share. The remainder of the market is fragmented across hundreds of regional and local distributors, many of them single-location family-owned operations.

The competitive set includes several distinct categories. SRS Distribution, now owned by Home Depot following its 2024 acquisition, represents the most credible scaled threat, though its pool supply business remains a fraction of POOL's footprint and Home Depot's strategic focus with SRS has centered on roofing and building products. Hayward Holdings, a leading equipment manufacturer, has periodically explored direct-to-dealer models, but doing so risks alienating the distribution channel that drives the vast majority of its volume. Leslie's, the largest specialty pool retailer, serves primarily the do-it-yourself consumer and competes at the retail level rather than for the professional contractor wallet that constitutes POOL's core business.

POOL's competitive moat rests on several reinforcing advantages. Its network of over 420 sales centers enables same-day or next-day delivery across most major markets, a capability that matters enormously to pool builders and service technicians who cannot afford jobsite delays. Route density drives logistics cost advantages that widen with each incremental location. The company stocks approximately 200,000 SKUs — by far the broadest assortment in the channel. Scale-driven purchasing power yields vendor rebates and early-buy program economics that smaller distributors simply cannot access, creating a structural cost advantage estimated at 5–10% on key product categories.

Switching costs for professional customers are meaningful if underappreciated. Pool builders and service companies rely on POOL for trade credit terms, technical support, warranty processing, and the POOL360 digital ordering platform, which integrates into daily workflow. Migrating away from this ecosystem involves operational disruption disproportionate to any marginal savings a competitor might offer.

The Amazon and Home Depot disintermediation risk, frequently raised by skeptics, is mitigated by the service-intensive nature of the business. Pool chemicals are hazardous materials with shipping restrictions. Equipment like pumps, heaters, and filters requires technical sizing expertise. Professional contractors value speed, credit, and knowledgeable counter staff — attributes poorly suited to e-commerce fulfillment models.

Barriers to entry are substantial. Replicating POOL's distribution density would require enormous upfront capital deployment in warehouse infrastructure, years of working capital investment in inventory, and the patient cultivation of vendor relationships that unlock preferential pricing tiers. No new entrant has achieved meaningful national scale in decades.

Underpinning the entire competitive position is the installed base of approximately 5.7 million in-ground pools in the United States. Each pool requires $1,200–$1,500 annually in maintenance chemicals, replacement parts, and periodic equipment upgrades regardless of macroeconomic conditions. This non-discretionary aftermarket stream — representing roughly 60% of POOL's revenue — provides a recurring, recession-resistant demand floor.

## 4. Financial Characteristics

POOL Corporation operates as the world's largest wholesale distributor of swimming pool supplies, equipment, and related outdoor living products. The financial profile is characteristic of a best-in-class specialty distributor, though several features distinguish POOL meaningfully from distribution peers.

The revenue model carries the typical margin structure of wholesale distribution, with gross margins in the 29–30% range, somewhat above the distribution sector average owing to POOL's private-label penetration, vendor rebate programs, and pricing discipline. Approximately 60% of sales are tied to non-discretionary maintenance, repair, and replacement spending — chemicals, parts, and equipment required to keep the installed base operational. Another 20% relates to renovation and remodel activity, with only about 20% exposed to new pool construction. This composition provides a recurring, defensive revenue base unusual for a distributor.

Operating margins have historically ranged from 13% to 15% in normalized conditions, reflecting strong operating leverage as volume flows through a largely fixed-cost branch network. Margins peaked near 16% during 2021–2022 when the COVID-driven pool construction boom pushed revenue to approximately $6 billion, well above trend. Subsequent normalization brought revenues back toward the $5.3–5.5 billion range.

What is genuinely distinctive is POOL's capital efficiency. The business requires minimal capital expenditure — roughly 1% of revenue — reflecting an asset-light model where inventory and receivables constitute the primary working capital investment. Working capital is seasonal, with inventory building through the first quarter ahead of pool season and cash harvesting heavily in the third and fourth quarters. Return on invested capital consistently exceeds 30%, a figure that would be exceptional in any industry but is remarkable for distribution. Return on equity runs even higher, amplified by a balance sheet that carries meaningful debt deployed primarily for share repurchases. Leverage has remained manageable, typically in the range of 1.5–2.5 times net debt to EBITDA.

Cash conversion is strong and consistent, with free cash flow generally tracking or exceeding net income over full cycles. This combination of high returns on capital, an asset-light model, and a defensive revenue mix tied to a non-discretionary installed base creates a financial profile that more closely resembles a franchise business than a typical distributor.

## 5. Red Flags

POOL Corporation rode an extraordinary tailwind from 2020 through 2022 as homebound consumers poured discretionary income into backyard upgrades. Revenue surged from roughly $3.2 billion in 2019 to approximately $6.2 billion in 2022 — nearly doubling in three years. The critical question is how much of that growth was structural versus a one-time pull-forward of demand. The installed base of pools did grow meaningfully (estimated 5–6% expansion in the U.S. pool count), which supports a permanently higher maintenance aftermarket. But new pool construction volumes, which peaked near 120,000 units in 2021–2022, have fallen back sharply as permits declined 30–40% from the peak. Revenue in 2023 and 2024 declined or stagnated, and there is no guarantee the floor has been reached. Management's narrative of a "higher base" of recurring revenue deserves scrutiny: chemical and maintenance spend is indeed non-discretionary, but it represents only a portion of the revenue base, and even aftermarket volumes can compress when consumers defer equipment replacement.

New pool construction is a big-ticket, highly discretionary purchase tightly correlated with housing turnover, home equity levels, consumer confidence, and financing costs. With mortgage rates elevated and housing affordability at multi-decade lows, the pipeline for new builds remains under pressure. POOL's builder customers — fragmented, often thinly capitalized small businesses — are acutely sensitive to these cycles.

Weather risk is non-trivial and unhedgeable. The second quarter typically generates 30–35% of annual revenue. A cold, wet spring across the Sun Belt can materially compress full-year earnings with no opportunity for recovery.

POOL has historically commanded 25–35x forward earnings, justified by its dominant distribution position and perceived defensive characteristics. But that multiple was assigned during a decade of uninterrupted growth. If earnings are in secular decline or even stagnant for several years, multiple compression alone could drive 30–40% downside. The company has used debt-funded share repurchases aggressively — a strategy that creates value when buying below intrinsic value but destroys it when executed near cyclical peak earnings at premium multiples. Buyback spending in 2021–2022 at elevated prices represents a permanent misallocation that will not be recovered.

Leslie's, despite its own operational struggles, represents a direct-to-consumer channel that bypasses POOL's distribution model. More concerning long-term is the potential for major manufacturers — Pentair, Hayward — to explore direct or hybrid distribution models, particularly as digital commerce reduces friction.

The bear case is internally consistent. A sustained housing downturn, persistently elevated interest rates, unfavorable spring weather, and continued post-COVID normalization are not independent risks — they are correlated. In a downside scenario, earnings could retreat toward the $6–8 per share range (versus the mid-cycle $13–15 that bulls assume), and a compressed multiple of 18–22x on trough earnings would imply a stock price 40–50% below recent levels.

Investors should press management on: what percentage of 2020–2022 revenue growth was pull-forward versus permanent installed-base expansion; at what level of new pool construction permits does the operating model show negative leverage; the internal hurdle rate governing share repurchase authorization; and whether any top-ten manufacturer partners have explored direct distribution models.

## 6. Investment Checklist

**1. Understandable Business: YES.** POOL is the world's largest wholesale distributor of swimming pool supplies, equipment, and outdoor living products. Buy from manufacturers in bulk, distribute to a fragmented base of pool builders, retailers, and service professionals through 420+ sales centers. Straightforward.

**2. Durable Competitive Advantages (Widening Not Narrowing): YES.** ~40% share of a fragmented market with the next competitor at ~5%. Route density, broadest SKU selection, vendor rebates, POOL360 digital platform, and switching costs for contractors all reinforce a moat that widens with each incremental location.

**3. Long Reinvestment Runway at High Returns: YES.** The installed base of 5.7M in-ground pools grows modestly each year, and each pool requires recurring maintenance. Greenfield and tuck-in acquisition runway remains long. Adjacent categories (outdoor living, irrigation, hardscaping) expand the TAM. ROIC consistently 30%+.

**4. High Returns on Incremental Capital: YES.** New sales centers require modest capital and reach profitability within 1–2 years. Tuck-in acquisitions integrate at reasonable multiples. ROIC has averaged well above 30% over the past decade — exceptional for distribution.

**5. Strong Free Cash Flow Generation: YES.** Asset-light model with capex at ~1% of revenue. Free cash flow conversion of net income generally exceeds 90–100% over full cycles. Highly predictable given the recurring maintenance revenue base.

**6. Conservative Balance Sheet: PARTIALLY.** Leverage of 1.5–2.5x net debt/EBITDA is manageable but not fortress-like. Debt has been used for buybacks, which is reasonable for this cash flow profile but introduces risk if earnings decline and leverage ratios expand.

**7. Aligned Management with Skin in the Game: YES.** Perez de la Mesa holds a substantial personal stake. Compensation is tied to operating income and ROIC metrics. Arvan holds meaningful equity. Alignment is credible.

**8. Rational Capital Allocation History: YES.** Steady reinvestment in organic growth, disciplined tuck-in acquisitions, growing dividend, significant buybacks. Shares outstanding down 25–30% over a decade. The one blemish: buybacks at peak 2021–2022 valuations were poorly timed.

**9. Favourable Industry Structure: YES.** Dominant distributor in a market with no credible scaled competitor. Fragmented customers with limited bargaining power. Suppliers need POOL's distribution reach. High barriers to entry from density economics, working capital, and vendor relationships.

**10. Reasonable Valuation Relative to Quality and Growth: PARTIALLY.** Historically trades at 25–35x earnings. At mid-to-high 20s P/E on normalised earnings, not cheap in absolute terms but may be reasonable for this quality and mid-to-high single-digit long-term growth potential. Entry price discipline matters.

**11. Resilience to Recession or Disruption: PARTIALLY.** Maintenance/repair (~60% of revenue) is highly resilient. But new construction and remodeling (~40%) are cyclical and discretionary. During 2008–2009, earnings declined meaningfully. Relatively insulated from tech disruption.

**12. Transparent Management Communication: YES.** Clear quarterly commentary on end-market trends with maintenance vs. discretionary breakdowns. Forthright about post-COVID normalization. Conservative guidance frameworks.

### Overall Assessment

POOL Corporation is a high-quality compounder operating as the dominant distributor in a structurally attractive niche. The business benefits from a large and growing installed base generating recurring maintenance demand, a widening scale advantage, exceptionally high returns on capital, and a proven management team. The primary risks are cyclical exposure to new construction, post-COVID normalization, and a premium valuation that demands continued execution. For long-term investors, POOL represents a rare combination of business quality, competitive positioning, and reinvestment opportunity, though entry price discipline is warranted.

## 7. Synthesis

### The 3–4 Things That Matter Most

**First, the installed base is the business.** Everything about POOL's investment case ultimately traces back to one number: 5.7 million in-ground pools in the United States, each requiring $1,200–$1,500 in annual non-discretionary maintenance. This creates a recurring revenue base that is genuinely defensive — pool owners must buy chemicals and replace failed equipment regardless of the economy. The COVID boom added an estimated 5–6% to the installed base, permanently enlarging this annuity. The financial analysis confirms it: 60% of revenue is maintenance/repair, free cash flow conversion exceeds 90%, and ROIC consistently tops 30%. This is a franchise business disguised as a distributor.

**Second, the competitive moat is as wide as it gets in distribution.** 40% market share with the next competitor at 5%. Over 420 sales centers creating route density that is essentially unreplicable. 200,000 SKUs. Vendor rebates and early-buy economics that smaller distributors cannot access. Professional switching costs embedded in credit terms, technical support, and POOL360 digital workflow. The Competitive Landscape analysis is correct that neither Amazon nor Home Depot can credibly disintermediate this — pool chemicals are hazmat, equipment requires technical expertise, and contractors need same-day delivery and trade credit. No new entrant has achieved meaningful national scale in decades. This moat is structural and widening.

**Third, the post-COVID normalization is the key near-term debate — and the Red Flags section makes a strong case for caution.** Revenue nearly doubled from 2019 to 2022, then pulled back. The bull case says the new baseline is permanently higher because the installed base grew. The bear case says discretionary spending (40% of revenue) has further to fall as housing weakens, and even maintenance volumes can compress when consumers defer equipment replacement. The truth is probably somewhere between, but the Red Flags analysis correctly identifies that buybacks executed at 2021–2022 peak valuations represent a permanent value destruction that management's otherwise excellent capital allocation record cannot erase.

**Fourth, valuation is the gatekeeper.** POOL has compounded at 20%+ annually for two decades, but the stock has historically traded at 25–35x earnings — a multiple that assumes perpetual compounding. If normalized earnings are $13–15/share and growth moderates to mid-single-digits, a 25x multiple gives you a fair stock price with limited upside from current levels. If earnings undershoot or the multiple compresses to 20x (entirely plausible in a tightening cycle), downside is 30–40%. This is a business you want to own forever but must buy at the right price.

### Key Tensions and Contradictions

The most revealing tension is between the Investment Checklist (9 YES, 3 PARTIALLY — one of the strongest scorecards across all companies analysed) and the Red Flags section's warning that the bear case is "internally consistent." Both are right. POOL is an objectively exceptional business that is simultaneously exposed to a correlated set of cyclical risks (housing, rates, weather, post-COVID normalization) that could compress earnings 40–50% from peak. The checklist evaluates the business; the Red Flags evaluate the price you're paying for it.

A second tension: management gets high marks for capital allocation discipline, yet the 2021–2022 buybacks at peak multiples on peak earnings are precisely the kind of error that a ROIC-linked compensation structure should prevent. Either the internal hurdle rate is too loose, or the repurchase framework doesn't adequately account for cyclical positioning.

### Open Questions for Further Research

1. **Installed base quantification:** What is the best estimate of net new pools added during 2020–2022, and what is the implied incremental maintenance revenue? Is management's "higher base" narrative supported by third-party data?
2. **Construction permits trajectory:** What are the latest monthly permit trends for new in-ground pool construction? Where are we relative to pre-COVID normalised levels?
3. **Buyback framework:** What is the internal valuation hurdle for share repurchases? Is there a mechanism to pause buybacks when the stock trades above intrinsic value?
4. **Manufacturer direct risk:** Have Pentair or Hayward made any moves toward direct distribution? What percentage of POOL's revenue comes from manufacturers with their own D2C ambitions?
5. **Margin normalisation:** What is the realistic normalised operating margin — the pre-COVID 12–13% or the current 14–15%? How much of the margin expansion is structural (private label, digital efficiency) vs. cyclical?

### Quality Assessment

POOL Corporation is a **textbook quality compounder** — dominant market share, recurring revenue from a non-discretionary installed base, asset-light economics, 30%+ ROIC, and a management team with a strong capital allocation track record. Among the five companies analysed in this research series, POOL has the most classically "compoundable" business model: simple, defensible, and generating high incremental returns on modest capital. The primary risk is not the business — it is the price. At a cyclically depressed valuation during the next housing downturn, POOL would be one of the most attractive long-term holdings in the market. At a premium multiple on peak-adjacent earnings, it is a good business at a fair-to-expensive price. Patience and entry discipline are everything.
