# FSSA Standing FAQ, October 2026

Part One of the internal FSSA FAQ. The questions every due-diligence meeting and RFP asks, in the order of the Stewart Investors template, with answers in the voice we use in meetings.

## Organisational overview

## What is FSSA Investment Managers?

We are a specialist manager of Asian and emerging-market equities, long-only, with investment staff in Hong Kong, Singapore, Edinburgh and London. We launched our first portfolio in 1988, around the time MSCI launched its Emerging Markets index. Today we manage about 29 billion US dollars across pan-Asian, single-country India, single-country China and global emerging markets strategies.

The philosophy has run through those four decades with little change. It was shaped by the firm's early architect, Angus Tulloch. It is a quality approach whose first emphasis is the quality of people: finding the small number of trustworthy management teams to whom we can give our clients' capital, and our own, for many years. That emphasis makes sense in markets that are often unkind to minority shareholders.

## What is your ownership structure?

FSSA is an autonomous investment team within First Sentier Group. The group is owned by Mitsubishi UFJ Trust and Banking Corporation, a wholly owned subsidiary of Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group, which completed its purchase from the Commonwealth Bank of Australia on the second of August 2019. From 2000 to 2019 the business was owned by the Commonwealth Bank.

Our autonomy rests on a governance agreement that has been in place for more than twenty years. It gives the team control of the decisions that matter in an investment business: whom we hire, how we pay them, where and how we invest, and how much capital we take.

## Why are you changing your name to First State Investments?

From the twenty-fourth of November 2026 we trade as First State Investments, the name the team carried from 1988 until 2019. The change comes with a new visual identity across Asia, Europe and the US. The philosophy, the team and its leadership, fund names, legal entities and regulatory registrations all stay as they are.

## What happened with Stewart Investors, and what did you take on?

Three senior Stewart Investors portfolio managers left in August 2025. After a review, First Sentier Group closed the business, and on the fourteenth of November 2025 FSSA took over management of the Asia Pacific, global emerging markets and Indian Subcontinent funds. The Worldwide funds stayed outside the transfer.

In meetings we put it this way. Of their roughly 12 billion, we declined the 2 billion of global equities and took the 10 billion that mapped onto our Asia, emerging markets and India capabilities. We spent two quarters bringing those portfolios into line with ours and speaking to every client. We kept about seven and a half billion. Most of what left was already leaving. The decision was the group's, after the departures; we did not drive it. We took the assets and none of the people, because merged teams rarely work.

## Is your GEM strategy the same as the Stewart Investors GEM fund?

No. They are different strategies with separate track records. Our Global Emerging Markets Focus strategy launched in 2017 out of the Asia team and has never shared a manager with theirs. The ex-Stewart emerging markets portfolios are now run in line with ours, and when we laid the two side by side there were only one or two names we would not have owned. The disagreements were about price, not quality.

## What strategies do you manage?

Asia Pacific, single-country China and Greater China, India, ASEAN and global emerging markets, across all-cap and focused versions.

The launch dates for the main lines run as follows. The first Asia Pacific portfolio launched in 1988. China Growth followed in 1992, Indian Subcontinent in 1994, Greater China Growth in 2002, and China A Shares in 2009. Global Emerging Markets Focus launched in 2017. Asian Growth and ASEAN All Cap are the other main lines. The former Stewart Investors Asia Pacific, emerging markets and India funds moved to FSSA in November 2025.

For our emerging markets track record, always quote GEM Focus from 2017.

## Why FSSA?

Five reasons.

Heritage. Investing in Asia since 1988, with one philosophy throughout.

Proximity. Most of the team sits in Hong Kong and Singapore, close to the companies, and we hold more than a thousand company meetings a year.

Long horizon. A five-year view at purchase, and an ideal holding period of ten to twenty years.

Alignment. Deferred profit share invested in our funds, and direct equity in the business through FSSA Holdings since 2025.

Capital preservation. Risk is defined as the permanent loss of capital, and we have historically been strongest in flat and falling markets.

## What is your capacity?

For GEM Focus, about 7 to 8 billion with today's holdings. Capacity is set by the liquidity of the smaller names we want to own at full weight, and the governance agreement lets the team close a strategy when it judges the limit reached.

## Investment approach

## What are your investment aims?

To compound clients' capital over the long term by owning high-quality companies bought at sensible prices, and to protect that capital when markets fall. For GEM Focus the target is roughly 3 percent a year ahead of the index after fees over a cycle, with positive absolute returns across the cycle. Neither can be reached by hugging the index.

## What is your investment philosophy?

We buy quality companies at a sensible price and hold them for the long term. Quality has three parts: management, franchise and financials. Management is by far the most important. We invest in people and real businesses, so we spend almost no time on the index, and we define risk as the risk of losing capital rather than of underperforming a benchmark. Preserving capital is the most important part of growing it.

In emerging markets the order Rasmus uses is: people as a filter, franchise as the engine, price as the discipline. Franchise gets us interested. Governance decides whether we are allowed to act on it.

## What is your investment process?

Four stages.

Idea generation is bottom-up, mostly from company meetings: more than a thousand a year across the team. Any analyst may pick up any company in any country.

Research. Every company has an owner who writes a full report, including an ESG section built from our own sector questionnaires, so a bank in India can be compared with one in Brazil. The whole team reviews every holding about twice a year.

Valuation. A conservative five-year free-cash-flow forecast, a required yield that rises with cyclicality and capital intensity, and from those an implied return, the IRR. That IRR is the one number compared across the book.

Decision. The team debates; the named portfolio manager decides. There is no investment committee and no house view.

## Is there a return hurdle?

Yes, about 13 percent in emerging markets. Nine percent is roughly what emerging markets have returned over 25 years, 3 percent is the alpha we owe clients, and 1 percent covers cash drag. Thirteen with certainty beats twenty without, so a higher-visibility business can hold a bigger weight on a lower IRR. TSMC is the standing example.

## How do you manage risk?

Mainly through what we own: high-quality companies with net-cash or conservative balance sheets, run by people we trust, bought at prices that leave a margin. On top of that sit portfolio rules: limits on single stocks, countries and sectors; liquidity monitoring; and a country framework that places each market in a risk bucket. Brazil sits structurally high, the North Asian exporters structurally low, and India is the one that moves, mostly with the oil price. The framework is a filter. It has stopped us doing things and has never made us buy anything.

Independent oversight comes from First Sentier's risk teams. We do not use leverage.

## How do you approach sustainable investing and ESG?

Sustainability work sits inside the research, done by the analysts who own the companies. There is no separate ESG team taking the decisions. In research, every report has an ESG section from our own questionnaires. In ownership, the gaps that work shows up become the engagement list for that company.

We exclude defence alongside tobacco and gambling because it is very hard to say where defence ends and offence begins. The ex-Stewart funds remain Article 9 under the EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation. It took about six weeks to evidence that every holding mapped to a sustainability goal, and the only exclusion gap was two brewers at under 3 percent of the fund.

## What is your approach to engagement and voting?

Engagement means asking the right question of the right person. Data goes to the ESG specialists; judgement goes to the chief executive. Each research note records the baseline and the gaps, and those gaps are what we raise next time we meet. Voting is done by the investment team. In 2025 we voted on 4,097 resolutions and opposed management on 3.4 percent of them. Board independence and tenure are the most common subject. In 2025 we wrote to Radico Khaitan, KEI, Luxshare and Trip.com about it.

## People

## Who is in the investment team and what are their roles?

About fifteen investment professionals, all generalist company analysts first. The Managing Partners are Martin Lau and Michael Stapleton. The lead portfolio managers are Martin Lau on Asia Focus, Asian Equity Plus and China Growth; Rasmus Nemmoe on GEM Focus; Rizi Mohanty on Asian Growth and ASEAN; Sree Agarwal on Indian Subcontinent and Asian Smaller Companies; Helen Chen on Greater China Growth and China Focus; and Winston Ke on China A and All China.

The strength of feeling about the analyst role shows in an absence: those who run portfolios do not use the PM title on the team slide. Martin sets the research pace with his own heavy schedule of company meetings.

## How is coverage organised?

Through a matrix of country responsibilities. Each person has a centre of gravity chosen for their skills, such as language and cultural knowledge, and nobody is confined to it. Every one of roughly 130 companies has an owner, and the whole team sees everything twice a week. Coverage moves a few names at a time, on request, so nobody loses a business model. Juniors are clustered; seniors are spread.

Language matters most in China and we insist on it. We also learned that language without international experience gives a single lens, so our Chinese colleagues all have careers outside the mainland.

## How much attention does GEM get outside Asia?

The emerging markets portfolio is about 85 percent Asia, so the whole team does emerging markets research most of the time. For the rest of the world, Angus spends most of his week on it, Chow in Singapore a good part, and Matt Gill, who joined from Polar Capital, most of his. The point of global emerging markets for the team is reference points. If you know Itaú you look at an Indian bank differently, and understanding MercadoLibre helped us on Sea.

## Has the team been stable? Who has joined?

Two years ago a senior India colleague left at our request and two others went with him. Sree, co-manager of India for a decade, took over the India fund. We then hired slowly: Manya from Arisaig on India and Asian consumer; a senior China-focused colleague from Genesis and Matthews Asia, now in Hong Kong; Matt Gill from Polar Capital, earlier at Stewart Investors, on non-Asia emerging markets; and an India new-economy specialist joining Singapore. Every candidate writes and presents a company report to the whole team, and offers need unanimous senior agreement. In India we took four people through and hired two. We consider the team complete, and deliberately carry one or two more than the minimum so there is always a bench.

## How do you manage key-person risk?

Each strategy has a lead and a co-manager drawn from the same team and the same research, so a handover is a change of signature on decisions the team already shares. Succession is planned ahead. Greater China passed from Martin Lau to Helen Chen as lead in March 2025 with Martin staying as co-manager, and further Greater China succession is the reason a senior colleague has moved to Hong Kong.

## How would you characterise the team culture?

Collegiate and flat. The most junior person is expected to challenge the most senior, and it does not change when guests are in the room; consultants are welcome to sit in on a team meeting and see it. Within one philosophy there is a spread of temperament: some of us weight governance first, some franchise first. Rasmus sits nearer the franchise end and it shows in the emerging markets book.

## How are the team paid, and how are they aligned with clients?

Pay has four parts. A competitive base salary. An annual cash bonus tied to three- and five-year performance of all our funds: one team, one mission. The largest part is a share of the firm's contractual profit allocation, which vests after three years and is invested in our funds meanwhile. And since 2025, direct equity in the business through FSSA Holdings, which every team member took up.

Team members also hold a significant part of their own wealth in FSSA funds.

## How diverse is the team?

The team spans several nationalities and is split across Hong Kong, Singapore, Edinburgh and London. Recent hires came from Arisaig, Genesis, Matthews Asia, Polar Capital and Flipkart. Firm-wide diversity data is published by First Sentier.

## Portfolio characteristics

## How different are your portfolios from the index?

Very. GEM Focus holds 37 companies and active share has run around 80 percent. Much of that comes from underweights in index heavyweights rather than off-benchmark names. At the end of June 2026 GEM Focus held Taiwan at 18.5 percent against 27.3 percent in the index, Korea at 15.8 percent against 23.7 percent, and China at 25.7 percent against 19 percent, with large overweights in Brazil, Mexico and Peru.

## How would you describe the style?

Quality growth, bought with attention to price. The portfolio does not fit a style box. Sector labels mislead: our consumer-discretionary overweight is mostly platforms and e-commerce, which have held up like staples when economies weaken. We own no energy, materials or utilities companies today, because few of them compound through a cycle.

## How concentrated is the portfolio, and why around 35 stocks?

Thirty to forty names gives enough spread to survive being wrong about any one company and few enough that each position matters. Sizing starts from the gap between price and what we think a business is worth in five years, never from the index weight. At the end of June 2026 the top five were SK hynix at 9.9 percent, TSMC at 8.9 percent, MediaTek at 7.4 percent, Tencent at 7 percent and Samsung at 5.9 percent. Concentration has risen somewhat, as it has in the index, and the portfolio has always been concentrated. The index itself is now more concentrated than we are: Korea and Taiwan are about half of it.

## What is turnover?

Name turnover runs around 15 percent a year, dollar turnover about 20 percent: a holding period of roughly five years. It went to 30 percent in 2022 when prices moved hard against unchanged views. Turnover is a budget we track rather than a rule we obey. One process at two speeds: when the market hands us a violent move against an unchanged view, as with SK hynix in 2026, the same process produces a faster decision.

## Performance

## How have your strategies performed?

For GEM Focus, the UK OEIC, class B accumulation, in sterling, to the end of June 2026, the figures run as follows.

Over three months the fund returned 29.2 percent against 23.3 percent for MSCI Emerging Markets, ahead by 5.9 points.

Over six months, 19.7 percent against 25.5 percent, behind by 5.8 points.

Over one year, 30.3 percent against 48.2 percent, behind by 17.9 points.

Over three years, 56.3 percent against 78.4 percent, behind by 22.1 points.

Over five years, 53.3 percent against 47.3 percent, ahead by 6 points.

Since inception, 93.9 percent against 92.5 percent, ahead by 1.4 points.

So: behind over one and three years, ahead over five and since launch. The gap is concentrated in steep, narrow rallies. In 2025 the MSCI Emerging Markets Quality index returned 14 percent against 34 percent for the main MSCI Emerging Markets index, the widest gap since the quality index launched in 2012.

Asia Focus, Greater China and India show the same pattern over three years.

## In what markets do you do best and worst?

Our portfolios do better in ordinary markets, meaning index returns of roughly minus 15 to plus 15 percent, and in falling markets than in steep rallies. We expect upside capture a little over 100 and downside capture in the high 80s to low 90s over a cycle. The last year compressed that, because the rally was led by cyclical, leveraged and state-owned stocks we choose not to own.

## Why has the last 18 months been hard?

Three parts. The AI winners we own kept pace. The cyclical rally in state-owned banks, Korean industrials and materials we missed on purpose. And the platforms we own, Tencent, Trip.com, Sea, MercadoLibre and TOTVS, were de-rated as supposed AI losers while their earnings grew. In the twelve months to March 2026 re-rating explained 12.1 points of the index return and 2 points of ours.

## Fees

## What are your fees?

Fees and ongoing charges for each fund are in the current key investor information document and prospectus. Segregated mandates and pooled-fund share classes for institutions are agreed case by case. The governance agreement gives the team, rather than the parent, control over fees and capacity. Fees are never quoted from memory: the figure comes from the fund document for the share class in question, and mandate pricing goes to the client team.

## Corporate sustainability

## Are you a B Corp?

First Sentier Investors became a certified B Corporation on the fourteenth of November 2022 with a score of 107.2, against a pass mark of 80.

## What are your climate commitments, and how are you doing against them?

We assessed all 236 holdings with our climate tool in 2025 and engaged 25 companies on climate. We set a target of 25 percent of companies in the top tier of net-zero alignment by 2025 and reached 1 percent; the target was far too optimistic. We have moved to the Net Zero Investment Framework, with a goal of 80 percent of holdings aligned, aligning or committed by 2030 and 85 percent by 2035. Portfolio carbon intensity is far below the index: 32 for the emerging markets portfolio against 245 for its index, and 28 for India against 540, measured in tonnes of CO2 per million US dollars of sales.
