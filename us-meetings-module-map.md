# The Seven-Module Meeting Map

## The map

Seven modules. Every meeting is a walk through some subset of these; every question lands inside one of them. Here is the anchor line for the whole trip — use it twice per meeting: "We were right about AI — we own the winners. The market paid concentration, not correctness. We've been harvesting the winners and buying the quality it mislabelled as losers."

The seven modules, in brief.

Module one, Foundry. TSMC is a monopoly the index can't hold and we can't match — 14 percent of MSCI Emerging Markets, capped at 10 percent for us. We own the theme through the supply chain.

Module two, Memory. The best business the cycle ever built — and the 520-billion-dollar Korean cluster is the supply that ends it. Own on low multiples, trim, don't chase.

Module three, AI winners and losers. The market split emerging markets into "AI" and "everything else" and mispriced both. We sold the first into strength and bought the second.

Module four, why the "losers" defend. Five mechanisms: interfaces, licences, local data, physical networks, and AI-as-a-cost-lever. The deflation accrues to whoever owns the customer.

Module five, interfaces and agents. Value migrates to whoever owns the customer interface; agents are the only real threat to it. Emerging-market interface owners are stickier than the West's.

Module six, Financials. We own lots of banks because they are AI beneficiaries with licences — trust franchises where credit growth is the compounding engine.

Module seven, Asia, country by country. Foundry in Taiwan, memory in Korea, no credit demand in China, credit acceleration in India — our 10 percent — and tricky macro in Indonesia, with one small quality bank.

How the modules chain: one and two are the winners we own and trim. Three is the performance story that sits on top of them. Four and five are why the rest of the book isn't roadkill. Six is the biggest expression of four and five. Seven is all of it, arranged on a map of Asia.

## Module one — Foundry

"TSMC is the only company on earth that everyone in AI — Nvidia, Apple, AMD, Google — must use and cannot leave. The index holds it at 14 percent; UCITS caps us at 10 percent. We're structurally underweight the best business in our universe, so we own the theme through the chain around it."

The argument. Monopoly at the leading edge: essentially all sub-3-nanometre AI silicon runs through one company's process. Pricing power is proven — it raises prices and the customers thank it. Concentration is the index's problem, not ours: at about 14 percent of MSCI Emerging Markets, and about 57 percent of Taiwan's entire index weight, TSMC alone makes passive emerging markets a semiconductor bet. Our expression: TSMC about 9 percent, near our cap; MediaTek about 7.3 percent; Silergy about 2.1 percent — the theme via design and analog around the fab, all trimmed into strength on a look-through basis.

Numbers to hold. TSMC is about 14 percent of MSCI Emerging Markets; the top ten of the index is about 33 to 35 percent; TSMC plus Samsung plus Hynix alone are about 29 percent of the entire index. Taiwan is now the largest country in MSCI Emerging Markets, at about 21 percent, overtaking China.

Watch for the question: "Aren't you just underweight the winner?" Yes, by rule, modestly — and the binding constraint is really the 40 percent aggregate bucket, where our 5-percent-plus names sit at about 39.8 percent. The cap enforces the discipline we'd run anyway.

## Module two — Memory

"HBM turned memory from a commodity into a franchise — for a while. The 800-trillion-won Korean cluster is both proof of demand and the supply that ends the cycle. That's why we own it on low multiples and have been selling all the way up."

The argument. Hynix, at 10.5 percent, and Samsung, at 6.2 percent, are our two biggest active memory positions — Hynix trimmed hard on a look-through basis, its weight rose only because the stock roughly quadrupled; Samsung actively added as the HBM4 laggard catch-up. The Korea tape is late-cycle: the KOSPI roughly doubled to above 9,000; Samsung plus Hynix are more than 50 percent of the index; record retail margin debt, about 37.7 trillion won, while foreigners net sold a record of about 47 trillion won in May alone. Supply is coming: the 520-billion-dollar national cluster — four new fabs plus HBM lines, about 2,450 trillion won committed domestically by 2040. Memory cycles die of exactly this.

Numbers to hold. Hynix at 10.5 percent is a live passive UCITS breach being remedied in orderly fashion — the rules now force the trims we wanted to make anyway. Verify before quoting: Hynix's share-count on the roughly four-times move, and refresh live weights — a circuit-breaker day has already hit Korea.

Watch for the question: "Is this a super-cycle?" A two-handed answer: demand is real and structural, but 800 trillion won of new fabs is the textbook end of every memory cycle. Position for both: own it cheap, keep trimming.

## Module three — AI winners and losers

"This wasn't a market that rewarded being right about AI — we were right, we own the winners. It rewarded being concentrated in three or four mega-caps we're capped on, and punished everything without an AI label regardless of fundamentals."

The argument. We own the winners: TSMC, Hynix, Samsung, MediaTek, Silergy all made real money. The gap versus the index is structure — capped on the biggest names — plus dispersion, where the "loser" bucket got sold indiscriminately. We were net sellers of the winners all year — weights rose anyway because the stocks doubled to tripled. That's discipline outrunning price, not chasing. The next leg: extreme narrow leadership is the most mean-reverting setup in markets. We don't need the winners to fall — we need breadth, and we've pre-positioned by rotating winners into mispriced quality.

Numbers to hold. The AI and semis cluster went from about 21 percent to about 35 percent of the book over the first half, purely on price, despite net selling. The emerging-markets "earnings growth" headline — about high-teens for 2026 — is itself flattered by memory; the under-appreciated part is the non-AI earnings. An honesty beat: name one real selection miss alongside the structural story — candour is what makes the rest believable.

Watch for the question: "Aren't your banks and consumer names AI losers?" That's Module four. Bridge line: "the market's loser label is doing no underwriting at all — let me tell you what actually defends these businesses."

## Module four — Why the "losers" stay defensible

"AI destroys businesses whose moat was information asymmetry or clerical labour. It cannot conjure a banking licence, a deposit franchise, a logistics network, or thirty years of local underwriting data. Our 'losers' own those."

The five mechanisms. First, owning the customer interface. Super-apps and daily-use franchises — Tencent, MercadoLibre, Nubank — are the front door AI has to knock on; models become suppliers to them, not replacements. Second, licences and regulation. Banking, insurance, and exchange licences are government-granted moats AI can't cross; regulators will not let an unregulated model take deposits or write risk. Third, proprietary local data. Decades of credit files, claims histories, and merchant flows in markets with thin bureaus — the training data is the moat, and the incumbent owns it. Fourth, physical networks. Branches where trust is physical, pharmacy density, logistics — atoms, not bits. AI optimises these networks; it doesn't replicate them. Fifth, AI as a cost lever. For an incumbent with the customer and the licence, AI is deflation in the cost line — cost-to-income down, underwriting sharper. The technology's benefits accrue to whoever already owns the relationship.

Link to the book. Every mechanism maps to holdings: interfaces — Tencent, MercadoLibre, Nubank; licences plus data plus branches — ICICI, HDFC, Credicorp, Capitec, BCA, Regional; physical networks — Raia Drogasil, Walmex, FTA.

Watch for the question: "Isn't that what people said about newspapers?" Newspapers owned neither the licence nor the customer relationship; classified revenue was an information-asymmetry toll. Our test for every holding: which of the five mechanisms does it own, and is AI a cost lever or a moat solvent for it?

## Module five — Interfaces and agents

"In every computing shift, value ends up with whoever owns the interface to the customer. Models are becoming utilities; the interface is the scarce asset. The one genuine threat to that is the agent — software that shops on your behalf and doesn't care whose logo is on the door."

The argument. Models commoditise — every quarter cheaper, more interchangeable — while interfaces compound. The pricing power in AI is migrating up the stack to distribution — the same lesson as browsers, app stores, search. Emerging-market interface owners are stickier than the West's: WeChat is identity plus payments plus commerce in one; MercadoLibre and Nubank own payments and credit, not just eyeballs. Payments-anchored interfaces are the hardest to disintermediate. The agent counter-case: if agents mediate commerce, brand and interface loyalty weaken. Watch where agents actually transact — they still need rails, payments, logistics, credit, which is what our names own. Agents may kill the storefront; they can't skip the rails.

Link to the book. Tencent about 6.8 percent — weight eased on price, we likely added shares; confirm dealing records before saying "trimmed." MercadoLibre and Nubank in Latin America; the banks are interface businesses too — the deposit app is the interface to a licence.

Watch for the question: "Why not just own the model makers?" In emerging markets you mostly can't, and you don't need to: the economics show up in interfaces, silicon, and memory — all three of which we own.

## Module six — Financials

"We own a lot of banks, on purpose. A quality emerging-market bank is all five defensibility mechanisms in one wrapper — licence, trust, local data, distribution, and AI as a pure cost lever — attached to the one thing AI can't generate: credit growth."

The argument. Banks are AI beneficiaries, not victims: cost-to-income falls, underwriting improves, and the deposit franchise — the expensive part — is untouched. No model gets a banking licence. The engine is credit growth, and it's where you want it: India accelerating, at about 17 percent system credit growth, broad-based across services, industry, and retail; Latin America compounding — Credicorp added to 4.4 percent, plus Nubank and Regional; Capitec in South Africa. Rate-cycle nuance: easing has stalled across most of emerging markets — near-term that supports margins, it just delays the volume tailwind. Brazil is the one big market actively cutting: a tailwind for the Brazil book.

Link to the book. India is about 10 percent and it is mostly this: ICICI, HDFC, Kotak, ICICI Lombard — trimmed on valuation discipline, from 13.4 percent to about 10 percent, not conviction. Plus Credicorp, Capitec, Nubank, Regional, BCA, Qualitas. Financials are the single biggest expression of Modules four and five.

Watch for the question: "Fintech or AI disruption of banks?" We own the disruptor where it's winning — Nubank — and the incumbents where the licence-plus-deposit moat holds. The bar for disruption is the licence, not the app.

## Module seven — Asia, country by country

"Walk the map west from Taipei: foundry, memory, a credit economy that won't borrow, one that's finally borrowing again, and a tricky macro where we own exactly one high-quality bank. Our Asia is a set of deliberate, different bets — not an index residual."

Taiwan, about 21 percent of the index — the foundry. Module one lives here. TSMC is about 57 percent of the country's index weight; a hawkish-tilt central bank, exports booming. Us: TSMC about 9 percent, MediaTek about 7.3 percent, Silergy — trimmed into strength throughout.

Korea, about 16 percent of the index — the memory. Module two lives here. A leveraged retail AI rally, record foreign selling, the 520-billion-dollar cluster. MSCI denied developed-market status again in June — Korea won on deliverability, foreign-exchange hours, and investor-ID plumbing, not on the economy; a future promotion would pull Samsung plus Hynix out of emerging markets entirely, resolving the benchmark's concentration by reclassification.

China, more than 20 percent of the index — no credit growth. The sweep: an economy in a deflationary loop — new loans at multi-year lows, weak household and corporate demand, medium and long-term borrowing falling. Stimulus pushes on a string when nobody wants to borrow; consensus sees little acceleration in 2026. Us: cut from about 34 percent to about 25 percent — Prosus from 6.1 percent to 3.7 percent, staples and baijiu exited. What we keep is interface businesses and franchises that self-fund — Tencent the core — not credit-cycle exposure.

India, about 12 percent of the index — credit acceleration, our 10 percent. The one big Asian economy where credit is genuinely accelerating: system credit about 17 percent year on year, up from about 11 percent a year ago, broad-based — services about 19 percent, industry about 15 percent versus 8 percent in financial year 2025, retail about 16 percent. The Reserve Bank of India on hold; oil is the swing risk to the financial-year-2027 path. Us: about 10 percent of the fund, and it is mostly high-quality financials — ICICI, HDFC, Kotak, ICICI Lombard — the direct play on that credit line. Trimmed from 13.4 percent on valuation, which proves the discipline; the holdings are the conviction.

Indonesia, about 1 percent of the index — tricky macro, one quality bank. The macro is genuinely awkward: the rupiah through 18,000 to the dollar, the worst since the Asian Financial Crisis era; Bank Indonesia forced into off-cycle hikes to defend it; fiscal and monetary policy pulling opposite directions; capital-control-flavoured foreign-exchange rules; Danantara governance questions — and MSCI reviewing a possible emerging-market-to-frontier demotion into November. Us: one small position, and it's the counter-example — BCA is high-free-float, transparent, over-capitalised, exactly the name that screens fine on every MSCI concern. Small exposure to the macro, quality exposure to the franchise.

A structural stat to deploy anywhere in this module: three companies — TSMC, Samsung, Hynix — are about 29 percent of the entire emerging-markets index. The consultant's country question is really a concentration question, and this map is the answer.
