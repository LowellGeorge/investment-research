# Computer Age Management Services — The Seven Questions

Computer Age Management Services, known as CAMS, is the eighteenth-largest position in the GEM portfolio at two-point-three percent, with a market cap of roughly four to five billion US dollars. Here is CAMS in seven questions.

---

## Cold open

CAMS just posted its third-quarter fiscal-2026 results — a quarter in which the company hit record revenue, record earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, and a forty-six-percent margin on a revenue line that grew only five-and-a-half percent year-on-year. The underlying numbers are extraordinary: return on net worth above forty percent, return on capital employed close to forty percent, market share in Indian mutual-fund services at around sixty-eight percent, and a sixty-five-percent dividend payout ratio. The non-mutual-fund business, which includes payments, insurance and alternatives, grew nearly twenty-five percent and now contributes fourteen-and-a-half percent of total revenue.

This is the Indian mutual-fund registrar duopoly compounder that most international investors have never looked at, running one of the highest return-on-capital businesses in emerging markets.

Seven questions.

---

## Question One: What is it?

CAMS is the dominant registrar and transfer agent for Indian mutual funds, operating as one leg of a duopoly with KFin Technologies. The business services twenty-nine asset-management companies, capturing around sixty-eight percent of industry mutual-fund assets under management. In practice, every time an Indian mutual-fund investor buys, redeems or rebalances a unit, a significant share of that transaction passes through CAMS-operated back-office systems.

The revenue structure has two layers. The mutual-fund layer is the core: transaction processing, record-keeping, know-your-customer services, investor communications. The non-mutual-fund layer is the diversification engine: CAMSPay (payment-processing and account-aggregator services, revenue up fifty-nine percent), the KRA business (registered intermediary KYC records, now the second-largest in the market after the NSE KRA integration), CAMSRep (insurance repository, forty percent market share, revenue up fifteen percent), and the alternatives business (private-equity fund administration, assets crossing three lakh crore rupees, revenue up sixteen percent).

The quarterly numbers tell the structural story. Consolidated revenue in the third quarter of fiscal 2026 reached three-point-nine billion rupees, up five-and-a-half percent year-on-year. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation reached a record one-point-eight billion rupees with a forty-six percent margin. Standalone net profit was one-point-two billion rupees. Return on net worth was forty-point-two percent.

The thing to internalise: CAMS is not an Indian financial-services company. It is a regulated-infrastructure duopoly with fixed costs, scalable technology, and pricing determined by the underlying growth of Indian financial savings. Once an asset-management company is on the CAMS platform, switching is structurally expensive and operationally risky — so revenue compounds with industry assets rather than with separate sales effort.

## Question Two: Can we trust them?

Yes — and the 2022-to-2024 take-rate-pressure cycle is the evidence.

Anuj Kumar is Chief Executive Officer. The company was spun off from a private-equity ownership structure and listed on Indian exchanges in 2020. Governance is through a broadly independent board with professional management, rather than promoter-family control — unusual among Indian mid-caps, and a quality signal.

The past-stress case that matters is the 2022-to-2024 industry regulatory cycle. Indian securities regulators reviewed mutual-fund expense-ratio caps, pressuring asset-management companies to reduce fees. The same pressure flowed through to registrars in the form of contract-renewal concessions. CAMS absorbed the take-rate pressure, diversified aggressively into non-mutual-fund revenue streams — payments, insurance, alternatives — and held the core EBITDA margin. Through the pressure period, margins went from the low-forty percent range to the mid-forty range. That is the operating discipline that matters: when pricing falls, efficiency rises.

The capital discipline signals from the latest print are straightforward. Forty-six percent earnings margin in the quarter. Return on capital employed close to forty percent. Dividend payout ratio maintained at sixty-five percent — the company returns the majority of every rupee of profit to shareholders. Cost growth deliberately contained at ten percent year-on-year, below the internal eleven-percent target. The board approved a share split from ten rupee face value to two rupee face value, improving liquidity. An interim dividend of three-and-a-half rupees per share was declared after the split.

The watch item is the absence of a founder-controlling-shareholder. Professional-management governance is a quality signal but means that succession discipline must be institutional rather than family-driven. Worth tracking leadership tenure carefully.

## Question Three: Why does value compound?

Three engines, plus a kicker.

The first engine is the Indian mutual-fund structural growth. Indian mutual-fund industry assets have compounded at roughly fifteen percent annually for a decade, driven by systematic investment plan registrations, retail participation, and the overall financialisation of Indian household savings. CAMS's mutual-fund servicing revenue compounds with industry assets. Equity assets under management crossed thirty lakh crore rupees — about three-point-six trillion US dollars — with CAMS servicing a sixty-six-percent share. New systematic investment plan registrations grew eighteen percent year-on-year, with CAMS holding a sixty-five percent market share. Every new retail investor entering the mutual-fund system generates recurring CAMS revenue.

The second engine is the duopoly-pricing protection. With only KFin Technologies as a credible competitor, pricing is rational. Switching costs are high because integrations are deep, regulatory compliance is at stake, and migration risk is real. The duopoly structure means revenue per basis point stays reasonably stable even through regulatory pressure periods.

The third engine is the non-mutual-fund diversification flywheel. Non-mutual-fund revenue is now fourteen-and-a-half percent of total and growing at nearly twenty-five percent year-on-year. CAMSPay revenue up fifty-nine percent. Alternatives assets crossing three lakh crore. CAMSRep insurance repository at forty percent market share. The non-mutual-fund earnings margin is thirteen percent today, up from sub-ten a few years ago, with a target of twenty-plus percent within three years. As non-mutual-fund revenue grows and its margin expands, blended group margins and growth both accelerate.

The kicker is the new-product adjacency flow. Recent new products include SIF — specialised investment funds — and GIFT City retail funds, Tata Mutual Fund launched the industry's first retail inbound fund in GIFT City. Each new regulated product category in Indian financial services creates a new CAMS mandate opportunity. The regulatory complexity that creates the moat also creates the incremental revenue.

## Question Four: What proves us right, and what to track?

Six specific metrics.

First, mutual-fund market share — sixty-eight percent today, stable is the quality test. Second, non-mutual-fund revenue as a percentage of total — fourteen-and-a-half percent today, progression toward twenty-five percent medium-term is the diversification test. Third, earnings margin — forty-six percent today, sustained above forty-five percent is the pricing-discipline test. Fourth, new AMC mandate wins — twenty-nine AMCs today, the Carnelian Asset Management mandate was added in the quarter; watch contract additions. Fifth, return on net worth — forty percent today, sustained above thirty-five percent confirms the capital-efficient compounder profile. Sixth, SIP market share — sixty-five percent today, stable or growing confirms the retail-flow capture.

## Question Five: What's it worth?

The honest framing. Q3 fiscal 2026 earnings per share on a standalone basis was four-point-nine-three rupees not annualised — so roughly twenty rupees annualised on the restated post-split base. Dividend payout is sixty-five percent. Return on net worth is forty-point-two percent. Cash and cash equivalents at year-end December were about one hundred billion rupees or one-point-two billion US dollars.

On trading multiples, CAMS trades at roughly thirty-five to forty times forward earnings. That is on the high side of global financial-infrastructure comparables, but is justified by forty-percent return on capital, sixty-eight-percent market share in a structurally-growing industry, sixty-five-percent dividend payout, and forty-plus-percent non-mutual-fund growth.

The simple framing: a regulated duopoly registrar in the fastest-growing major savings market globally, running at forty-percent return on capital with a sixty-five-percent dividend payout, trading at mid-thirty-times forward earnings. Comparable set: Indian stock exchanges, depository services, custodians — BSE, CDSL — trade at similar or higher multiples for similar financial-infrastructure characteristics.

The key sensitivity is Indian mutual-fund industry growth. A sharp deceleration in systematic investment plan flows or a regulatory cap on total-expense ratios would compress both revenue and multiple.

## Question Six: Where is the market wrong?

Two variant perception angles.

First: consensus treats CAMS as an Indian small-cap or mid-cap financial-services name with limited analyst coverage outside India, priced as cyclical financials. The variant is that CAMS is a regulated-infrastructure duopoly with the same business characteristics as stock exchanges, depositories, and credit bureaus — businesses that globally trade at premium multiples because the regulatory moat is unusually deep. The non-mutual-fund diversification is taking CAMS away from a single-product-dependence concern and toward a genuine financial-infrastructure conglomerate.

Second: the non-mutual-fund business is priced as negligible, when it is growing nearly twenty-five percent year-on-year at thirteen-percent margins and targeting twenty-plus-percent margins within three years. Once the non-mutual-fund revenue crosses twenty percent of total and the margin starts converging with the mutual-fund margin, blended group growth accelerates and the multiple reflects it.

The honest counter: Indian small-and-mid-cap equity volatility is real. The premium multiple leaves no room for disappointment, and any mutual-fund industry deceleration hits the stock disproportionately. Position sizing should reflect that a growth disappointment compresses the multiple rapidly.

## Question Seven: What could kill it, and when do we sell?

The real risks, ranked.

One: mutual-fund industry regulatory intervention. A total-expense-ratio cap or a registrar fee cap would compress revenue. Two: Indian equity market correction. Equity assets under management is around seventy percent of the mutual-fund industry; a sharp drawdown compresses CAMS fees. Three: KRA pricing pressure. Ongoing industry discussions on KRA pricing could reduce the non-mutual-fund margin. Four: major AMC contract loss. Losing a top-five asset-management company mandate would be a material revenue event. Five: new entrant. Indian regulators could authorise a third registrar-and-transfer-agent to break the duopoly — low probability but a tail risk. Six: fintech disintermediation — direct-to-investor platforms gaining scale independent of the current registrar infrastructure.

Specific sell triggers. Earnings margin falling below forty percent for two consecutive quarters without a clear one-off — pricing discipline breaking. Mutual-fund market share declining meaningfully for two consecutive quarters — duopoly structure cracking. Non-mutual-fund revenue growth decelerating below fifteen percent for two consecutive quarters — diversification thesis breaking. Systematic investment plan market share loss. A material adverse regulatory ruling on total-expense ratios or KRA pricing. Anuj Kumar leaving without a credible internal successor.
