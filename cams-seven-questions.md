# CAMS — Seven Questions

An investment summary of Computer Age Management Services — India's dominant mutual-fund registrar and transfer agent — prepared on the 2nd of July 2026, through a lens of quality and long-term ownership.

## Question One: Business Description — What Is It?

CAMS is the tollbooth of Indian mutual-fund savings: the country's largest registrar and transfer agent, servicing about 68 percent of industry assets — 55.1 lakh crore rupees of average assets under management — in a settled duopoly with KFin Technologies at about 31 percent. It runs the mission-critical back office — transaction processing, systematic-investment-plan administration, investor records, KYC, distributor commissions — for 10 of the top 15 asset managers, earning tiered basis-point fees on assets under management. Around this core it has built a set of adjacent platforms — CAMSPay in payments, CAMS Alternatives in alternative-investment-fund servicing, CAMS KRA in KYC registration, enlarged by the acquisition of the NSE KRA book, the Bima Central insurance repository, and an account aggregator — which together reached 15.3 percent of revenue in financial year 2026 and are growing at 20 to 25 percent. It is Chennai-headquartered, listed in October 2020, asset-light, and debt-free.

## Question Two: Management and Governance — Can We Trust Them?

CAMS is that comparatively rare Indian listed company with no controlling promoter: Warburg Pincus's vehicle sold down post-IPO, leaving a board-led, institutionally owned business — foreign institutional investors at about 55 percent — chaired by Dinesh Kumar Mehrotra, with Anuj Kumar as managing director since 2018, recently re-appointed for five years. As a SEBI-regulated market intermediary it lives under continuous supervisory scrutiny, which reinforces conservatism. The numbers carry the governance signature we look for: about 39 percent return on equity, no leverage, 854 crore rupees of cash, and a payout of about 65 percent — 305 crore rupees in financial year 2026 — rather than empire-building. Cost discipline is explicit — operating-expense growth capped below 9 percent and employee costs below 5 percent for financial year 2027 — and mergers and acquisitions have been confined to small, adjacent bolt-ons — Fintuple, Think Analytics, the NSE KRA book — measured in tens of crores, not thousands. The watch item is the flip side of no promoter: board quality and managing-director succession matter more here than in founder-led peers.

## Question Three: Investment Thesis — Why Does Value Compound?

A toll on India's savings formalisation: mutual-fund industry average assets under management grew 19.2 percent in financial year 2026 and systematic-investment-plan culture keeps broadening; CAMS's fee pool is contractually tied to that asset base.

A duopoly with prohibitive switching costs: an asset manager changing its registrar and transfer agent risks its entire investor record and servicing continuity; share has been stable at about 68 percent, and CAMS's roughly 65 percent share of new systematic-investment-plan registrations effectively pre-books tomorrow's assets.

Yield compression is the toll, volume the engine: tiered pricing means revenue grows slower than assets under management — in financial year 2026, revenue up 6.6 percent on assets up 21 percent — but management expects future yield decline below the historical 3 to 3.5 percent per year, leaving low-double-digit revenue growth through the cycle.

Non-mutual-fund optionality reaching scale: 15.3 percent of revenue growing more than 20 percent — KRA up 28 percent, Alternatives up 25 percent, CAMSPay up 23 percent in financial year 2026 — with segment margin guided from 16.5 percent to about 20 percent, a second compounding leg on the same rails.

Quality economics: more than 45 percent EBITDA margins, about 39 percent return on equity, negligible capital intensity, high cash conversion, and a generous payout — earnings growth is not bought with balance-sheet risk.

## Question Four: Key Milestones and KPIs — What Proves Us Right, and What to Track?

Mutual-fund average-assets growth and CAMS share — share holding at about 68 percent, and about 65 percent of new systematic-investment-plan registrations, is the single most important confirmation.

Yield compression — mutual-fund yield decline staying below the guided roughly 3 percent per year; a steeper print would break the low-double-digit revenue algorithm.

Non-mutual-fund delivery — more than 20 percent revenue growth sustained and segment margin reaching about 20 percent; KRA revenue holding flat in financial year 2027 despite the 20 percent industry price cut.

Cost discipline and margin — operating-expense growth less than 9 percent, EBITDA margin holding around 45 to 46.5 percent; the next-generation transaction platform live in the first half of financial year 2027.

And a consensus check, from FactSet — the Street models revenue of 1,703 crore rupees in estimated financial year 2027 rising to 2,182 crore by financial year 2029, a compound growth rate of about 13 percent, and earnings per share of 22.5 rupees rising to 30.1, a compound growth rate of about 16 percent; rating consensus is a buy — 19 of 21 positive.

On the financials, in crore rupees on a consolidated basis: total revenue rose from 1,136.5 in financial year 2024, to 1,422.5 in 2025, to 1,516.2 in 2026. Net profit went from 351.0, to 464.7, to 472.0. Profit-after-tax margin ran 30.9 percent, then 32.7 percent, then 31.1 percent. And basic earnings per share, split-adjusted, went from 14.4 rupees, to 19.1, to 19.2. Note that financial years end the 31st of March; the 2024 and 2025 earnings-per-share figures are adjusted for the financial-year-2026 five-for-one share subdivision, which raised the share count from about 49.4 million to about 248 million; and financial-year-2026 profit was held back by the KRA price cut and a 25 crore rupee asset write-down.

## Question Five: Five-Year Fair Value and Return — the Team's View

This is not a cyclical in the industrial sense, but revenue is leveraged to Indian equity-market levels through assets under management, so a fair-value view should assume at least one flat-to-down market year in five. At 792 rupees the stock trades on about 41 times trailing and about 35 times estimated financial-year-2027 consensus earnings — well below its post-IPO norm after the soft financial year 2026.

Illustrative scenarios, as placeholders for the team's house numbers, with financial-year-2026 earnings per share of 19.2 rupees as the base. The bull case: assets-under-management compounding persists and non-mutual-fund margins inflect — about 17 percent earnings-per-share compound growth to about 42 rupees by financial year 2031 on a mid-30s exit multiple gives a mid-teens internal rate of return, including about 2 percent dividend yield. The base case: consensus-like earnings-per-share compound growth of about 13 to 14 percent to about 36 rupees on a roughly 30 times exit multiple gives a high-single-digit to about 10 percent internal rate of return. The bear case: a prolonged equity bear market plus regulatory fee pressure holds earnings-per-share growth to about 7 percent and the multiple de-rates to about 20 to 22 times — a modestly negative internal rate of return even with dividends. The asymmetry is acceptable only because the franchise itself is very unlikely to be impaired in the bear case — the multiple is the risk, not the moat.

## Question Six: Variant Perception — Where the Market Is Wrong

After financial year 2026 delivered just 6.6 percent revenue growth and near-flat profit-after-tax against a 21 percent surge in assets under management, the market has begun pricing CAMS as a structurally fading toll — the multiple has de-rated to about 35 times forward from a much richer history. The variant view is that financial year 2026 was a compression year, not a new trend: it absorbed a one-off 20 percent industry-wide KRA price cut, a telescoping-tier reset triggered by unusually fast asset growth, and an asset write-down — while the volume engine strengthened, with record systematic-investment-plan registrations, 65 percent incremental share, and record equity net sales. Consensus already shows the arithmetic normalising to about 13 percent revenue and about 16 percent earnings-per-share growth over financial years 2027 to 2029, and 19 of 21 analysts are positive; the multiple hasn't followed. The second underappreciated leg is mix: non-mutual-fund at 15 percent of revenue growing more than 20 percent with margins guided from 16.5 percent to 20 percent quietly adds a growth business to what is priced as a mature annuity. The market is right that yields will keep compressing; it likely overweights the toll and underweights the traffic.

## Question Seven: Risks, Premortem, and Sell Discipline

The premortem — if this goes wrong in five years, the likely causes are these. A regulatory fee squeeze: SEBI's new Mutual Funds Regulations of 2026 and total-expense-ratio rationalisation pressure asset-manager economics, which is passed through to registrar-and-transfer-agent fees faster than the guided roughly 3 percent per year. A market drawdown: a multi-year Indian equity bear market shrinks assets-under-management-linked revenue against a partly fixed cost base. Competitive erosion: KFin wins new-fund mandates on price, or a large asset manager in-sources or dual-sources, chipping the roughly 68 percent share. Operational or cyber failure: CAMS is systemically embedded; a serious data or processing incident would carry regulatory and franchise consequences disproportionate to its size. And succession risk: with no promoter anchor, a poorly handled managing-director transition or board drift would erode the governance premium.

Sell discipline: trim or exit on, first, sustained mutual-fund-share loss toward 60 percent or visible loss of a top-five asset-manager mandate; second, yield compression persistently running above about 5 percent per year; third, direct regulatory capping of registrar-and-transfer-agent fees; or fourth, capital allocation turning to large, unrelated acquisitions. Tolerate — indeed expect — soft years driven by market levels; those are opportunities, not triggers.
