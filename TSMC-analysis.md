# TSMC — Investment Analysis

**Date:** 15 March 2026
**Disclaimer:** This analysis is based on training data through mid-2025. Figures and leadership details should be verified against the most recent filings.

---

## 1. Business History

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company was founded in 1987 by Morris Chang in Hsinchu, Taiwan, backed by the Taiwan government through its development fund (holding a 48% stake) and Philips Electronics (27.5%). Chang, then 55, had spent 25 years at Texas Instruments rising to group vice president before a brief stint at General Instrument. His insight was structural: semiconductor companies wasted billions maintaining fabs that ran below capacity, while fabless designers lacked access to manufacturing. TSMC would manufacture chips exclusively for other companies, owning no competing product lines — the pure-play foundry model. At the time, the idea was considered commercially unviable by much of the industry; dedicated foundries did not exist as a category.

The first years were difficult. TSMC's initial 6-inch fab in Hsinchu ran at roughly 30% utilization, and the company was unprofitable in its first year of production (1988). The critical early inflection came in the late 1980s and early 1990s as the fabless semiconductor industry emerged — companies like Qualcomm (founded 1985), Broadcom (1991), and Nvidia (1993) needed a trusted manufacturing partner without product conflicts. TSMC's neutrality became its defining competitive advantage. The company listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange in 1994 and issued ADRs on the NYSE in 1997, broadening its capital access.

Through the 1990s and 2000s, TSMC systematically advanced through process nodes, but the pivotal structural shift came as Moore's Law economics changed. At the 28nm node (circa 2011–2012) and below, the capital required for leading-edge fabs escalated so sharply that competitors began dropping out. GlobalFoundries abandoned 7nm development in 2018. Samsung remained but struggled with yields. TSMC's decision to invest aggressively in extreme ultraviolet lithography (EUV) at 7nm and 5nm, committing billions before customer revenue materialized, proved decisive. By 2020, TSMC was producing Apple's A-series and M-series chips, Nvidia's data center GPUs, and AMD's Zen processors — effectively becoming the sole high-volume manufacturer of the world's most advanced silicon.

TSMC has made relatively few acquisitions, preferring organic growth. The notable exception was its 2009 acquisition of WaferTech (its U.S. joint venture) and ongoing investments in packaging technologies like CoWoS and InFO, which blurred the line between foundry and assembly. The company's geographic expansion — announced fabs in Arizona (2020), Japan (Kumamoto, with Sony, 2021), and Germany (with Bosch, Infineon, and NXP, 2023) — represents the most significant structural change in its history, driven by geopolitical pressure to diversify beyond Taiwan.

Chang retired twice — first as CEO in 2005 (returning in 2009 after a period under Rick Tsai), then permanently as chairman in 2018, handing leadership to C.C. Wei and Mark Liu. Throughout these transitions, the cultural DNA has proven remarkably stable: an engineering-first discipline, obsessive focus on yield improvement, and the foundational principle that TSMC never competes with its customers. That neutrality, established in 1987 as a necessity to attract skeptical first clients, has endured as perhaps the most valuable intangible asset in the semiconductor industry.

## 2. Management & Incentives

TSMC's leadership transition from founder Morris Chang, who retired as chairman in June 2018, stands as one of the most successful succession events in global semiconductor history. The dual-leadership structure that initially replaced Chang — Mark Liu as chairman and C.C. Wei as CEO — has executed with remarkable consistency, delivering on aggressive capital expenditure plans and technology roadmap commitments through multiple cycles.

C.C. Wei, who has served as CEO since 2018 and was previously co-CEO alongside Liu from 2013, is a career TSMC veteran with over three decades at the company. His background is deeply technical, having led operations and advanced technology development before ascending to the top role. Wei has overseen the successful ramp of 7nm, 5nm, and 3nm process nodes largely on schedule, a track record that is extraordinary given the compounding complexity of leading-edge fabrication. Mark Liu, chairman since 2018, similarly spent his entire career at TSMC and provides complementary expertise in research and development. The pair's chemistry and division of responsibilities — Liu on strategic and external matters, Wei on operations and customer relationships — has functioned smoothly. However, both men are now in their late sixties or early seventies, making next-generation succession planning a live concern. TSMC has not publicly named successors, though the company has historically cultivated deep bench strength among senior vice presidents overseeing operations, R&D, and business development.

Insider ownership at TSMC is modest in percentage terms, which is typical for a company of its $500-billion-plus market capitalization. Directors and senior management collectively hold a fraction of one percent. That said, executive compensation includes equity-linked components — bonuses are tied to profitability metrics and operational targets, with a meaningful portion of variable pay delivered through profit-sharing and stock-based awards. Fixed salaries for top executives are relatively modest by U.S. standards; the variable component, linked to return on equity and earnings growth, can be several multiples of base pay, aligning incentives with shareholder returns.

Capital allocation has been disciplined and transparent. Management telegraphed the move to $30-billion-plus annual capital expenditure well in advance and has delivered returns on invested capital consistently above its weighted average cost of capital, typically in the 20–30% ROIC range. Free cash flow generation has been robust even amid record spending cycles, and the dividend has grown steadily, reflecting confidence in sustainable earnings power. The decision to build fabs in Arizona, Japan, and Germany was communicated clearly, with management realistic about cost premiums outside Taiwan.

The board comprises a mix of independent directors and TSMC veterans, with independents representing a majority. Notable independent directors have included academics, former government officials, and global business leaders, providing genuine oversight rather than rubber-stamping. TSMC operates on a one-share-one-vote basis — no dual-class structure.

On credibility, TSMC management has an exceptional record of doing what it says it will do. Technology node timelines, capital expenditure guidance, and margin targets have been met or exceeded with unusual regularity over the past decade. The few misses — such as modest delays in early 3nm yield optimization — were communicated proactively. This reliability is a core reason the stock commands a premium multiple relative to semiconductor peers. The key risk remains concentration: Wei and Liu are not easily replaceable, and TSMC's institutional knowledge resides heavily in a relatively small leadership cohort in Hsinchu. Investors should monitor succession disclosures closely in coming proxy seasons, as a generational leadership transition is likely within the next two to four years.

## 3. Competitive Landscape

TSMC dominates the pure-play foundry market with an estimated 55–60% share of total global foundry revenue as of 2024, a figure that climbs above 90% when isolating production at the most advanced process nodes (sub-7nm). This concentration reflects a structural dynamic in which semiconductor fabrication has become one of the most capital- and knowledge-intensive industries in the world, with barriers to entry that are effectively insurmountable for new entrants and increasingly difficult for incumbents to overcome.

The competitive field is narrow. Samsung Foundry, TSMC's closest rival in leading-edge logic, has struggled with yield issues at its 3nm GAA (gate-all-around) node and has failed to win significant volume from top-tier fabless designers beyond its captive Samsung LSI division. Intel Foundry Services, rebranded under Pat Gelsinger's IDM 2.0 strategy, is attempting to re-enter the merchant foundry business but remains at least one to two process generations behind TSMC; its Intel 18A node targets parity with TSMC's N2, but volume production timelines have repeatedly slipped. GlobalFoundries exited the leading-edge race entirely in 2018 and now competes in mature and specialty nodes, a profitable but structurally different business. SMIC, China's national champion, has demonstrated limited ability to produce 7nm-class chips using multi-patterning DUV lithography, but remains cut off from EUV equipment under U.S. export controls, capping its technological ceiling for the foreseeable future.

TSMC's competitive advantages compound in a self-reinforcing cycle. Its technology leadership — it was first to volume production at 7nm, 5nm, and 3nm — attracts the highest-value customers (Apple, NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm), whose design wins generate the revenue and margin to fund the next generation of capital expenditure, which exceeded $30 billion annually by 2024. This creates powerful ecosystem effects: EDA vendors, IP providers, and OSAT partners optimize their tools and processes around TSMC's design rules first, raising switching costs for fabless companies that would need to re-qualify designs on a competing foundry at significant cost and schedule risk. The result is a customer base that is deeply locked in, evidenced by Apple's unbroken reliance on TSMC for every A-series and M-series chip since the A9.

Pricing power follows directly. TSMC has implemented successive wafer price increases — notably 6–9% in 2022 and further increases for N3E and N2 — with minimal customer attrition, because there is no credible alternative supplier at the frontier. Gross margins have expanded into the 53–58% range, exceptional for a capital-intensive manufacturer.

The durability of these advantages is high but not absolute. The most plausible erosion paths include: sustained U.S. and allied government subsidies enabling Intel to close the technology gap (the CHIPS Act allocated $52.7 billion, with Intel receiving the largest tranche); a geopolitical disruption involving Taiwan that forces customers to diversify supply chains regardless of cost; or a paradigm shift in compute architecture — such as chiplet-based heterogeneous integration or novel materials — that reduces the importance of monolithic leading-edge density. Samsung's pivot to GAA transistor architecture at 3nm before TSMC was a technical gamble that has not yet paid commercial dividends, but it illustrates that architectural innovation could in theory leapfrog incremental node scaling.

In the value chain, TSMC sits at the critical bottleneck between fabless chip designers and end markets spanning AI training, smartphones, automotive, and data centers. Its position is analogous to that of a toll bridge: virtually every advanced logic chip must pass through its fabs. This structural position, combined with a multi-year technology lead, deep customer integration, and a capital expenditure cadence that competitors cannot match, makes TSMC's competitive moat among the widest in the semiconductor industry.

## 4. Financial Characteristics

TSMC's financial profile is exceptional among semiconductor companies and, indeed, among capital-intensive manufacturers globally. The company operates a pure-play foundry model, generating revenue entirely from fabricating chips designed by its customers rather than selling proprietary products. This asset-light-for-clients, asset-heavy-for-TSMC structure creates significant barriers to entry and extraordinary returns once scale is achieved.

Revenue mix has shifted decisively toward High Performance Computing (HPC), which now represents roughly 40–45% of total revenue and has been the primary growth driver, fueled by AI accelerator and data center demand. Smartphones contribute approximately 30–35%, down from historical dominance but still substantial given TSMC's near-monopoly on leading-edge mobile processors. IoT and automotive each account for mid-single-digit to low-double-digit percentage shares, with automotive growing steadily as chip content per vehicle rises. Geographically, North America dominates at around 65–70% of revenue, reflecting the concentration of TSMC's largest customers — Apple, NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm, and Broadcom — while China contributes roughly 10% and has been managed carefully amid geopolitical constraints.

Margins are the clearest marker of TSMC's competitive moat. Gross margins have generally operated in the 53–58% range in recent years, a remarkable level for a business with annual capital expenditures routinely exceeding $30 billion. Operating margins run in the 42–48% range, and net margins typically fall between 35–42%. These figures are substantially above those of other capital-intensive manufacturers and reflect both pricing power at leading-edge nodes and disciplined cost management. Margins do exhibit some cyclicality, compressing modestly during inventory corrections before recovering as utilization rates normalize, but the amplitude of these swings is notably more moderate than that experienced by memory manufacturers or integrated device makers.

Return on equity has consistently exceeded 25% and has frequently approached or surpassed 30%, while return on invested capital generally sits in the 20–30% range. These returns are extraordinary given the capital intensity of the business, where annual capex has scaled from roughly $10–15 billion five years ago toward $30–36 billion more recently, directed primarily at building advanced 3nm and 2nm capacity as well as overseas fabs in Arizona, Japan, and Germany. Approximately 70–80% of capex is growth-oriented rather than maintenance, underscoring the company's aggressive capacity expansion posture.

Cash conversion is strong. TSMC typically converts a high proportion of net income into free cash flow, though in periods of peak investment spending, free cash flow yield compresses. The balance sheet carries a net cash position or very modest net debt, with a debt-to-equity ratio well below 0.5x, affording substantial financial flexibility. Working capital dynamics are favorable: the company collects receivables efficiently and manages inventory tightly relative to the complexity of its manufacturing processes. TSMC's dividend policy returns a meaningful portion of earnings to shareholders while preserving ample reinvestment capacity.

What distinguishes TSMC's financial profile is the combination of foundry-model scalability, leading-edge technology premiums, and disciplined capital allocation that produces semiconductor-industry-leading margins and returns despite bearing the full burden of fabrication capital expenditure — a financial outcome no competitor has been able to replicate at comparable scale.

## 5. Red Flags

TSMC's single greatest vulnerability is existential and unhedgeable: the company fabricates roughly 90% of the world's most advanced semiconductors on an island that China claims as sovereign territory. No amount of financial engineering or operational excellence mitigates the risk that a Chinese blockade, invasion, or even sustained military intimidation campaign around Taiwan could halt global advanced chip production overnight. The U.S. CHIPS Act and TSMC's own overseas fab buildouts are implicit acknowledgments that this concentration risk is intolerable — yet those fabs will not reach meaningful scale before the late 2020s at the earliest, and even then will represent a fraction of Taiwan-based capacity. Investors should note that TSMC's insurance coverage for war and political violence is, for all practical purposes, nonexistent at the scale of loss a cross-strait conflict would entail. The company's stock trades as though this tail risk has a near-zero probability; any re-pricing of that assumption would be severe and sudden.

On governance, TSMC benefits from the legacy reputation of founder Morris Chang, but the company's relationship with the Taiwanese government warrants scrutiny. The government, through the National Development Fund and related entities, remains a significant shareholder, and Taiwan's strategic interest in keeping TSMC domestically anchored creates a principal-agent tension with minority shareholders who might prefer faster geographic diversification. Board independence, while improved over the years, still operates within a culture where government priorities carry implicit weight. The dual-listing structure (TWSE primary, NYSE ADRs) also means ADR holders have structurally weaker governance rights and must navigate foreign jurisdiction risk in any shareholder dispute.

Customer concentration is an underappreciated fragility. Apple alone accounts for roughly a quarter of revenue, and NVIDIA's share is climbing rapidly as AI accelerator demand surges. If Apple were to bring even a modest portion of chip design in-house for non-cutting-edge nodes, or if NVIDIA's growth trajectory disappoints, the revenue impact would be immediate and concentrated. TSMC's supply chain itself has a single point of failure: ASML is the sole manufacturer of EUV lithography systems essential for nodes at 7nm and below. Any disruption to ASML — whether from Dutch export control tightening, production delays, or force majeure — directly constrains TSMC's capacity expansion.

The overseas fab strategy, while strategically necessary, is financially punishing. TSMC has acknowledged that Arizona manufacturing costs run 50% or more above Taiwan levels, driven by higher labor costs, regulatory complexity, and the absence of the deeply integrated supplier ecosystem that exists around Hsinchu. The Japan and Germany fabs face similar, if somewhat less extreme, cost headwinds. These projects represent tens of billions in incremental capex that will dilute returns on invested capital for years before reaching full utilization. Management has guided for long-term gross margins in the 53% range, but if geographic diversification costs prove stickier than expected — or if subsidies arrive slower or smaller than anticipated — margin compression toward the high 40s is plausible.

Taiwan's seismic risk is perennially cited but genuinely material: the April 2024 Hualien earthquake temporarily disrupted production, and TSMC's fabs, despite advanced seismic engineering, sit in one of the world's most active earthquake zones. A direct hit on the Tainan or Hsinchu science parks during a period of full utilization would cause months of disruption.

Historically, TSMC has navigated downturns with discipline, but the 2008–2009 cycle saw revenue drop over 25%, and the post-pandemic inventory correction of 2022–2023 demonstrated that even TSMC is not immune to the semiconductor cycle. The current AI-driven supercycle invites comparison to prior capacity buildouts that ended in oversupply — the 2000 dot-com bust being the most extreme precedent.

The bear case is straightforward: TSMC spends $40 billion-plus annually on capex to build fabs in expensive geographies, AI demand plateaus or fragments across competing architectures, margins compress by 500-plus basis points, and the geopolitical risk premium on Taiwan re-rates upward following a Chinese military escalation. In that scenario, the stock could trade at 12–14x earnings rather than the 20x-plus it commands today, implying 30–40% downside.

Investors should be pressing management on several points: What is the contractual commitment from the U.S. and other governments on subsidy disbursement timing and clawback conditions? What is the realistic timeline for Arizona to reach cost parity, if ever? How does TSMC model the scenario where its two largest customers collectively represent over 40% of revenue and one of them diversifies foundry partners? And most critically — what is the business continuity plan, in concrete operational terms, if Taiwan Strait shipping lanes are disrupted for 30, 60, or 90 days?

## 6. Investment Checklist

**1. Understandable Business: YES.** TSMC operates a pure-play semiconductor foundry model: it manufactures chips designed by other companies but does not design or sell its own branded chips. The revenue model is straightforward — wafer fabrication services priced by process node, volume, and complexity. The business is capital-intensive but conceptually simple to grasp.

**2. Durable Competitive Advantages (Widening Not Narrowing): YES.** TSMC's moat is arguably widening. It holds a commanding lead in advanced-node manufacturing (3nm, 2nm), with only Samsung as a distant second competitor at the leading edge and Intel attempting to re-enter. Each successive node increases the difficulty and cost for would-be challengers, reinforcing TSMC's position. The ecosystem effects — EDA tools, IP libraries, and design rules all optimized for TSMC first — create powerful lock-in.

**3. Long Reinvestment Runway at High Returns: YES.** The semiconductor content per device continues to grow across smartphones, data centers, automotive, IoT, and AI accelerators. The AI infrastructure buildout in particular has created a multi-year demand surge for advanced packaging (CoWoS) and leading-edge nodes. TSMC can redeploy tens of billions annually with strong visibility on demand.

**4. High Returns on Incremental Capital: YES.** TSMC has historically generated ROIC in the mid-20% to 30%+ range, well above its cost of capital. Its pricing power on leading-edge nodes, combined with high utilization rates, supports strong incremental returns even as capex has escalated beyond $30 billion annually.

**5. Strong Free Cash Flow Generation: PARTIALLY.** TSMC generates substantial operating cash flow (exceeding $30 billion annually), but aggressive capex — driven by leading-edge expansion, overseas fabs, and advanced packaging capacity — has compressed free cash flow relative to earnings. FCF conversion should improve once the current investment cycle normalizes, but near-term free cash flow yield is modest.

**6. Conservative Balance Sheet: YES.** TSMC maintains a net cash position and carries modest leverage relative to its cash-generating ability. The company funds most capex from internal cash flows. Even with the elevated investment cycle, the balance sheet remains healthy with investment-grade credit ratings.

**7. Aligned Management with Skin in the Game: PARTIALLY.** Management has demonstrated deep institutional commitment and long tenure, but direct equity ownership by senior management is relatively modest. Alignment is more cultural and reputational than driven by large personal equity stakes.

**8. Rational Capital Allocation History: YES.** TSMC has been disciplined and forward-looking. It invests heavily to maintain its technology lead, pays a steady and growing dividend, and has avoided value-destructive acquisitions. It has resisted vertical integration into chip design, preserving its trusted foundry model. The overseas fab investments are a strategic response to geopolitical risk rather than empire-building.

**9. Favourable Industry Structure: YES.** The foundry industry has consolidated with TSMC holding roughly 60% overall share and 90%+ at the leading edge. The fabless-foundry model is structurally entrenched. Customer switching costs are high due to process co-optimization and qualification cycles. The industry structure is oligopolistic at advanced nodes and favorable for the incumbent leader.

**10. Reasonable Valuation Relative to Quality and Growth: PARTIALLY.** TSMC typically trades at 20–25x forward earnings, which is not inexpensive but arguably justified for a business of this caliber. However, valuation sensitivity to the AI cycle and geopolitical risk premiums warrant careful analysis. Investors should be cautious about paying peak-cycle multiples during an AI-driven supercycle.

**11. Resilience to Recession or Disruption: PARTIALLY.** Revenue is cyclical, and a broad downturn would reduce chip demand. Leading-edge positioning provides some insulation, as AI spending may prove more durable. The more significant risk is geopolitical — concentration in Taiwan exposes it to cross-strait tensions. Natural disaster risk (earthquakes, water scarcity) also merits monitoring.

**12. Transparent Management Communication: YES.** TSMC holds quarterly earnings calls with detailed commentary on demand, capacity, and technology roadmaps. Management has been consistent and credible in its guidance. Communication has been notably straightforward about challenges, including geopolitical risks and overseas expansion costs.

### Overall Assessment

TSMC represents one of the highest-quality businesses in the global technology ecosystem, scoring affirmatively on most criteria. Its widening moat in advanced semiconductor manufacturing, disciplined capital allocation, and strong industry structure make it a compelling long-term holding. The primary caveats are temporarily compressed free cash flow due to heavy investment, moderate management equity alignment, a valuation requiring confidence in sustained AI-driven growth, and meaningful geopolitical concentration risk in Taiwan. For investors with a multi-year horizon and tolerance for geopolitical uncertainty, TSMC's fundamental quality and strategic positioning remain difficult to match in the semiconductor industry.

## 7. Synthesis

### The 3–4 Things That Matter Most

**First, TSMC is the closest thing to a natural monopoly in advanced semiconductors.** The competitive landscape analysis makes this unambiguous: 90%+ share at the leading edge, a self-reinforcing cycle of technology leadership attracting top customers whose revenue funds the next node, and ecosystem lock-in through EDA tools, IP libraries, and design rule optimization. No competitor — not Samsung, not Intel — has a credible path to parity within the next five years. This is not a typical market-share lead; it is a structural bottleneck through which virtually all of the world's most advanced computing must pass. The financial characteristics confirm the moat's value: 53–58% gross margins and 25–30% ROIC in a business that spends $30 billion-plus annually on capex. These are not normal numbers for a manufacturer. They are the economics of irreplaceability.

**Second, the geopolitical risk is not a footnote — it is the central investment question.** Every other section of this report ultimately circles back to Taiwan. The business history explains why TSMC is there. The competitive analysis explains why moving is ruinously expensive. The Red Flags section states the uncomfortable truth: the stock is priced as though a cross-strait disruption has near-zero probability, and any re-pricing of that assumption overwhelms all other factors. The overseas fabs in Arizona, Japan, and Germany are a partial hedge, but they will take years to scale, cost 50%+ more to operate, and will never replicate the Hsinchu ecosystem's depth. An investor in TSMC is making an implicit bet on geopolitical stability in the Taiwan Strait. That bet may well prove correct, but it should be sized accordingly.

**Third, the AI supercycle is real but creates its own risks.** HPC now drives 40–45% of revenue and is growing fastest. NVIDIA and the hyperscalers are spending at unprecedented rates on AI training infrastructure, and nearly all of it flows through TSMC's fabs and CoWoS packaging. This is a genuine secular shift, not hype. But the Red Flags analysis correctly notes that every prior semiconductor supercycle — 2000, 2007, 2021 — ended with an inventory correction. The question is not whether AI demand is real (it is), but whether the current rate of spending is sustainable or whether customers are over-ordering to secure capacity, a dynamic that historically precedes sharp corrections.

**Fourth, the management succession question is approaching.** C.C. Wei and Mark Liu have executed brilliantly in the post-Morris Chang era. But both are aging, and TSMC has not publicly identified successors. The company's institutional culture is strong enough that a transition should be manageable, but TSMC has never been led by anyone other than Chang or his hand-picked deputies. The next leadership generation will be the first true test of whether TSMC's excellence is cultural or personal.

### Key Tensions and Contradictions

The most striking tension is between the competitive analysis (moat widening, advantages compounding) and the Red Flags (geographic concentration, customer concentration, margin dilution from overseas expansion). Both are true simultaneously. TSMC's moat is widening *because* it is concentrated in Taiwan — the Hsinchu ecosystem's density and efficiency are precisely what make the moat so deep. Diversifying geographically is strategically necessary but competitively dilutive. Every dollar spent on an Arizona fab earning lower returns is a dollar not spent deepening the Taiwan advantage. Management must navigate this paradox for the next decade.

A second tension: the Investment Checklist gives "YES" to durable competitive advantages and rational capital allocation, but "PARTIALLY" to recession resilience. This correctly captures a company that is simultaneously best-in-class and exposed — the moat protects against competitors but not against demand shocks or geopolitical discontinuities.

### Open Questions for Further Research

1. **Arizona cost trajectory:** What is the realistic path to cost parity (if any) for the Arizona fabs? How much of the 50% cost premium is structural vs. transitional?
2. **AI spending durability:** What proportion of current HPC orders are "true" demand vs. capacity hoarding by hyperscalers? What does TSMC's order book look like 12–18 months out?
3. **Succession pipeline:** Who are the leading internal candidates to succeed Wei and Liu? What is the realistic timeline?
4. **ASML dependency:** How exposed is TSMC to a disruption in EUV tool delivery? What is the replacement timeline if ASML faces production constraints?
5. **China revenue management:** How is TSMC navigating the evolving U.S. export control regime regarding its ~10% China revenue?

### Quality Assessment

TSMC is a **generational quality business** — one of perhaps a dozen companies globally that occupy a structurally irreplaceable position in the modern economy. The competitive moat is wide and widening. The financial profile is exceptional. Management execution has been outstanding. It scores higher on fundamental business quality than almost any large-cap technology company.

But quality and investability are not the same thing. The geopolitical risk is binary and unhedgeable. The valuation assumes sustained AI-driven growth. And the current heavy-investment phase temporarily suppresses the free cash flow that would otherwise make the quality self-evident. TSMC is the rare company where the business analysis is straightforward — this is clearly an extraordinary franchise — and the investment analysis is genuinely difficult. Entry point and position sizing matter enormously.

### Priority Next Steps

1. Obtain and analyse TSMC's latest quarterly earnings (20-F and Q4 2025/Q1 2026 results) for updated margin and capex guidance
2. Deep-dive on Arizona fab economics — review subsidy disbursement status, workforce ramp, and cost structure vs. Taiwan
3. Build a scenario model stress-testing a 30% revenue decline (2008-style) against the current cost structure
4. Research cross-strait military posture indicators and insurance/hedging options for Taiwan exposure
5. Analyse CoWoS packaging capacity constraints and timeline for expansion — this is the near-term binding constraint on AI revenue

- [**TSMC Annual Technology Symposium**](https://www.tsmc.com/english/dedicatedFoundry/technology) (typically June) — Flagship event for new process nodes and roadmap updates. Detailed coverage from [SemiAnalysis](https://www.semianalysis.com/), [WikiChip](https://en.wikichip.org/wiki/tsmc), and Tom's Hardware.
- **TSMC Monthly Revenue Reports** — Unusually, TSMC discloses unaudited monthly revenue (published ~10th of each month via TWSE). Available on the [investor relations site](https://investor.tsmc.com/). Near-real-time demand signals used by analysts to model quarterly results.

### Suggested Learning Path

Start with the [Acquired podcast episode](https://www.acquired.fm/episodes/tsmc) for a comprehensive narrative, read Chris Miller's [*Chip War*](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Chip+War+Chris+Miller) for geopolitical context, watch [Asianometry's](https://www.youtube.com/@Asianometry) TSMC and EUV videos for technical depth, then follow [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/) and [SemiAnalysis](https://www.semianalysis.com/) for ongoing analysis. Monitor [quarterly earnings calls and monthly revenue reports](https://investor.tsmc.com/) for real-time tracking.
