# MercadoLibre — Seven Questions

An investment summary of MercadoLibre — the Latin American e-commerce and fintech group, US-listed — prepared on the 2nd of July 2026, through a lens of quality and long-term ownership.

## Question One: Business Description — What Is It?

MercadoLibre is Latin America's dominant commerce-and-payments ecosystem, operating across 18 countries, with Brazil, Mexico, and Argentina the economic core. Three flywheels reinforce each other. First, the marketplace — first-party and third-party retail, plus the Mercado Envios logistics network that now delivers most items within 48 hours. Second, Mercado Pago — payments, digital accounts, and an asset-management product whose users grew 29 percent year on year in the first quarter of 2026. Third, Mercado Credito — consumer, merchant, and credit-card lending built on proprietary ecosystem data. Advertising is a fourth, higher-margin leg scaling on top of marketplace traffic. In the first quarter of 2026 the ecosystem processed 19 billion US dollars of gross merchandise volume, up 42 percent year on year, and 87 billion US dollars of total payment volume, up 50 percent.

## Question Two: Management and Governance — Can We Trust Them?

This is a founder-built franchise going through a deliberate, well-telegraphed generational handover. Ariel Szarfsztejn — who joined in 2017 and built the logistics network before running the whole commerce business — became chief executive on the 1st of January 2026, with founder Marcos Galperin staying close to strategy, culture, and capital allocation as executive chairman. The succession was announced eight months in advance and promoted from within, which is what one wants to see from a quality franchise.

The capital-allocation record is the strongest governance signal: management has repeatedly traded near-term margin for durable share — cutting take rates since 2024, with unique buyers up 62 percent since; building fulfilment ahead of demand; and now front-loading credit-card issuance losses — and has been explicit that harvesting margin early would be the mistake. Governance is conventional for a US-listed company, with Delaware incorporation and Nasdaq disclosure standards; the main watch items are founder influence from the chairman seat and the rising complexity of a bank-like balance sheet inside a retailer.

## Question Three: Investment Thesis — Why Does Value Compound?

Structural runway: Latin American e-commerce penetration and financial inclusion both remain well below developed-market levels; Brazil's online retail market alone is forecast to more than double by 2031.

A compounding ecosystem moat: logistics density, payments ubiquity, and proprietary credit data reinforce one another — each new buyer, seller, and card lowers unit costs and improves underwriting in ways single-product rivals cannot match.

Share gains during the investment cycle: the first quarter of 2026 delivered the fastest revenue growth in almost four years — up 49 percent to 8.8 billion US dollars — with management citing record market share, accelerating items sold, up 56 percent in Brazil, and improving retention, while competitors spend heavily.

Fintech optionality maturing into a second core business: the credit book nearly doubled year on year to 14.6 billion US dollars, with credit cards — at 6.6 billion, up 104 percent — now 46 percent of the book. Upfront provisioning depresses today's earnings while building a long-duration, data-advantaged lender.

And proven earnings power underneath: revenue compounded from 7.1 billion US dollars in 2021 to 28.9 billion in 2025, with operating income rising from 0.4 billion to 3.2 billion — the model has already demonstrated it can expand margins when investment normalises.

## Question Four: Key Milestones and KPIs — What Proves Us Right, and What to Track?

Market share versus the Asian entrants — MercadoLibre holds about 35 percent of Brazilian e-commerce against Amazon at about 16 percent and Shopee at about 10 percent; items-sold growth and retention are the cleanest reads on whether share gains continue.

Credit quality through the cycle — non-performing loan ratios, provisioning coverage, and net interest margin after losses as the card book seasons; management notes Brazil consumer lending remains double-digit-margin profitable.

Margin trajectory — operating margin was 6.9 percent in the first quarter of 2026, versus about 12 to 13 percent in 2023 and 2024; the thesis needs visible margin recovery as take-rate cuts and card issuance costs annualise.

Fintech engagement — Mercado Pago monthly active users, up 29 percent, and assets under management, up 77 percent, as leading indicators of the second flywheel.

And a consensus check, from FactSet — the Street models revenue of about 40.7 billion US dollars in estimated financial year 2026, rising to about 63.7 billion by financial year 2028, with earnings per share roughly flat this year at about 39 dollars before doubling to about 79 dollars by 2028; rating consensus is a buy — 22 of 26 positive, no sells.

On the financials, in US dollars: revenue rose from 10.5 billion in 2022, to 14.5 billion in 2023, to 20.8 billion in 2024, to 28.9 billion in 2025, and was 8.8 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone. Operating income went from 1.0 billion, to 1.8, to 2.6, to 3.2 billion, with 0.6 billion in the first quarter. Operating margin ran 9.8 percent, 12.6 percent, 12.7 percent, then 11.1 percent, and 6.9 percent in the latest quarter. Net income climbed from 0.5 billion to 2.0 billion across those years, and diluted earnings per share from 9.53 dollars in 2022 to 39.40 dollars in 2025.

## Question Five: Five-Year Fair Value and Return — the Team's View

This is a framework, not false precision. At about 1,772 US dollars the stock trades at about 45 times estimated financial-year-2026 earnings, but that multiple is inflated by a deliberately depressed earnings year: on consensus it falls to about 31 times 2027 earnings and about 22 times 2028. The valuation question is really whether the investment cycle converts into the earnings recovery consensus already sketches.

Illustrative scenarios, as placeholders for the team's house numbers. The bull case: share gains hold, the card book seasons profitably, and margins rebuild toward the 2024 level; earnings per share compound about 30 percent or more and the multiple holds — a high-teens-to-20-percent internal rate of return. The base case: consensus is broadly right, earnings per share about 79 dollars by 2028 and growing beyond, with some multiple compression to the mid-20s — a low-to-mid-teens return. The bear case: a Latin American credit downturn hits an unsecured book of more than 15 billion US dollars while Asian competitors force take rates lower; earnings per share stagnate near 40 dollars and the multiple de-rates to about 20 times — a materially negative outcome. The credit book makes the tails fatter in both directions than for a pure retailer.

## Question Six: Variant Perception — Where the Market Is Wrong

After a roughly 35 percent twelve-month drawdown, the market reads the margin compression as competitive erosion — evidence that Shopee, Temu, Shein, and Amazon are finally taxing the franchise, and that the earnings-downgrade cycle, with 16 of 18 financial-year-2026 estimates revised down, marks a broken compounding story.

The variant view is that causality runs the other way: the profit-and-loss pressure is chosen, not imposed. Take-rate cuts, logistics build-out, and upfront card provisioning are all discretionary investments made from a position of strength — during the most intense competition in the company's history, growth accelerated to a near-four-year high, and management reports record share and improving retention. Credit accounting compounds the optical problem: provisions are booked at origination while revenue follows over the loan's life, so the faster the card book grows, the worse current earnings look. If the book seasons in line with the profitable consumer-loan record, both earnings power and the multiple are set too low. The market is right that unsecured Latin American credit deserves a discount; it likely overestimates how much of today's margin decline is permanent.

## Question Seven: Risks, Premortem, and Sell Discipline

The premortem — if this goes wrong in five years, the likely causes are these. A credit cycle: a Brazilian or regional downturn produces losses on a rapidly-scaled, largely unsecured book of 14.6 billion US dollars whose newest vintages, the cards, are untested through stress. Competitive attrition: Asian platforms with global scale economics keep take rates and shipping subsidies structurally elevated, so the "investment phase" never ends. Macro and currency: depreciation in the Brazilian real, Argentine peso, and Mexican peso, and Argentine policy reversals, translate strong local-currency execution into weak US-dollar outcomes. Fintech disruption: Nubank, Pix-based credit, and open banking commoditise the payments and lending edge faster than the data moat compounds. And transition risk: the founder handover dilutes the long-term culture, or the chairman-and-chief-executive split blurs accountability.

Sell discipline: trim or exit on, first, sustained loss of e-commerce share in Brazil or Mexico; second, credit losses that break the fintech unit's through-cycle profitability rather than just its growth-phase optics; third, margin recovery repeatedly deferred once the card book matures; or fourth, capital allocation drifting toward empire-building outside the ecosystem. Size the position acknowledging that this is now, in part, an emerging-market consumer lender.
