# Nu Holdings — The Seven Questions

Nu is the ninth-largest position in the GEM portfolio at three-point-four percent, with a market cap of around seventy billion US dollars. Here is Nu in seven questions.

---

## Cold open

Nu just closed 2025 with one hundred and thirty-one million customers — sixty-two percent of the adult population of Brazil. Fourth-quarter net income was eight hundred and ninety-five million US dollars, up fifty percent year-on-year, at a record thirty-three percent return on equity. The efficiency ratio punched below twenty percent for the first time in the company's history, at nineteen-point-nine. Full-year revenue grew forty-five percent to sixteen-point-three billion US dollars. And in January 2026 the company received conditional regulatory approval for a United States national bank charter.

This is the quarter where "is it really a bank yet?" stopped being the right question. It is a bank. A profitable one. The right question is what *this specific* bank compounds into.

Seven questions.

---

## Question One: What is it?

Nu is the largest digital bank in Latin America by customer count and, as of 2025, by profitability. Three markets, one model.

Brazil has one hundred and thirteen million customers, an eighty-six percent activity rate, and is now the largest private financial institution in the country by customer count, serving sixty-two percent of the adult population. Mexico has fourteen million customers, about fifteen percent of the adult population, with the banking licence advancing and Nu now the leading issuer of new credit cards there. Colombia has surpassed four million customers, with credit card approval rates roughly tripling through 2025.

The product stack is full-service: credit card, digital account, personal and secured loans, investments, insurance, small-business banking, payroll loans. On top sits a digital ecosystem — NuCel for mobile, NuPay for checkout, NuTravel — with over twelve million unique active users. Deposits reached forty-two billion US dollars in the fourth quarter, up twenty-nine percent. The total credit portfolio reached thirty-three billion US dollars, up forty percent, and secured loans within that grew seventy-one percent.

The thing to internalise: Nu is not a fintech app with a bank licence bolted on. It is an infrastructure-scale retail bank built from scratch in twelve years, running at roughly thirty-five percent adjusted return on equity with a cost-to-income ratio below twenty percent. No incumbent emerging-market bank has that combination.

## Question Two: Can we trust them?

Yes — and the fourth-quarter print is the best evidence yet that the founder-led culture and the capital discipline are both intact.

David Vélez — Colombian, Stanford, ex-Sequoia — founded Nu in 2013, still runs it, and still owns about twenty percent of the economics and seventy-five percent of the votes through the dual-class structure. Youssef Lahrech, formerly of Capital One, is President and Chief Operating Officer. Guilherme Lago is Chief Financial Officer.

The past-stress case that matters is 2022 to 2023. Brazilian rates went to thirteen-point-seven-five percent. Consumer credit in Brazil deteriorated sharply across the sector. Nu's loss line blew out on cue — non-performing loans climbed, the market panicked, the stock halved at one point. Management did not flinch. They tightened underwriting, slowed card issuance in high-risk cohorts, kept investing in Mexico, and held the long-term investment cadence. By late 2024 the problem cohorts had rolled off cleanly. By the fourth quarter of 2025 the fifteen-to-ninety-day non-performing loan ratio was four-point-one percent and the ninety-plus-day ratio was six-point-six percent, both below the stress peak, while the credit book grew forty percent. That is the proof: they underwrote through a cycle without breaking the model.

The capital discipline signals from the latest print are clear. Return on equity reached thirty-three percent for the quarter, with full-year net income stepping up from two billion US dollars in 2024 to two-point-nine billion in 2025. The efficiency ratio fell below twenty percent for the first time in the company's history, despite ongoing investment in the United States charter and Mexico expansion. Management explicitly framed 2026 as "an investment and inflection year" — telling the Street in advance that the efficiency ratio will face near-term upward pressure from return-to-office costs, artificial intelligence investment, and globalisation. They also introduced a new Managerial P&L framework in the fourth quarter for enhanced transparency, fully reconciled to international accounting standards — operators volunteering clearer disclosure, not less. Total capital sits at nearly nine billion US dollars, with more than two billion in excess operating-entity capital and another three billion at the holding company. The balance sheet is comfortably over-capitalised for growth.

The watch item is succession. Dual-class voting means Vélez controls the company regardless of economic stake. Depth of leadership beyond Vélez, Lahrech and Lago has not been publicly tested. Worth tracking as the bench builds out for a United States market entry.

## Question Three: Why does value compound?

Three engines, plus a kicker the Street has not fully priced.

The first engine is average revenue per active customer compounding on a near-fixed cost base. Monthly revenue per active customer — which Nu calls ARPAC — hit fifteen US dollars in the fourth quarter of 2025, up twenty-seven percent year-on-year. The cost to serve is about one US dollar per month and barely moves as products stack. Every incremental product cross-sold onto a customer — personal loan, secured loan, payroll, insurance, investments — falls almost entirely to gross profit. This is the classic super-app margin structure, except delivered through a banking profit-and-loss where regulators count the capital, not through an ad-funded platform. Risk-adjusted net interest margin held at ten-and-a-half to eleven percent throughout 2025.

The second engine is the Mexico and Colombia runway. Brazil is now at sixty-two percent of the adult population — the top-of-funnel growth in Brazil is largely done, with expansion now moving into higher-income cohorts: the super-core segment grew a hundred percent in 2025 and the high-income segment grew forty percent. The unit economics are proven; the runway is *outside Brazil*. Mexico at fifteen percent penetration has another four-to-six times of headroom. Colombia is still in land-grab mode. Both are following the Brazilian playbook with Brazilian unit economics as the blueprint.

The third engine is the secured lending mix shift. Secured loans grew seventy-one percent in 2025 — payroll, government worker-severance-backed credit, home equity. This structurally *lowers* the risk profile of the book while keeping net interest margin attractive. The regulatory headwind from rule changes to the Brazilian worker-severance fund cost fifty to sixty percent of secured originations in the fourth quarter, which is the main reason to watch the first-quarter 2026 print carefully, but the underlying mix shift is pointed in the right direction.

The kicker nobody is fully pricing is the United States charter. Conditional regulatory approval came in January 2026. This is not Nu pivoting to US retail banking — that would be value-destructive. This is Nu targeting the fifty million Hispanic and immigrant consumers in the United States who are underserved by incumbents, and for whom Nu already has a platform built in Spanish and Portuguese. The cross-border expat flywheel — money movement between Brazil and the United States, and between Mexico and the United States — is a natural wedge. If this ramps even modestly, the addressable market expansion is enormous and the operating leverage is huge. The technology stack is already built.

## Question Four: What proves us right, and what to track?

Six specific metrics.

First, monthly revenue per active customer — fifteen US dollars today. Watch progression toward twenty to twenty-five over two to three years as cross-sell matures. Second, Mexico customer growth and monetisation — fourteen million customers but revenue per customer far below Brazil. This is the operating leverage story for 2026 and 2027. Third, the efficiency ratio trajectory — management has guided upward pressure near-term, so a sustained reading below twenty-five percent through the investment year is the real test. Fourth, secured loan portfolio growth excluding the worker-severance-fund loans affected by regulation — we want to see the mix shift continue despite the regulatory hit. Fifth, United States customer onboarding pace — first public disclosures likely in the second half of 2026. Sixth, the Brazil ninety-plus-day non-performing loan ratio — at six-point-six percent now; stability or continued improvement confirms the credit cycle is behind us.

## Question Five: What's it worth?

The honest framing. Full-year 2025 net income was two-point-nine billion US dollars. The fourth-quarter annualised run-rate is roughly three-point-six billion. Market cap at time of writing is around seventy billion, so the stock trades at a trailing multiple of roughly twenty to twenty-five times earnings, and closer to eighteen to twenty times on forward. Full-year return on equity is around twenty-eight percent, with the fourth quarter at thirty-three percent, on limited leverage by bank standards. Tangible book value is still expanding in the mid-thirty-percent range annually. There is more than two billion US dollars of excess capital in operating entities, with modest capital return optionality beginning to emerge.

The simple framing: this is a thirty-percent-plus return-on-equity bank growing revenue forty-five percent, with an efficiency ratio below twenty percent and a fresh leg of growth in the United States, trading in the high teens on forward earnings. Compared to HDFC Bank in India at roughly eighteen times and seventeen percent return on equity, or Kaspi in Kazakhstan at eleven to twelve times and forty-percent-plus return on equity, Nu sits roughly in the middle. Priced for quality, but not priced for the United States charter actually delivering.

The key sensitivity is the Brazilian consumer credit cycle. Base case assumes no major deterioration. If Brazil enters a sharper slowdown and non-performing loans spike back toward the 2022-to-2023 peak, both earnings and multiple compress together.

## Question Six: Where is the market wrong?

Two variant perception angles.

First: Nu is no longer a high-growth fintech. It is a scaled, profitable universal bank, and the market is still paying for story. Twenty-five times earnings on a business doing thirty-percent-plus return on equity, forty-five percent revenue growth, and an efficiency ratio below twenty percent is not obviously expensive. The framing has shifted more slowly than the fundamentals. The sell-side comparable set is still "Brazilian fintechs" when the right comparison is large retail banks — JP Morgan, HDFC, ICICI — adjusted for growth. On that frame, Nu is trading at a discount to the quality of the franchise.

Second: the United States charter is optionality that the market rounds to zero. Consensus treats the United States expansion as a distraction. But the addressable market is fifty million underserved Hispanic consumers and Nu has the only Spanish-and-Portuguese-native, mobile-first, low-cost-to-serve stack in the world. Even five million United States customers at a modest Mexican-style revenue-per-customer would be meaningfully accretive on a relatively tiny incremental cost base.

The honest counter: emerging-markets banks that try to expand into the United States have a graveyard-sized history. Santander, BBVA, Scotiabank all overpaid or mis-sold United States expansion. The regulatory charter process is not a small thing and ramp risk is real. Position sizing should reflect that the kicker is asymmetric, not certain.

## Question Seven: What could kill it, and when do we sell?

The real risks, ranked.

One: the Brazilian consumer credit cycle. If unemployment rises and wage growth slows, the credit-card and secured-loan cohorts that were underwritten in 2024 and 2025 deteriorate. This is the single biggest earnings risk. Two: Brazilian regulatory shock. The worker-severance-fund rule change already cost fifty to sixty percent of secured loan originations in the fourth quarter. A cap on interchange or on revolving credit rates would hit Nu's margin structure directly. Three: Mexico execution — banking licence approval, agent-network build-out, credit-cycle discipline in a market less familiar than Brazil. Four: the United States charter becomes a capital sink if the regulatory ramp takes longer, if customer acquisition costs exceed Brazilian unit economics, or if management attention is diluted. Five: founder-dependent culture — Vélez, Lahrech, and Lago are the spine. If two of the three leave inside twelve months, the strategic coherence bears genuine risk. Six: digital-banking competitive convergence. Itaú, Bradesco and Santander are investing aggressively in digital. Competitive intensity in Brazilian primary-banking acquisition is real.

Specific sell triggers. Two consecutive quarters of the ninety-plus-day non-performing loan ratio rising while credit portfolio growth decelerates — that is a structural credit problem, not seasonality. Revenue per active customer stalling or declining year-on-year for two consecutive quarters — cross-sell flywheel breaking. Efficiency ratio breaking above thirty percent for more than one quarter without a clear, bounded investment explanation. United States charter ramp producing material losses or writedowns within twenty-four months of launch. Mexico monthly active customer growth dropping below twenty percent year-on-year. And any of Vélez, Lahrech, or Lago leaving without a named, credible successor in place.

## Sign-off

One company, seven questions, twelve minutes. Nu is the emerging-markets bank that the textbooks said couldn't be built: scaled, digital-native, profitable, founder-led, with genuinely new product launches continuing at pace. The 2026 investment year tests whether the United States and Mexico legs take hold. The next print — first-quarter 2026 results on the fourteenth of May — will show how much the worker-severance-fund regulatory hit and the seasonal first-quarter non-performing loan uptick matter against the structural story. Watch revenue per customer, Mexico growth, and the first-quarter non-performing loan trajectory, in that order.
