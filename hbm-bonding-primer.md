# HBM Bonding: Technologies, Timelines, and the Hynix–Samsung Battleground

A working primer on HBM memory bonding — covering MR-MUF, TC-NCF, and hybrid bonding, with market context for SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics.

## The three bonding technologies, in plain terms

HBM is made by stacking twelve to sixteen thinned DRAM dies on top of a logic base die and wiring them vertically with through-silicon vias. The question is how you physically join and stabilise each layer. There are three answers, in rough order of sophistication.

TC-NCF — Thermal Compression bonding with Non-Conductive Film — is the incumbent method used by Samsung and Micron. A film-type adhesive material is applied after each chip is stacked, then heat and pressure bond the layers one at a time. It works, but it is slow, and the film creates alignment and flow-consistency problems that get worse as stacks get taller.

MR-MUF — Mass Reflow Molded Underfill — is SK Hynix's proprietary process and the single biggest reason for its HBM dominance. Instead of bonding chip-by-chip with film, MR-MUF heats and interconnects all the vertically stacked chips at once, then floods the gaps with a liquid epoxy molding compound. This is faster, gives better thermal dissipation, and reduces warpage. Crucially, it is hard to copy: Hynix has an exclusive epoxy compound supplied under long-term contract with Japan's Namics, which enables uniform gap-filling and encapsulation — a key reason Samsung and Micron have been unable to replicate the technology, on top of the patent barrier. Both TC-NCF and MR-MUF still connect layers via micro-bumps — tiny solder balls between the dies.

Hybrid bonding — sometimes called copper-to-copper bonding — is the next-generation approach. It eliminates the micro-bumps entirely and fuses copper pads on one die directly to copper pads on the next. By minimising the spacing between chips it dramatically reduces stack thickness, and significantly improves data transfer speed and heat dissipation. The catch is manufacturing difficulty: it requires atomically flat surfaces, achieved through chemical-mechanical polishing to extreme tolerances, and yields are currently poor.

## Why bonding is becoming the binding constraint

The forcing function is JEDEC's package height limit. HBM4 packages must fit within a strict 775-micron height limit, so as you add layers, each die must get thinner. Sixteen-layer stacks require wafers thinned to around 30 microns, versus roughly 50 microns for twelve-layer designs — and with that height ceiling, conventional bump-based methods leave little room for further scaling. Thermocompression-based methods can stretch to sixteen layers thanks to relaxed JEDEC standards, but as Nvidia and other customers keep raising requirements, hybrid bonding adoption is considered inevitable in the long run. Beyond sixteen layers — HBM5 is expected at twenty-plus — bumps essentially run out of road. An important later refinement of this claim appears in the section on the JEDEC height limit.

## Timelines — the state of play

Right now, in 2026, HBM3E still dominates — roughly 66 percent of total HBM output in 2026, down from 87 percent in 2025. HBM4 mass production began around February 2026, timed to Nvidia's Rubin platform launching in the second half of 2026. Notably, Samsung shipped the world's first mass-production twelve-layer HBM4 in February — a genuine reversal of the HBM3E cycle, where Hynix supplied over 70 percent of the HBM3E going into Blackwell. Samsung's HBM4 uses its most advanced 1c DRAM process with a 4-nanometre base die from its own foundry; Hynix outsources its HBM4 base die to TSMC, at 12 nanometres, under their "One-Team" alliance, and runs Advanced MR-MUF to minimise production risk.

In the second half of 2026 comes sixteen-layer HBM4. This is where bonding strategies first visibly diverge. Nvidia has asked suppliers whether they could deliver sixteen-layer HBM as early as the fourth quarter of 2026. Hynix is targeting mass production of its sixteen-layer HBM4 by the third quarter of 2026 — using MR-MUF, with dies thinned to 30 microns. Samsung, by contrast, is going straight at hybrid bonding: it is reportedly already supplying hybrid-bonded HBM4 prototypes to key customers including Nvidia — but yields remain around 10 percent, and it is building a dedicated HBM4 hybrid bonding line at its Cheonan campus, with plans to extend to HBM4E.

In 2027 and 2028 comes HBM4E. Samsung has indicated it will introduce hybrid bonding starting with its seventh-generation sixteen-layer HBM4E, roughly targeting sixteen-layer HBM4E products by 2028. HBM4E pairs with Rubin Ultra in 2027.

In 2029 and 2030 comes HBM5 — the real inflection. The consensus view is that HBM5 is the practical inflection point for widespread hybrid bonding adoption — achieving ultra-high stacks beyond twenty layers at finer pitches requires it. Counterpoint expects Hynix to launch HBM5 around 2029 to 2030, in line with the next AI GPU cycle, with hybrid bonding entering full-scale mass production from that point. That is consistent with Nvidia's roadmap, where HBM5 adoption comes only in late 2028 at the earliest, paired with the GPU generation after Rubin Ultra.

The strategic asymmetry is nicely summarised by one Korean industry veteran: Samsung is focusing on hybrid bonding early because it struggled to match rivals in current adhesive-based technologies, while SK Hynix is managing its pace — developing hybrid bonding as a backup while aiming to maximise its industry-leading MR-MUF technology for as long as possible. That said, Hynix is not asleep: it has reportedly completed validation of twelve-high hybrid-bonded HBM and is working to raise yields, and it is adopting an integrated hybrid bonding solution from Applied Materials and BESI that combines polishing, surface treatment, bonding, and die placement into a single flow — plus commissioning development from Hanmi Semiconductor and Hanwha.

## Market shares

Hynix holds roughly 53 to 60 percent of HBM by most estimates, with Samsung and Micron splitting the rest — Micron at an estimated 10 to 21 percent, Samsung the balance. Samsung and Hynix together control about 90 percent of global HBM production. Later sourcing in this document puts Hynix's share of the high-end, Nvidia-relevant segment at over 70 percent, with HBM now more than 40 percent of Hynix's total DRAM revenue; the spread reflects different measures — units versus revenue versus HBM3E-only. The market itself is forecast to grow 58 percent to 54.6 billion dollars in 2026, and Hynix has guided to a 50 percent industry compound annual growth rate from 2024 to 2028. Both Hynix and Micron say their 2026 HBM supply is fully sold out.

## Framing it for the portfolio

The investment question is not "which bonding technology wins" — everyone agrees hybrid bonding wins eventually. It is whether the transition point is where the moat resets. Hynix's dominance is built on MR-MUF, plus the Namics exclusivity, plus the Nvidia relationship — an advantage specific to the bump-based era. When the industry moves to copper-to-copper bonding, that specific moat expires and everyone starts from something closer to scratch. Samsung's bet is that by suffering through 10 percent yields now, it accumulates hybrid bonding learning curves years before it is mandatory — and it has vertical integration, with SEMES equipment in-house and its own foundry for base dies, that Hynix lacks. Hynix's counter is classic incumbent logic: milk the proven process, keep yields and Nvidia qualification pristine, and buy the best external hybrid bonding toolchain from Applied Materials and BESI so it is ready when economics actually favour switching.

The key dates to remember: the third quarter of 2026, Hynix's sixteen-high HBM4 mass-production target, and MR-MUF's high-water mark; the fourth quarter of 2026, Nvidia's exploratory sixteen-layer delivery window; around 2028, Samsung's sixteen-high HBM4E on hybrid bonding — the first volume test of whether its early bet pays; and 2029 to 2030, HBM5 at twenty-plus layers — the point long framed as the competitive reset. Watch Samsung's hybrid bonding yield trajectory as the single most informative datapoint: if it climbs from around 10 percent toward economic viability faster than expected, the 2028 crossover becomes a real threat to Hynix's share rather than a footnote.

A caveat: this space moves fast, and several of these dates — especially Samsung's HBM4E timing and Nvidia's sixteen-layer pull-in — come from Korean trade press with an eighteen-month history of slippage in both directions. Worth flagging as quarterly-refresh items.

## How the stack got taller: the HBM lineage

Is twelve to sixteen dies in a pile the way it has always been? No — the stack started much smaller. The twelve-to-sixteen figure is where we are now; HBM began with just four dies in the pile. Here is the full lineage.

The first generation, plain HBM, arrived in volume in 2015 — four-high stacks, one gigabyte per stack, one gigabit per second per pin, first used in AMD's Fury X.

HBM2 followed in 2016 and 2017 — four-high and eight-high stacks, four to eight gigabytes, around two to two-point-four gigabits per second per pin, in Nvidia's P100 and V100 and AMD's Vega.

HBM2E, in 2019 and 2020, moved to eight-high and then twelve-high stacks, sixteen to twenty-four gigabytes, three-point-two to three-point-six gigabits per second, in Nvidia's A100.

HBM3, in 2022, ran eight-high and twelve-high, sixteen to twenty-four gigabytes, six-point-four gigabits per second, in Nvidia's H100.

HBM3E, in 2024 and 2025, ran eight-high and twelve-high, with sixteen-high demoed, twenty-four to thirty-six gigabytes, eight to nine-point-six gigabits per second, in Nvidia's H200 and Blackwell.

HBM4, in 2026, has twelve-high ramping and sixteen-high in the second half, thirty-six to forty-eight gigabytes, ten to eleven-point-seven gigabits per second, for Nvidia's Rubin.

HBM4E, expected around 2027 and 2028, moves to sixteen-high, forty-eight to sixty-four gigabytes, for Rubin Ultra.

And HBM5, expected around 2029 and 2030, is projected at twenty-plus-high, for the post-Rubin GPU generation.

"High" is the terminology. An "eight-high stack" means eight DRAM dies stacked on the base die. When you read "twelve-layer" or "twelve-high HBM4," those are the same thing. The generation name — HBM3E and so on — describes the interface standard: speed, channel architecture, protocol — while the high count describes the physical stack, and each generation typically ships in more than one height. So twelve-high HBM3E and eight-high HBM3E coexist in the market at different price points and capacities.

The "E" means "Extended." HBM2E and HBM3E were not new JEDEC standards from scratch — they were mid-cycle speed-and-density stretches of the parent generation, driven by customers, mostly Nvidia, wanting more before the next full standard was ready. HBM4E will be the same relative to HBM4.

The stack got taller for one reason: capacity per GPU. Bandwidth improved mainly through faster pins and wider interfaces — HBM4's big move was doubling the interface from 1,024 to 2,048 bits. But capacity — how many billions of parameters fit next to the GPU — only grows by making each die denser or piling more dies up. Die density improves slowly, since it is tied to the DRAM process node, so stacking became the pressure valve. That is why the count marched four, eight, twelve, sixteen, twenty-plus.

And that is precisely why bonding became the story. At four-high and eight-high, nobody cared much about bonding — TC-NCF was fine, there was height budget to spare. But each added layer forces every die to be thinned further — about 50 microns at twelve-high, about 30 microns at sixteen-high — and thinner dies warp more, dissipate heat worse, and are harder to bond without defects. MR-MUF is essentially the technology that made twelve-high economically yieldable, which is how Hynix won the HBM3 and HBM3E cycle. Each jump in stack height has changed who leads: eight-to-twelve was where MR-MUF beat TC-NCF and handed Hynix the crown; sixteen-to-twenty is where MR-MUF itself is expected to run out of road and the game resets around hybrid bonding. Stack height is the clock; bonding is the mechanism.

The timelines, market shares, and bonding roadmap above were drawn from live web research at time of writing, with sources dated December 2025 through May 2026: TrendForce, the Korea Herald, Counterpoint's April 2026 report, and others. But "current" is not the same as "definitive": these are roadmap targets from trade press and research houses, and this industry slips — and occasionally pulls forward — constantly. For fund-facing work, web reporting should be treated as the sketch and primary sources as the audit: earnings-call transcripts for what Hynix and Samsung management have actually committed to on HBM4 ramp timing and hybrid bonding capex, expert networks for yield realities, and consensus estimates for HBM revenue mix.

## The JEDEC height limit, unpacked

What does it mean that "the forcing function is JEDEC's package height limit"? JEDEC is the standards body for the memory industry — a consortium where the manufacturers, Hynix, Samsung, and Micron, and their customers, Nvidia, AMD, and others, agree common specifications, so that an HBM stack from any supplier will physically fit and electrically work in any compliant GPU package. Think of it as the body that decides the dimensions of the plug and socket, so everyone's kit interoperates.

One of the things JEDEC specifies is the maximum height of the finished HBM package: originally 720 microns, relaxed to 775 microns for HBM4. That ceiling exists because the HBM stacks sit right next to the GPU die on the same silicon interposer, and the whole assembly has to be flat enough for the heat spreader and cooling solution to make even contact across everything. If one memory stack stood taller than the GPU, you would get gaps, poor thermal contact, and mechanical stress.

"Forcing function" just means the constraint that compels everything else to change. The ceiling is fixed, but the industry wants to fit ever more dies inside it: twelve, then sixteen, then twenty-plus. The only way to fit more floors into a building of fixed height is to make each floor thinner — hence about 50 microns per die at twelve layers, about 30 microns at sixteen. For scale, a human hair is roughly 70 microns, so each memory chip is already less than half a hair thick. Thinner dies warp, crack, and trap heat. The solder micro-bumps between the dies also eat into the height budget — every bump layer is vertical space not spent on silicon. Hybrid bonding removes the bumps entirely — copper fused directly to copper, zero gap.

Now for the verification, and an important update. Checked against sources: JEDEC initially specified 720 microns, achievable for eight-high and twelve-high HBM3 and HBM3E with both MR-MUF and TC-NCF; as the industry moved toward sixteen-high HBM4, hybrid bonding looked like an inevitable requirement to stay within 720 microns — but after some lobbying, JEDEC relaxed the height to 775 microns for HBM4, for both twelve-layer and sixteen-layer stacks, formalised in the HBM4 standard released April 2025. One detail on why 775 microns specifically: it is the standard thickness of a silicon wafer, and HBM must sit at the same height as the logic die it is co-packaged with — going taller would require thicker logic wafers that current equipment is not designed to handle.

And the ceiling is apparently moving again. JEDEC is reportedly planning to relax the height spec further, to around 900 microns, taking effect from seventh-generation HBM4E, as reported in April 2026. This cuts directly against the framing that hybrid bonding becomes "mandatory" at HBM5: if the ceiling rises to 900 microns, the arithmetic gets a reprieve — easing the standard reduces the need for ultra-thin wafers, simplifies stacking, and could delay hybrid bonding adoption, with existing thermocompression bonders potentially supporting higher layer counts.

This matters in three ways. First, it is a pattern, not a one-off — this is the second time JEDEC has relaxed the ceiling rather than force a technology transition, which tells you the standards body, where the memory makers sit on the committee, will bend the spec before it breaks the incumbents' processes. Second, it extends the runway of Hynix's MR-MUF moat — the competitive reset placed at 2029 to 2030 could arrive later or more gradually than the hybrid bonding bulls expect. Third, it makes Samsung's early hybrid bonding bet look more expensive: if bump-based bonding remains viable deeper into HBM4E and HBM5, Samsung is paying the 10-percent-yield learning-curve tax earlier than it strictly needed to. There is also a supply-chain read: it favours the thermocompression bonder makers, like Hanmi, over the hybrid bonder ecosystem, for a while longer.

The counterweight, per SemiAnalysis: HBM does not actually need the interconnect density hybrid bonding offers, and its yield and cost challenges may not be worth it. Hybrid bonding for HBM has always been a height solution more than a performance necessity — which is exactly why a taller ceiling defers it. The corrected formulation: hybrid bonding's arrival keeps getting negotiated backwards, and the 900-micron proposal is the latest deferral. A live variable, not a fixed date.

## Die, stack, package — what "package height" means

"Package" is chip-industry jargon for the finished, sealed unit — the complete HBM product after all manufacturing is done. There are three levels of physical object. The die is a single bare chip of silicon — one DRAM layer, or the logic chip at the bottom; fragile, microscopically thin, never used naked. The stack is the pile: twelve or sixteen DRAM dies sitting on the logic base die, wired together vertically. The package is that stack plus everything wrapped around it to make it a usable component — the protective epoxy molding compound filling the gaps and encasing the sides, the bonding material between layers, and the connection points underneath that let it be mounted onto the interposer next to the GPU. It is the difference between a bare stack of paper and a bound book with covers.

"Package height" is simply how tall that finished unit is — 775 microns for HBM4, about three-quarters of a millimetre. Everything has to fit inside: sixteen dies, fifteen bonding layers between them, the thicker base die, the molding compound, plus the top die, which is deliberately left thicker than the others so it can survive being handled by machines in later assembly steps.

The reason the package is what gets specified — rather than the dies or the stack — is that the package is the thing the customer touches. Nvidia does not care how Hynix divides up the internal budget between silicon and glue; it cares that the finished brick is exactly flat with the GPU die beside it, so one cooling plate can press down evenly across the whole assembly. JEDEC standardises the external dimensions precisely so that a Samsung package, a Hynix package, and a Micron package are physically interchangeable on the same board — that is what makes it a standard rather than a proprietary part. The height is fixed from the outside in: if the customer-facing brick cannot grow and you want more floors inside it, every internal component has to shrink to compensate.

## The physical scale of an AI GPU: tile, not tower

Here is a common misconception worth correcting. If you picture a pile of chips next to a GPU that is large multiples bigger — a tower — delete that mental image. First, a clarification: HBM is stacked DRAM, not NAND. NAND is the storage-grade memory in SSDs; it also gets stacked, but that is a different product entirely.

An HBM stack is not a skyscraper next to the GPU — it is a tile. Here is the precise scale ladder, from smallest to largest.

One DRAM die in a sixteen-high stack is about 30 microns thick — less than half the width of a human hair, about a third of a sheet of paper. Its footprint, though, is roughly 10 by 11 millimetres — a fingernail. Each chip in the pile is proportioned like a sheet of A4 paper: vast in area relative to its thickness.

The full sixteen-die HBM package is about 775 microns tall — that is, zero-point-seven-seven-five of a millimetre. A standard credit card is zero-point-seven-six millimetres thick. So the entire sixteen-storey HBM4 stack — dies, bonds, base die, molding — is the thickness of a credit card. Sixteen storeys, but each storey is cling-film thin. The package is about thirteen times wider than it is tall. A flat tile, not a tower.

The GPU die itself is also about 775 microns thick — deliberately ground to exactly the same height as the HBM packages, so a flat cold plate can press evenly on all of them. Its footprint is much bigger: Nvidia's big AI dies are over 800 square millimetres, around 26 by 33 millimetres — a large postage stamp. Blackwell fuses two of those side by side.

The silicon interposer — the wiring board that the GPU die and its six to eight HBM tiles all sit on — is a thin sheet of silicon around 100 microns thick, one sheet of paper, and roughly credit-card area for the current generation. The package substrate underneath, the board with the contact pads, adds a couple of millimetres, and a metal lid on top brings the whole finished GPU package to maybe three to four millimetres thick and roughly palm-sized — call it eight to ten centimetres on a side for the biggest current parts.

Everything above that is cooling. The chunky object in photos, several centimetres tall, is almost entirely heatsink, vapour chamber, or cold plate. The silicon underneath is a wafer-thin sandwich. By height, a GPU module is maybe 5 percent silicon and 95 percent thermal management. The "large multiples bigger" visual was real — it was the radiator, not the engine.

The tie back to bonding: the whole battleground — MR-MUF versus TC-NCF versus hybrid bonding, JEDEC's 775-micron ceiling, dies thinned to 30 microns — is a fight over the internal budget of something the thickness of a credit card. The reason the engineering is so hard, and the moats so defensible, is precisely that there is no room. Nobody is building towers; everyone is trying to fit one more floor into a building that is legally forbidden from getting taller. When JEDEC debates 775 versus 900 microns, it is arguing over a sixth of a millimetre — and billions of dollars of capex allocation swing on it.

## Chemical-mechanical polishing: who performs it, who sells it, and the yield gap

Who does the polishing, and where are yields right now? First, who performs it: the memory makers themselves, in-house. Chemical-mechanical polishing — CMP — is a front-end, fab-grade process. The hybrid bonding flow involves many tools traditionally used exclusively by fabs; outsourced assembly and test firms, like ASE and Amkor, have relatively little experience with the deposition, etch, plating, polishing, and surface-activation steps involved — and the cleanliness requirements plus extra tooling drive a huge cost increase. So hybrid bonding pulls HBM packaging further into the memory makers' own fabs and away from any outsourcing option: Hynix polishes Hynix's wafers, Samsung polishes Samsung's. The precision required is why: the surface roughness threshold is around half a nanometre for the dielectric and one nanometre for the copper pads — literally counting atoms.

Who sells the tools: Applied Materials, overwhelmingly. Applied holds roughly 70 percent of the global polishing market through its Reflexion platform family and dominates leading-edge polishing at every major foundry and memory customer; Ebara of Japan holds about 25 percent, with particular strength at Japanese and Korean memory customers. In hybrid-bonding polishing specifically the concentration is even more extreme: one Korean industry analysis puts it bluntly — in hybrid-bonding polishing, Applied Materials is, quite literally, the god; alternatives exist in Ebara and Korea's KC Tech, but neither has any track record of supplying polishing for hybrid bonding. Lam Research is positioning its SABRE 3D platform for hybrid bonding surface prep — a challenger to watch.

The strategic masterstroke is that Applied Materials has bundled the polishing step with the bonder itself. Applied acquired an around-9-percent stake in BESI, becoming its largest shareholder, and together they built the Kinex system — the industry's first integrated die-to-wafer hybrid bonder, combining all critical process steps, polishing, plasma treatment, bonding, and die placement, in one machine. The integrated solution is already running in mass production on TSMC's line for AMD's 3D V-Cache. The analyst comparison: the two companies' equipment could be supplied in tandem, much as Lasertec's mask inspection tools travel alongside ASML's EUV systems.

The proof point landed in March 2026: SK Hynix placed its first order for a mass-production hybrid bonding inline system — Applied's polishing and plasma equipment integrated with BESI's die bonder — at around 20 billion Korean won, roughly 14 million dollars, per tool, for installation on a development line. Hynix will also bring in a Hanwha Semitech tool for quality testing; Samsung has BESI equipment for development and has begun testing a hybrid bonder from its in-house affiliate SEMES — though the SEMES tool is understood to be less mature. Note the asymmetry: Samsung is trying to build a captive equipment path via SEMES; Hynix is simply buying the best external kit.

Now the yields: roughly 10 percent, needing 60 percent. Industry consensus is that HBM hybrid bonding yield currently hovers around 10 percent, and commercialisation requires crossing at least the 60 percent threshold. The reason it is so brutal is arithmetic, not any single defect mode: the technique is proven in image sensors and TSMC's 3D V-Cache — but those bond two layers; HBM is a sixteen-to-twenty-layer stack, and a failure at any one layer scraps the entire chip. If each individual bond yields 95 percent, sixteen of them compound to about 44 percent; each bond needs to be well above 97 percent just to clear the commercial bar. That compounding is the whole game — it is why per-bond perfection matters so much more here than in any prior hybrid bonding application, and why the timeline keeps sliding right.

One reconciliation note: these sources put Hynix's HBM share at over 70 percent, with HBM now more than 40 percent of its total DRAM revenue — higher than the 50 to 60 percent quoted earlier. The spread reflects different measures, but the over-70-percent figure for the high-end segment is probably the right one to carry for the Nvidia-relevant market. The clean takeaways: the yield gap, 10 percent to 60 percent, is the single number that dates every hybrid bonding timeline, and Applied Materials plus BESI have engineered a Lasertec-and-ASML-style bundle around the hardest step — a second-order way to own the transition regardless of whether Hynix or Samsung wins it.

## A micron primer

What does "30 microns" actually mean? A micron — properly a micrometre — is one millionth of a metre, or one thousandth of a millimetre. So 30 microns is zero-point-zero-three millimetres — three hundredths of a millimetre. The ladder: one metre is a thousand millimetres; one millimetre is a thousand microns; one micron is a thousand nanometres — the "nanometre" in chip-process names like 4-nanometre.

Physical anchors: a human hair is about 70 microns across; a sheet of paper about 100 microns thick; a red blood cell about 8 microns; a DRAM die in a sixteen-high stack, 30 microns — less than half a hair, a third of a sheet of paper; the full HBM package, 775 microns — the credit card; JEDEC's proposed new ceiling, 900 microns, or zero-point-nine of a millimetre.

The chip world straddles two units. Packaging dimensions — die thickness, stack heights, bump pitches — live in microns. Transistor dimensions live a thousand times smaller, in nanometres: Samsung's 4-nanometre base die, TSMC's 12-nanometre. In HBM discussions, microns mean the physical construction of the stack; nanometres mean the circuitry etched onto each die. When dies get thinned from about 50 microns to about 30 microns for sixteen-layer stacks, each finished memory chip is ground down from half-a-hair thick to under-half-a-hair thick — approaching the thickness of cling film, ten to fifteen microns, while remaining a fingernail-sized sheet of brittle crystal. That is why handling, warpage, and breakage become the yield problem.

## Samsung's dual track: what "going hybrid early" actually means

What is Samsung saying no to by going hybrid early? Is it also doing TC-NCF somewhere in HBM4, or is it trying to leapfrog HBM4 and go straight to HBM4E? The answer is: neither leapfrog nor abandonment — Samsung is running both tracks simultaneously, on different products. No one is saying no to bump-based bonding today; the strategies differ in when each company plans to switch and what they are stretching in the meantime. Here is Samsung's actual production matrix.

HBM4 twelve-layer, in mass production today, uses TC-NCF. The twelve-layer HBM4 Samsung started shipping in February 2026 — the world first — is built with its bump-based process, branded Advanced TC-NCF. Samsung has been squeezing the process rather than replacing it — reducing the gap between stacked wafers to under 7 microns for HBM4 — and its executives have publicly pushed back on the MR-MUF-is-better narrative, arguing their solution suits twelve-to-sixteen-layer stacks. So the product currently winning design slots in Rubin is conventional-bonded. Its edge over Hynix in HBM4 is not packaging at all — it is the front end: the more advanced 1c DRAM process and 4-nanometre in-house base die, delivering 11.7 gigabits per second against Hynix's more conservative 1b DRAM plus TSMC 12-nanometre combination.

HBM4 sixteen-layer is the hybrid bonding insertion point. This is where Samsung's early bet actually lands. As far back as 2024, Samsung stated it had used thermocompression bonding up to twelve-stack but considered hybrid bonding necessary for sixteen-stack and above, and it verified a sixteen-layer hybrid-bonded HBM3 sample using equipment from its subsidiary SEMES. The 10-percent-yield prototypes it is sampling to Nvidia are sixteen-layer HBM4. The new Cheonan hybrid bonding line is initially focused on HBM4 mass production, expanding to HBM4E after. The plan: twelve-high on bumps, sixteen-high on copper — within the same generation.

So what is Samsung saying no to? Precisely one thing: building a rival to MR-MUF. There was an intermediate move available — develop a mass-reflow-style process to fix TC-NCF's throughput and thermal weaknesses at twelve to sixteen layers, and fight Hynix on its own ground. Samsung cannot easily take it — Hynix's patents plus the exclusive Namics epoxy block the path — and after losing the HBM3E cycle with TC-NCF it evidently concluded that perfecting the bump era is a race it structurally loses. So it skips the intermediate weapon and spends its catch-up energy on the post-bump era instead. That is the real content of "going hybrid early" — not skipping a product generation, but skipping a process generation.

The irony: the two-track structure is now symmetrical. Hynix is also two-track — MR-MUF as primary for HBM4 and HBM4E, hybrid bonding validated in parallel, with twelve-high validation complete and the Applied Materials and BESI tool ordered. Both companies hold both technologies. The difference is which track carries the flagship product and which is the hedge. For Samsung, hybrid bonding is the spearhead and TC-NCF the bridge; for Hynix, MR-MUF is the spearhead and hybrid bonding the insurance policy. Hynix's caution is also partly capital discipline: hybrid bonding equipment costs far more and consumes substantially more fab floor space, so if Advanced MR-MUF matches the performance with good yields, Hynix would rather stick with it for another generation.

## Front end versus back end

What does "front end" mean here? "Front end" and "back end" are the industry's terms for the two great halves of making a chip. Front end is making the circuitry on the wafer — the cleanrooms, the lithography machines, the hundreds of steps that etch billions of transistors onto a polished silicon wafer. It is where the nanometre vocabulary lives — "1c DRAM" or a "4-nanometre base die" are front-end claims about how small and dense the transistors are. The front end determines how fast the chip can run, how much data each die can hold, and how much power it burns.

Back end is everything after the wafer is finished. The wafer gets sliced into individual dies, and those dies are thinned, stacked, bonded, wired, encapsulated, and tested. All the packaging. MR-MUF, TC-NCF, hybrid bonding, polishing for bonding surfaces, the 775-micron ceiling, die thinning to 30 microns — all back end.

A quick decoding note: 1c and 1b are DRAM node names. DRAM processes stopped using honest nanometre numbers years ago and now use a letter sequence — 1x, 1y, 1z, 1a, 1b, 1c — each a step finer, all loosely "ten-something nanometres." So 1c is one generation more advanced than 1b: smaller cells, more capacity per die, better power. The base die is the logic chip at the bottom of the HBM stack that manages traffic — with HBM4 it moved onto proper logic processes for the first time, which is why foundry node names like 4-nanometre and 12-nanometre suddenly appear in memory discussions.

So Samsung's HBM4 lead comes from the wafer, not from the stacking. Its dies are built on a more advanced DRAM node — 1c versus Hynix's 1b — and its base die on a finer logic node — its own 4-nanometre versus the 12-nanometre TSMC makes for Hynix — which is what lets its parts run at 11.7 gigabits per second. The bonding holding the stack together is the older, supposedly inferior TC-NCF.

This reframes the competitive picture usefully. The HBM3E cycle was decided in the back end — Hynix's MR-MUF beat Samsung's TC-NCF on yield and thermals, and that packaging edge won about 70 percent share. The HBM4 cycle, at least in its opening twelve-layer phase, is being contested in the front end — Samsung took the aggressive-node risk on both the DRAM and the base die while Hynix deliberately chose mature, high-yield options. Each company is leading with its historical strength and hedging its historical weakness. Hynix's bet: packaging yield decides who ships in volume, again. Samsung's bet: speed and power efficiency decide who wins the premium Rubin slots, and its foundry gives it a front-end weapon Hynix has to rent from TSMC.

## The sixteen-layer showdown — first framing, then a correction

There is a claim worth unpacking: that the sixteen-layer showdown later this year is where both fronts collide in a single product. Let me give the initial framing, then correct it.

The initial framing. Through the twelve-layer HBM4 phase, each company is only really being tested on one of the two halves. Samsung's twelve-layer product uses aggressive front end — 1c DRAM, 4-nanometre base die — but comfortable back end, since TC-NCF handles twelve layers fine, especially under the relaxed 775-micron ceiling. Hynix's twelve-layer product is the mirror image: comfortable front end — mature 1b DRAM, proven TSMC base die — and its signature back end, MR-MUF, operating well within its known limits. The sixteen-layer product for Nvidia's Rubin, in the second half of 2026, was framed as the first product where both halves get stressed simultaneously for both companies: Samsung must prove its front end delivers at scale — 1c yields being its historical Achilles heel — and prove a back-end process currently yielding around 10 percent can reach commercial viability; Hynix must prove its conservative front end is not too slow for the premium Rubin slots and prove MR-MUF can handle sixteen dies thinned to 30 microns. One qualification window, one dominant customer, grading four things at once.

Now the correction: the logic is asymmetric, not symmetric. The neat formulation "both halves get stressed for both companies" is true for Samsung and only half-true for Hynix. Going from twelve to sixteen layers does not change Hynix's front end at all: the DRAM dies are the same 1b-node dies, just more of them, ground thinner — and thinning is a back-end operation. So the sixteen-layer test genuinely stresses only one thing at Hynix: whether MR-MUF works at 30-micron dies and sixteen layers. Its front end sails through unchanged because nothing about it changes.

The Hynix "front-end test" that the original framing gestured at is real but different in kind — a specification risk, not an execution risk: whether 1b-based HBM4 hits the speed bins Nvidia wants — Nvidia raised its HBM4 speed requirements mid-development, above the original JEDEC spec, and there was reporting through 2025 about suppliers scrambling to meet the higher target — and whether Samsung's 11.7-gigabit-per-second parts capture the premium slots. But that risk exists identically at twelve layers; it is a property of the HBM4 generation, not the sixteen-layer product.

The corrected picture is asymmetric, which is more interesting. Samsung at sixteen-high carries compound execution risk — unproven front end, its 1c yields, multiplied by unproven back end, its 10-percent-yield hybrid bonding. Two coin flips that both need to land, which is why its sixteen-high timeline is the one everyone doubts. Hynix at sixteen-high carries concentrated, single-point risk — one process, MR-MUF, pushed to the outer edge of what bump-based bonding is believed capable of. Everything else in its stack is proven. If MR-MUF clears sixteen layers, Hynix wins the round almost by default; if it strains, Hynix has no fallback at that layer count except the hybrid bonding hedge it has deliberately kept a generation behind Samsung's.

The real shape of the showdown: not both companies tested on both fronts, but one company running two experiments at once versus one company betting everything on a single experiment it is historically brilliant at. Portfolio-wise the correction matters: the probability of Hynix shipping sixteen-high on time is meaningfully higher than Samsung's — but the tail risk is worse. A Samsung failure is expected and priced; an MR-MUF failure at sixteen layers would be a genuine thesis break for the packaging moat. Either way, the second-half-of-2026 qualification window is close to a controlled experiment — same product spec, same customer, same deadline, two opposite strategies — and Nvidia's sixteen-high qualification outcomes, as they leak through supply-chain press, are the highest-value single datapoint of the second half of 2026 for the Hynix and Samsung positions.
