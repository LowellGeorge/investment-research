## Synthesis

Five things matter most about WALMEX, and they pull in different directions.

**First, the competitive moat is genuinely widening.** WALMEX outperformed ANTAD self-service same-store sales for ten consecutive quarters through Q3 2025, with the gap reaching 280 basis points — the widest since mid-2021. Price perception improved 260 bps year-over-year. The ecosystem businesses (Bait telecom at 23.5 million users, Walmart Connect ad revenue growing 27%, financial services credit disbursements up 139%) are creating new layers of competitive advantage that no domestic peer can replicate. This is not a company resting on its scale; it is actively compounding its advantages.

**Second, the profit-revenue divergence is real and demands explanation.** FY25 revenue grew 5.5% but net income fell 7.3%. Management attributes this to a higher effective tax rate, but SG&A trended adversely through the year (from 16.5% to 17.2% before moderating), and Q1 2025 operating cash flow was negative MXN 4.4 billion. The earnings call deep dive reveals that management deferred all 2026 guidance to a future investor day — a signal of genuine uncertainty about the near-term earnings trajectory. The Red Flags analysis surfaces the COFECE antitrust ruling, which structurally constrains WALMEX's supplier negotiating leverage for a decade, and could erode one of the company's most important cost advantages over time.

**Third, the leadership transition is the most significant governance risk.** Nearly every C-suite seat turned over within twelve months: CEO Caride resigned abruptly in August 2025 after just 16 months, replaced by Cristian Barrientos Pozo. A new COO (Lewellen), Sam's Club CEO (Rajashekhar), CLO, and CMO were installed simultaneously, and CFO Garcia departs by August 2026. The earnings call transcripts reveal a palpable shift in tone — from Caride's confident "doubling the business faster" rhetoric in Q2 to Barrientos' cautious "preparing for when the numbers come" posture in Q4. The influx of Walmart US executives (Lewellen openly stating "Walmart has no boundaries") raises the question of whether local entrepreneurial culture — which produced genuinely innovative businesses like Bait and Cashi — will be preserved or homogenized.

**Fourth, the primary sources revealed things web research alone would have missed.** The earnings call deep dive uncovered that Bait customers spend 2x the average ticket — the strongest evidence for the ecosystem flywheel thesis — yet management explicitly refused to set a user target ("I'm not going to put a number in the market that holds me accountable"). Walmart Connect ad revenue growth decelerated sharply from 27% at mid-year to just 5% in Q4, which management attributed to macro pressure on supplier advertising budgets — a canary for Mexican consumer spending that no sell-side note flagged prominently. The 30% SKU rationalization at small formats is aggressive enough to risk alienating value-conscious customers in a downturn. And the Q4 2024 tax benefit from revaluing fixed asset useful lives suggests the FY25 "higher tax rate" is partly normalization, not a new headwind — meaning the underlying profit trajectory is weaker than the one-off framing implies.

**Fifth, the valuation reflects a quality business at a reasonable price, but demands growth.** At ~18x trailing earnings with 17-20% ROIC, zero debt, and a 3% dividend yield, WALMEX is not expensive for its quality. But the current multiple requires sustained mid-to-high single-digit revenue growth — and FY25's 5.5% missed the 6-7% guidance. The bear case (low single-digit revenue growth + cost pressures + COFECE constraints + leadership disruption) would justify a meaningfully lower multiple.

**Key tensions and contradictions between analyses:** The Competitive Landscape agent sees widening advantages and a self-reinforcing flywheel; the Red Flags agent sees COFECE structurally eroding supplier leverage and eCommerce deceleration from platform transition risk. The Investment Checklist gives a YES on capital allocation; the Earnings Call Deep Dive reveals management deferring forward guidance and refusing to commit to specific ecosystem KPIs. The Corporate Culture agent praises the EDLP DNA that predates Walmart; the same agent warns that Bentonville-centric management appointments may dilute local innovation capacity. These are genuine tensions, not analytical errors — they reflect a business at an inflection point between its dominant past and an uncertain platform future.

**Overall quality assessment:** WALMEX is a high-quality business by almost any measure — dominant market position, strong returns on capital, conservative balance sheet, proven capital allocation discipline, and a long reinvestment runway in a structurally growing market. The ecosystem strategy (Bait, Connect, Cashi, health) is genuinely differentiated and could transform the earnings profile over the next decade. However, the simultaneous leadership overhaul, COFECE constraints, eCommerce platform transition friction, and softening Mexican consumer environment create a window of elevated execution risk.

## 1. Business History

The origins of what is now Latin America's largest retailer trace back not to Bentonville, Arkansas, but to a crowded sidewalk outside E.J. Korvette on Fifth Avenue in New York City. In the late 1950s, Jeronimo Arango — the son of a Spanish immigrant who had built a textile fortune in Mexico — watched a crowd of New Yorkers queueing to enter the pioneering discount department store. The idea struck him as directly transferable to a Mexican market still dominated by traditional, high-margin retailers. He persuaded his father to lend the equivalent of $240,000, recruited his brothers Placido and Manuel, and in 1958 opened Aurrera Bolivar, a discount store in downtown Mexico City. Immediate success drew the hostility of incumbent retailers, but by 1965 the brothers operated eight Aurrera locations generating $16 million in annual sales.

The 1960s brought Jeronimo Arango's first critical outside partnership. In 1965, Jewel Companies of Chicago organized a joint venture with the Arango brothers; by 1966, Jewel had acquired a 49% stake for roughly $20 million. Placido and Manuel Arango cashed out and pursued other ventures. Jeronimo, however, stayed and went to Jewel's Chicago headquarters to study modern retailing operations — logistics, procurement systems, category management. This period embedded an operational discipline into the company's DNA that would persist for decades: relentless cost control married to format innovation. The Jewel relationship eventually dissolved, but the American operational playbook Arango absorbed became permanent infrastructure.

Through the 1960s and 1970s, Arango built Cifra into a multi-format conglomerate that anticipated consumer segmentation well before the concept became standard emerging-market retail strategy. Superama, a compact supermarket format, launched in 1960. Vips, a family restaurant chain, followed in 1964. In 1970, Arango introduced two formats simultaneously: Bodega Aurrera, a no-frills warehouse store targeting lower-income households, and Suburbia, a family-oriented apparel department store. By the time Cifra went public on the Bolsa Mexicana de Valores in 1976, the company operated across five distinct formats spanning groceries, general merchandise, discount warehousing, restaurants, and apparel. Annual sales growth exceeded 20% through much of the 1980s, despite the devastating 1982 peso devaluation and the broader Latin American debt crisis — testament to how deeply Cifra's low-cost positioning resonated with Mexican consumers under economic stress.

The first genuinely transformational inflection point came in 1991, when Cifra and Walmart Stores, Inc. signed a joint venture agreement. The partnership created two entities: Commercializadora Mexico-Americana, to facilitate cross-border trade under the approaching NAFTA framework, and Club Aurrera, modeled on Sam's Club, which targeted small businesses and was soon rebranded as Sam's Club. The first Sam's Club in Mexico City that year was Walmart's first store anywhere outside the United States, making Mexico the testing ground for Walmart's entire international expansion thesis. The NAFTA era accelerated the logic: duty-free importation of goods from the U.S. and Canada gave the joint venture structural cost advantages that domestic competitors could not replicate.

In 1997, Walmart escalated from partner to controlling owner, acquiring a 51% stake in Cifra and effectively absorbing the company. The renaming to Wal-Mart de Mexico followed. In April 2000, Walmart raised its ownership to 60%. The transition was not merely cosmetic. Walmart imposed its Everyday Low Cost/Everyday Low Price (EDLC/EDLP) philosophy as the core operating doctrine, replacing the more ad-hoc pricing strategies Cifra had employed. The company also began rationalizing its format portfolio: in 2001, all 24 Aurrera hypermarkets were converted to Walmart Supercenters. Management, however, made the shrewd decision to preserve the Bodega Aurrera brand, recognizing its deep equity among lower-income Mexican consumers. That bet proved prescient — by November 2024, WALMEX celebrated the opening of its 2,500th Bodega Aurrera, making it the company's dominant format and the single most important driver of physical expansion. Over time, WALMEX also divested non-core legacy Cifra assets: the Vips restaurant chain was sold to Alsea in 2014, and the Suburbia department stores were sold to Liverpool in 2017 for approximately $850 million, completing a multi-decade simplification from conglomerate to focused retailer.

The second structural transformation was geographic. In 2005, Walmart entered Central America by acquiring a 33% stake in Central American Retail Holding Company (CARHCO), the former Royal Ahold operation spanning Guatemala, El Salvador, Costa Rica, Honduras, and Nicaragua. Walmart raised its CARHCO stake to 51% by 2006. Then, in December 2009, Walmart de Mexico acquired the remaining Central American operations from Walmart Stores and two minority partners for over $1.4 billion pesos, and in early 2010 renamed itself Walmart de Mexico y Centroamerica. This gave WALMEX roughly 925 stores across five Central American countries and converted the company from a single-country operator into a regional platform.

Through these structural changes, certain cultural elements remained constant. The EDLP/EDLC productivity loop — in which lower costs fund lower prices, which drive higher volumes, which further reduce unit costs — has been the company's central operating principle since the Walmart takeover in 1997 and echoes the discount DNA Jeronimo Arango absorbed from E.J. Korvette in 1958. As CEO Guilherme Loureiro stated at the 2024 Investor Day, WALMEX sells approximately 63% more per square meter than its Mexican competitors, a direct output of this loop. The company also retained a deep orientation toward underserved, lower-income consumers. Where American Walmart pursued the broad middle class, WALMEX's most successful innovations — Bodega Aurrera, Mi Bodega Aurrera (launched 2005 for towns under 50,000 people), Bodega Aurrera Express (2008, for dense urban neighborhoods) — deliberately targeted the base of the pyramid.

The most recent chapter, beginning around 2020 under Loureiro's leadership, has been the evolution from retailer to ecosystem. WALMEX launched BAIT (Bodega Aurrera Internet y Telefonia) as a mobile virtual network operator, reaching 21.5 million active users by mid-2025 and becoming Mexico's third-largest internet access provider. Cashi, a digital wallet offering credit, remittances, and payments, was built to serve the financially unbanked customers already flowing through WALMEX stores daily. Health memberships, priced as low as MXN 30 per month, began in pilot in 2022 and scaled rapidly. Walmart Connect, the company's retail media business, grew 34% in 2023 alone. The thesis, articulated repeatedly by management, is that the five million daily store visitors in Mexico represent an acquisition channel for digital services that no pure-play fintech or telecom can match on cost.

Leadership transitions have tested this trajectory. Loureiro, a Brazilian who led WALMEX from 2016 to mid-2024 and oversaw the doubling of the business in under nine years, was promoted to oversee Walmart's operations across Canada, Chile, Mexico, and Central America. Ignacio Caride, previously head of omnichannel operations and a Mercado Libre veteran who joined Walmart in 2018, succeeded him as CEO in April 2024 but resigned in August 2025 after barely fifteen months in the role, leaving Cristian Barrientos Pozo as interim CEO. This rapid turnover at the top introduces execution risk at a moment when the ecosystem strategy demands sustained investment and integration.

What emerges from this history is a company that has been through three distinct lives: Jeronimo Arango's multi-format Mexican discount conglomerate (1958–1997), Walmart's rationalized single-country EDLP operator (1997–2010), and the omnichannel regional ecosystem that began taking shape after the Central American acquisition and has accelerated dramatically since 2020. Through all three phases, the constant has been a commitment to serving price-sensitive consumers and a willingness to adopt external operating models — first from Jewel, then from Walmart corporate — while adapting them to Mexican realities. The company that started with one discount store on Calle Bolivar now operates over 4,100 stores across six countries, runs a top-three mobile network, and extends credit to customers who have never held a bank account. The founding instinct — that discount access creates durable loyalty among underserved consumers — has proven remarkably persistent across seven decades.

## 2. Management & Incentives

WALMEX is in the midst of the most comprehensive leadership turnover in its recent history. In August 2025, CEO Ignacio Caride resigned abruptly — described by Bloomberg as departing "for personal reasons" — after a period of disappointing profitability (net income had dropped 10% in Q2 2025 despite 8% revenue growth). Cristian Barrientos Pozo, who was then running Walmart Chile, stepped in as interim CEO on August 1 and was confirmed permanently on October 7, 2025. In a span of roughly three months, the company also installed Paul Lewellen as Chief Omnichannel Operating Officer, Prathibha Rajashekhar as CEO of Sam's Club, Javier Andrade as Chief Merchandising Officer, and Michelle Benavides as Chief Legal Officer. Then in March 2026, WALMEX announced that CFO Paulo Garcia would depart by August 2026, to be replaced by Camilo Canedo Priesca (previously CFO of Walmart Canada). The average tenure of the current executive committee is roughly 1.5 years. This is a material governance risk: nearly every C-suite seat has turned over within a twelve-month window.

That said, the nature of these appointments partially mitigates the risk. Barrientos has 26 years at Walmart, including 13 years in Mexico and Central America across Bodega Aurrera operations, Central America country management, and the Chile CEO role. He holds an MBA from Universidad Adolfo Ibanez. Lewellen brings 35 years of Walmart U.S. operations experience, rising from store associate to SVP of Operations, with an MBA from John Brown University. Garcia, the outgoing CFO, was a seasoned external hire (22 years at Unilever, then CFO of Ahold Delhaize Europe) who joined WALMEX in August 2021 and provided continuity through the transition. His replacement, Canedo, is another Walmart-system insider who led financial planning for U.S. e-commerce and then served as CFO of Walmart Canada. This is the Walmart Inc. playbook in action: Bentonville rotates its best operators through international markets, and WALMEX is effectively a subsidiary that draws from the global talent pool rather than a standalone company with its own bench.

The board consists of eleven regular members, of whom five (approximately 50%) are classified as independent under Mexican securities law — well above the BMV's minimum threshold of 25%. The board includes four women (around 40%), and it met seven times in 2024 with 94.7% average attendance. The chairman is Enrique Ostale, a Chilean retail veteran who himself served as WALMEX CEO from 2014 to 2019 after running Walmart Chile and Central America before that. He holds an MSc in accounting and finance from the London School of Economics. In early 2025, Tom Ward (a Walmart Inc. executive) resigned from the board and was replaced by Rachel Brand, a former U.S. Associate Attorney General, and later in the year, Ernesto Cervera Gomez also departed. Several board seats are held by Walmart Inc. executives, which reflects the 70% parent ownership. The board's dual role — serving both the minority public float and the controlling parent — is the central governance tension at WALMEX and one investors should monitor.

Walmart Inc. owns approximately 70% of WALMEX's outstanding shares (roughly MXN 700 billion in value at current prices). This means the parent has overwhelming economic alignment with minority shareholders on fundamentals like revenue growth and margin expansion, but potential misalignment on transfer pricing, related-party transactions, and capital allocation timing. Management's direct equity ownership is minimal compared to the parent's stake. WALMEX, as a Mexican-listed subsidiary, does not disclose individual executive shareholdings with the same granularity as U.S. proxies, and there is no evidence of meaningful insider purchases by the new leadership team. In practical terms, "skin in the game" at WALMEX is primarily exercised through the parent's dominant position rather than through direct executive ownership.

WALMEX discloses limited detail on executive pay relative to U.S. norms. From investor day commentary and annual filings, the structure appears to follow the standard Walmart Inc. framework: a base salary, short-term variable compensation tied to operational metrics (likely same-store sales growth, EBITDA, and inventory performance), and longer-term equity participation through Walmart Inc. restricted stock units. At the 2023 Investor Day, management referenced a compensation model consisting of "fixed payment plus variable and compensation by [equity] strokes," with a premium built in for the Mexico market. The company has publicly invested in associate-level compensation — reducing turnover by 30 percentage points in 2022 through wage increases — and cited ongoing investments in its "associate value proposition" through 2025. However, specific CEO or CFO pay figures, equity vesting schedules, and performance hurdle disclosures are not publicly available with the precision one would see in a U.S. proxy statement. This opacity is a minor negative for governance quality assessment.

Capital allocation is an area of genuine strength. In FY2025, the company generated MXN 82.4 billion in cash from operations, deployed MXN 36.3 billion in capital expenditure (primarily new stores and logistics), and returned MXN 28.9 billion in dividends plus MXN 8.8 billion in share buybacks — totaling MXN 37.7 billion to shareholders, or roughly 46% of operating cash flow. The annualized dividend of MXN 1.69 per share translates to an approximately 3% yield, and the five-year dividend growth rate runs at about 8.4%. The company opened 71 stores in the first three quarters of 2025, contributing 1.8% to total revenue growth, ahead of its own investor day guidance. Working capital improved materially (inventories were 3.4 days lower year-on-year in Q3 2025), and the company has no net debt. This is a capital allocation framework that balances reinvestment with returns, and management has consistently delivered on its stated targets.

On the quantitative side, the execution record is solid. WALMEX gained market share against ANTAD self-service for ten consecutive quarters through Q3 2025, with the gap reaching 280 basis points — the widest since mid-2021. The company met its 2023 Investor Day target of doubling the business from 2015 levels in under nine years. E-commerce grew 20% in Q3 2025, and new businesses (Walmart Connect, Bait mobile, Cashi fintech) have been delivered on schedule: Bait reached 23.5 million users, and the company secured its fintech license. On the qualitative side, Barrientos has maintained strategic continuity after the CEO transition — the three priorities he articulated (EDLP, availability, e-commerce acceleration) are identical to the prior regime's, which signals discipline rather than disruption. CFO Garcia's forward guidance on margins and share gains in Q3 and Q4 2025 proved accurate. The one area where rhetoric and reality may diverge is the pace of e-commerce acceleration; marketplace growth decelerated to 11.2% in Q3 2025 (down from higher rates), and management has been vague on specific targets for 2026, deferring to a future "Walmex Day" for guidance.

The bottom line: WALMEX's management team is almost entirely new, which is the principal governance concern. But the individuals are Walmart-system veterans placed by a controlling parent with enormous economic incentive to get the appointments right. Capital allocation is disciplined, the board has adequate independence by Mexican standards, and the track record of operational execution is strong. The main risks are the opacity of executive compensation, the inherent conflict between controlling and minority shareholder interests, and whether so many simultaneous leadership changes might disrupt execution during a soft consumer environment.

## 3. Competitive Landscape

Mexico's retail sector, valued at roughly USD 420 billion in 2025, is a two-tier market. The formal, modern-trade channel is an oligopoly dominated by four listed chains — WALMEX, Soriana, Grupo Chedraui, and La Comer — that together account for more than half of organized grocery sales. Beneath them sits an enormous informal tier of tiendas de la esquina (corner shops) and open-air markets that still command a meaningful share of household food spending, particularly in lower-income and rural areas. The convenience segment, dominated by FEMSA's OXXO with over 24,000 locations and nearly 29% of Mexico's total retail footprint, occupies a separate but increasingly overlapping competitive space. Warehouse clubs add another dimension, with WALMEX's Sam's Club competing directly against Costco Mexico's roughly 40 units.

Within the formal grocery channel, WALMEX's scale advantage is overwhelming. Its approximately 4,265 stores generate roughly 3.9 times the revenue of its nearest pure supermarket competitor, Soriana, which operates around 800 stores and produces approximately USD 9 billion in annual sales on essentially flat growth. Chedraui, the third-largest player, runs about 618 stores across 25 states in Mexico and has been aggressively expanding its small-format Supercito brand (277 units by late 2025, with 130 more planned annually), producing solid results — outperforming ANTAD for 19 consecutive quarters by Q1 2025. La Comer, by contrast, is a niche premium operator with roughly 85 stores and USD 2.5 billion in revenue, targeting upper-income consumers in Mexico City, Guadalajara, and Monterrey through its City Market and Fresko formats. La Comer competes on experience and curation rather than price and is not a meaningful threat to WALMEX's core customer base.

WALMEX's moat rests on several interlocking advantages that are difficult to replicate individually, let alone in combination. The most fundamental is cost leadership anchored in scale. With over MXN 1 trillion in annual revenue (FY25), WALMEX commands purchasing leverage that no domestic competitor can match. This is amplified by access to Walmart Inc.'s global procurement network, logistics expertise, and technology stack. Management has been explicit that the company's DNA is "everyday low pricing" (EDLP), and the numbers validate this: price perception improved 260 basis points year-over-year in Q4 2025, a direct result of sustained investment in price gaps against competitors. Crucially, management has stated that gross margin expansion (to 24.2% in FY25, up 10 bps) is not achieved by sacrificing price competitiveness but through mix improvement, private-brand penetration (up 50 bps YoY), and shrink reduction powered by AI-driven demand forecasting and replenishment optimization.

The multi-format portfolio is another structural advantage. Bodega Aurrera serves price-sensitive consumers in lower-income and rural markets; Walmart Supercenters address the broad middle class; Sam's Club captures the warehouse club segment; and Walmart Express and Bodega Aurrera Express push into proximity retail. Management described Bodega as the "vehicle for growth" at Q3 2024 earnings, and the Express format directly contests the convenience-store territory historically owned by OXXO. This format breadth means WALMEX can capture consumer spending across income tiers and shopping occasions without cannibalizing its own banners. By contrast, Soriana has struggled to articulate a coherent multi-format story, and Chedraui's Supercito, while growing rapidly, remains far smaller in absolute footprint.

The supply chain constitutes a third pillar. WALMEX sources 83% of its inventory from products manufactured within Mexico, reducing foreign-exchange exposure and logistics complexity, while still leveraging Walmart's global scale for imported goods. The company's MXN 6 billion investment in 2025, including AI-powered distribution centers in the Bajio region and Tlaxcala, aims to cut logistics costs by 15–20% over time. This kind of capital-intensive infrastructure spending is something smaller rivals cannot easily match.

The most telling competitive metric is WALMEX's sustained outperformance of ANTAD (Mexico's national retail association benchmark). By Q3 2025, WALMEX had outpaced ANTAD self-service same-store sales for ten consecutive quarters, with the gap widening to 280 basis points — the widest since mid-2021. In Q4 2025, the gap remained strong at 190 basis points. This is not a one-quarter anomaly; it reflects a multi-year structural divergence that speaks to genuine competitive superiority rather than cyclical tailwinds. The consistency of this outperformance, maintained across varying macroeconomic conditions including a softer consumer environment in 2024–2025, underscores the durability of the advantage.

WALMEX's pricing power is unusual for a discount-oriented retailer. The EDLP model deliberately trades short-term margin for traffic and loyalty, yet the company has still expanded EBITDA margins to 10.2% for FY25 (up 50 bps). This margin expansion alongside price investment reflects operating leverage from scale, new revenue streams, and mix benefits — not price increases passed to consumers. Walmart Connect, the retail media network, grew 25–27% year-over-year in FY25 and represents a high-margin income source that effectively subsidizes lower shelf prices, a dynamic that smaller competitors cannot replicate without comparable digital traffic.

Barriers to entry in Mexican formal retail are substantial. The physical footprint required (WALMEX added 186 stores and 212,000 square meters of selling floor in FY25 alone), the supplier relationships built over decades, the technology and logistics infrastructure, and the working capital demands collectively make greenfield entry from a new domestic player virtually impossible. Even for existing players, the competitive gap is widening: Soriana is stagnating, Chedraui is growing but from a much smaller base, and La Comer occupies a different market segment entirely.

The most plausible erosion of WALMEX's position comes not from traditional grocery competitors but from three adjacent threats. First, eCommerce pure-plays and marketplaces, particularly Mercado Libre and Amazon Mexico, are expanding their grocery and essentials offerings. WALMEX has responded aggressively: eCommerce GMV grew 17% in FY25 to 9.1% of total GMV, with on-demand and marketplace segments showing double-digit growth. The company's 4,265 physical stores double as fulfillment nodes, an asset that pure digital competitors lack. Second, quick commerce and delivery platforms (Rappi, Cornershop/Uber) threaten to disintermediate the store visit, particularly in urban areas. WALMEX's own on-demand capabilities partially neutralize this, but the space remains fluid. Third, OXXO's sheer ubiquity — 24,000-plus stores functioning as payment points, parcel pickup locations, and increasingly as mini-grocery outlets — represents a proximity advantage that WALMEX's Express format is only beginning to contest. OXXO's integration with financial services and eCommerce logistics (Amazon pickup, Mercado Libre payments) gives it an ecosystem stickiness that goes beyond traditional retail competition.

WALMEX sits at the most powerful node in Mexico's consumer goods value chain. Its buyer power over suppliers like Grupo Bimbo and Kimberly-Clark de Mexico is significant — these companies need WALMEX's shelf space more than WALMEX needs any individual supplier. The emergence of Walmart Connect further tilts the relationship: suppliers now pay WALMEX for advertising access to its customer base, transforming the retailer from a distribution channel into a media platform. The co-branded card and Cashi digital wallet initiatives extend WALMEX's reach into financial services, increasing data capture and customer lock-in.

In summary, WALMEX holds the strongest competitive position in Mexican retail, underpinned by scale, cost leadership, format diversity, and a growing digital ecosystem. The advantages are self-reinforcing: scale enables lower prices, which drive traffic, which attracts suppliers and advertisers, which fund further price investment. The primary risks are not from traditional competitors — who are falling further behind — but from the structural shift toward digital commerce and the expansion of convenience-format rivals into adjacent categories.

## 4. Financial Characteristics

WALMEX generates over MXN 1 trillion in annual revenue (MXN 1,011,598M for FY25), derived almost entirely from the sale of consumer goods through physical stores and an accelerating eCommerce channel. Mexico dominates the revenue base, contributing MXN 836,428M (82.7% of total) in FY25, with Central America accounting for the remaining MXN 175,170M (17.3%). Within Mexico, the Bodega Aurrera discount formats represent the vast majority of the 3,316-store fleet, with 2,698 Bodega Aurrera units alone dwarfing the 338 Walmart Supercenters and 176 Sam's Clubs. Central America adds 949 units across six countries. This is overwhelmingly a food and general merchandise retailer, with "other income" (membership fees, rental income) representing only MXN 7,104M, or 0.7% of total revenues. The eCommerce channel, while still a fraction of the whole at 9.1% of GMV, is growing at 17.1% annually and represents the highest-growth vector in the business. "New businesses" — including Bait (telecom), Cashi (fintech), Connect (advertising), and health programs — are nascent but accretive to gross margin and increasingly material to the strategic narrative. Beneficios, the loyalty platform, reached 49.6 million active users, providing a data layer across the ecosystem.

WALMEX operates on the thin-margin, high-velocity model characteristic of discount retail, but its margins are notably strong relative to peers. FY25 gross margin was 24.2%, up 10 basis points year-over-year, reflecting the contribution of higher-margin new businesses and private label penetration (17.2% in Mexico, 19.5% in Central America). SG&A ran at 16.6% of revenue, up from 16.3% in FY24, driven by growth investments in eCommerce and supply chain. EBITDA margin was 10.2%, down 20 basis points on a full-year basis but improving in the second half — Q3 and Q4 each delivered 10.5%. Operating income margin was 7.8%, versus 8.1% in FY24. Net income margin compressed more sharply to 4.9% from 5.6%, almost entirely due to a higher effective tax rate: income taxes surged 25.7% to MXN 18,986M despite pre-tax income declining fractionally from MXN 68,931M to MXN 68,877M. This tax headwind — not operational deterioration — was the primary drag on bottom-line profitability. For context, Soriana's EBITDA margin sits around 7.5%, making WALMEX's 10.2% a 270-basis-point advantage, reflecting superior scale, procurement leverage, and operational discipline. Parent Walmart Inc. runs a consolidated operating margin nearer 4.5–5.0%, making WALMEX structurally more profitable than the broader group.

WALMEX's capital returns are exceptional for a retailer of its scale. Using the Q4 2025 balance sheet (total equity of MXN 235,580M) and FY25 net income of MXN 49,891M, return on equity comes to approximately 21.2%. ROIC, using operating income of MXN 78,494M net of the 27.6% effective tax rate and invested capital (equity plus lease liabilities minus excess cash), lands in the vicinity of 20%. These figures substantially exceed parent Walmart's consolidated ROE of approximately 23% and ROIC of approximately 12%, reflecting WALMEX's capital-light lease structure and dominant competitive position in a structurally growing market. The absence of funded debt is the key differentiator — WALMEX carries zero long-term financial debt on its balance sheet, with all non-current liabilities consisting of lease obligations (MXN 83,988M), deferred tax liabilities, and employee benefits. This is a genuinely debt-free operating company.

FY25 operating cash flow was MXN 82,438M, representing a conversion ratio of 80% against EBITDA of MXN 103,447M, or 165% of net income. This strong conversion is partly structural: as a retailer, WALMEX collects cash at the point of sale while paying suppliers on extended terms. The working capital cycle is negative — accounts payable of MXN 123,904M substantially exceed the sum of inventories (MXN 107,451M) and receivables (MXN 26,635M). At year-end FY25, payables financed approximately 115% of inventory, meaning suppliers effectively fund the entire inventory and then some. This negative working capital position is a hallmark of dominant grocery retailers and provides WALMEX with a permanent source of float.

Total assets stood at MXN 495,273M at December 2025, with total equity of MXN 235,580M and total liabilities of MXN 259,693M. There is no funded financial debt. Net cash (cash minus financial debt, excluding leases) was MXN 28,591M. Including lease liabilities of MXN 89,175M (current plus non-current), the enterprise is modestly leveraged, but this is entirely operational leverage through leased store footprint, not balance sheet risk. The current ratio of approximately 0.97x (MXN 164,109M in current assets versus MXN 169,717M in current liabilities) is typical for large-format retailers that fund operations through payables rather than drawing down liquidity. Intangible assets of MXN 42,647M likely reflect goodwill from historical acquisitions of the Central American operations.

FY25 investing cash outflows totaled MXN 38,982M in long-lived assets. Depreciation and amortization was MXN 24,953M, implying roughly MXN 25B in maintenance capex and MXN 12–14B in growth capex. The capex-to-revenue ratio of approximately 3.8% is consistent with the grocery retail peer set. However, WALMEX is entering a step change: the company announced a MXN 125B (approximately USD 6B) investment plan for 2025, roughly triple the historical run rate, funded from operating cash flow and balance sheet capacity. This investment targets 1,500 new stores through 2029, supply chain automation (new distribution centers in Guanajuato and Tlaxcala), and omnichannel infrastructure. If executed, this will temporarily compress free cash flow but should extend WALMEX's structural advantages in store density and logistics. For 2026, a further 10% capex increase to approximately MXN 43B has been announced.

WALMEX's inventory management is disciplined — inventories declined 2.9% year-over-year to MXN 107,451M even as revenue grew 5.5%, implying meaningful improvement in days inventory on hand. Accounts receivable of MXN 26,635M grew 7.1%, reflecting expansion of fintech and B2B activities. The payables base of MXN 123,904M grew modestly, keeping days payable outstanding stable. Net working capital changes contributed positively to cash flow in FY25, with inventory reductions and payables expansion more than offsetting receivables growth and tax payments.

WALMEX exhibits low cyclicality owing to its positioning as the lowest-cost grocery and general merchandise retailer in Mexico. Revenue grew in every year from FY20 through FY25, with no year posting negative top-line growth even through the pandemic and subsequent inflationary period. Same-store sales growth in Mexico ranged from 1.4% to 4.4% across the quarters of FY25, reflecting resilience rather than volatility. The Bodega Aurrera format, which accounts for the majority of the store base, explicitly targets value-conscious consumers and tends to gain share during periods of economic stress as consumers trade down. Seasonal patterns are modest, with Q4 consistently the strongest quarter due to holiday spending (Q4 FY25 revenue of MXN 282,849M versus MXN 240–246M in other quarters). The primary macro risk is Mexican consumer purchasing power erosion through inflation or peso depreciation, though the EDLP model provides a natural hedge against trade-down behavior.

What is distinctive about WALMEX's financial profile is the combination of zero financial debt, negative net working capital, dominant scale advantages yielding EBITDA margins 250+ basis points above the nearest domestic competitor, and ROE north of 20% — all achieved in a defensive, low-cyclicality consumer staples business. The emerging risk is execution on the MXN 125B capex program, which represents a genuine inflection in capital intensity and will test the company's ability to maintain returns on invested capital while nearly tripling its growth investment rate.

## 5. Red Flags

**The Bribery Stain That Never Fully Washed Out.** WALMEX sits at the center of one of the most notorious corporate corruption cases of the past two decades. In 2012, the New York Times revealed that Walmart's Mexican subsidiary had paid approximately USD 24 million in bribes to local officials to fast-track store permits during its period of explosive expansion. Worse, Walmart's own internal investigation had surfaced the problem as early as 2005, only for senior executives to shut it down without remediation. The eventual 2019 global settlement cost Walmart USD 282.7 million across the SEC and DOJ, and the parent operated under a compliance monitor for two years. For investors in WALMEX specifically, the question is not whether this is "priced in" — it is whether the institutional culture that enabled systematic bribery and then a cover-up has been genuinely reformed, or merely papered over with governance window-dressing. The fact that WALMEX's board requires only 25% independent directors — the bare Mexican legal minimum — does not inspire confidence. Walmart US still controls roughly 70% of the equity, making WALMEX a controlled company where minority shareholders are structurally subordinate.

**Governance: A Controlled Company With Revolving Leadership.** The abrupt resignation of CEO Ignacio Caride in August 2025 — barely 16 months into his tenure, during a quarter in which EBITDA contracted 90 basis points and net profit fell 10% — raises serious questions. The official explanation was "personal reasons," but the timing, coinciding with margin deterioration and the fallout from the COFECE antitrust ruling, invites skepticism. The appointment of Cristian Barrientos Pozo as interim CEO, a Walmart lifer imported from Chile, underscores the degree to which WALMEX's executive suite is a revolving door managed from Bentonville, not Mexico City. Paul Lewellen, the new Chief Omnichannel Operating Officer, also arrived directly from Walmart US, proudly declaring on the Q4 2025 call that "Walmart has no boundaries" and that Mexico would run the same global technology playbook. This is not local leadership — it is colonial management. The risk for minority shareholders is that strategic decisions are optimized for Walmart Inc's global P&L, not for WALMEX's standalone value. Transfer pricing arrangements between parent and subsidiary remain opaque, and with roughly 70% ownership, Walmart US can extract value through technology licensing fees, shared service charges, and procurement arrangements that never face genuine arm's-length scrutiny.

**The Profit-Revenue Divergence Is Real and Getting Worse.** FY2025 revenue grew 5.5%, but net income fell 7.3%. Q4 2025 net profit of MXN 14.6 billion missed analyst expectations of MXN 16.68 billion — a 3.9% year-over-year decline even in what should have been the strongest seasonal quarter. Management blamed a higher effective tax rate, but this explanation deserves forensic interrogation. In Q4 2024, the CFO acknowledged a one-off tax adjustment related to revaluation of fixed asset useful lives that temporarily lowered the ETR; the FY2025 tax rate increase may simply be the normalization after that benefit unwound. If so, it is not a headwind — it is the removal of a tailwind. Meanwhile, SG&A expenses have been trending adversely all year: 16.5% in Q1, rising to 17.2% in Q2, before a modest improvement to 17.0% in Q3. Management's euphemism of splitting expenses into "running the business" (labor cost inflation) and "growing the business" (tech, eCommerce, remodels) is a convenient rhetorical device that obscures the fundamental problem: costs are growing faster than revenue, and operating leverage is going in reverse. Q1 2025 operating cash flow was negative MXN 4.44 billion, attributed to working capital swings and tax payments, but negative operating cash flow in any quarter for a company of this scale warrants close scrutiny — particularly when the CFO simultaneously acknowledges that days payable declined due to lower purchases, a dynamic that suggests weakening demand rather than strategic supplier management.

**COFECE: A Ten-Year Sword of Damocles.** The Mexican antitrust regulator COFECE concluded a four-year investigation in 2024, ruling that WALMEX had leveraged its dominant market position for 13 years to impose abusive conditions on suppliers — specifically, discretionary discounts that discouraged suppliers from offering competitive terms to rival retailers. The fine itself (MXN 93.4 million, roughly USD 5 million) is trivial. The behavioral remedies are not. WALMEX is prohibited for ten years from retaliating against suppliers who collaborate with competitors, from imposing pricing conditions that restrict suppliers' ability to offer better terms elsewhere, and from requesting supplier information about commercial agreements with other retailers. COFECE can impose fines of up to 8% of WALMEX's total income for noncompliance. This structurally erodes one of WALMEX's most important competitive moats — its buying power leverage. On the Q4 2024 call, an analyst from JPMorgan directly asked whether the COFECE restrictions were lengthening the cash conversion cycle. The CFO's denial was emphatic ("absolutely no, 100% no"), but the question itself reveals sell-side concern about the operational impact. The irony of COFECE potentially being dismantled by Morena lawmakers does not eliminate the risk; it replaces a rules-based antitrust framework with political discretion, which is arguably worse.

**eCommerce Deceleration and Platform Transition Risk.** GMV growth slowed from roughly 20% at mid-year to 13.3% in Q4 2025, precisely during the period when WALMEX was migrating to Walmart's global eCommerce platform. Management frames this as temporary friction from platform transition; a skeptic sees it as evidence that a U.S.-designed system may not adapt smoothly to Mexico's fragmented logistics, cash-on-delivery preferences, and tienda-de-la-esquina competitive landscape. The 30% SKU reduction at Bodega Aurrera Express and Mi Bodega — presented as "rationalization" — could equally be read as a blunt cost-cutting measure that risks alienating the value-conscious customers these formats are supposed to serve, particularly in a macro environment where transactions were already declining 0.5% in Q4.

**Central America: The Forgotten Drag.** Deflation, weakened consumption, and intensifying competition — particularly in Costa Rica — make Central America a source of silent earnings erosion. FX translation effects add noise that makes it easy for management to present constant-currency metrics that look better than the cash reality. Investors should demand segmental disclosure of Central American cash flows, not just revenue and EBITDA in constant currency.

**The Bear Case.** Mexico's consumer economy remains under pressure: GDP growth expectations are a tepid 1.5% for 2026, remittances in dollar terms are declining, and real wage growth is uncertain. WALMEX's response — price investment to widen the gap against competitors — is rational but margin-destructive if volume does not follow. The combination of rising labor costs (with further labor reform on the horizon), COFECE behavioral restrictions that weaken supplier negotiations, eCommerce platform execution risk, and a leadership team in perpetual transition creates a scenario where revenue growth decelerates toward low single digits while cost pressures compound. In that world, the current valuation — roughly 25x earnings for a business generating negative quarterly operating cash flow — looks generous.

**Questions Investors Should Be Asking.** What is the total annual value of services, technology licensing, and procurement fees paid by WALMEX to Walmart Inc and its affiliates? What was the full-year FY2025 effective tax rate versus the normalized rate, and what portion of the Q4 2024 tax benefit was genuinely one-off? Can management provide quarterly segmental cash flow statements for Central America? What is the expected timeline for a permanent CEO, and why has Walmart not appointed one? What specific KPIs is management tracking for the eCommerce platform migration, and what is the fallback plan if GMV growth continues to decelerate? Has WALMEX received any additional communications from COFECE regarding compliance with the behavioral remedies? And finally: what is the board's assessment of the adequacy of anti-corruption controls, fourteen years after the bribery scandal first surfaced internally?

## 6. Investment Checklist

### 1. Understandable Business
**Verdict: YES**

WALMEX operates retail stores across Mexico and Central America under well-known formats: Bodega Aurrera (discount), Walmart Supercenter, Sam's Club, and Walmart Express. It sells groceries, general merchandise, and increasingly offers adjacent services (telecom via Bait, financial services via Cashi, advertising via Walmart Connect). The business model is straightforward: buy goods at scale, sell them at everyday low prices, and earn a margin on high throughput.

### 2. Durable Competitive Advantages (Widening Not Narrowing)
**Verdict: YES**

WALMEX generates roughly 3.9x more revenue than its nearest supermarket competitor (Soriana) and operates 3.5x more stores. Its scale advantage is self-reinforcing: higher purchasing volume drives lower costs, enabling lower prices, which drives more traffic, which funds more investment. The company has outperformed ANTAD self-service same-store sales for 10 consecutive quarters, with the gap widening to +280 bps. The new ecosystem verticals (Bait with 23.5M users, Walmart Connect ad revenue +27%, financial services credit +139%) are adding entirely new moats on top of the retail flywheel.

### 3. Long Reinvestment Runway at High Returns
**Verdict: YES**

Management stated at Investor Day 2024 that they doubled the business in under 9 years while increasing ROIC from 14.7% to 20.6%. Mexico's modern retail penetration still has room to grow — the country has a large, young, urbanizing population. WALMEX opened 115 new stores in Q4 2025 alone and earmarked USD 6 billion for further expansion. The ecosystem businesses (telecom, financial services, education) represent entirely new runways. eCommerce penetration at 9.1% is still well below developed-market peers.

### 4. High Returns on Incremental Capital
**Verdict: YES**

Current ROIC of ~17.5% and ROE of ~26.9% are exceptional for a retailer. Importantly, ROIC rose from 14.7% to 20.6% as the business doubled — meaning incremental capital was deployed at returns above the existing base, not below it. The new ecosystem verticals are high-margin, asset-light businesses built on top of the existing store footprint, suggesting incremental returns are even higher than the core retail business.

### 5. Strong Free Cash Flow Generation
**Verdict: YES**

FY25 CFO was MXN 82.4B against capex of MXN 36.3B, yielding implied FCF of approximately MXN 46B. This comfortably funded dividends of MXN 28.9B and buybacks of MXN 8.8B while still growing the store base aggressively. FCF conversion is robust — the business benefits from negative working capital and minimal maintenance capex relative to growth capex.

### 6. Conservative Balance Sheet
**Verdict: YES**

Total debt-to-equity is only 34.3%. The company held MXN 28.6B in cash at FY25 year-end. There is no meaningful financial leverage risk. WALMEX operates with a capital-light model and has no dependence on credit markets. The balance sheet is a fortress by retail standards.

### 7. Aligned Management with Skin in the Game
**Verdict: PARTIALLY**

WALMEX is ~70% owned by Walmart Inc., which ensures strategic alignment with the world's largest retailer but limits minority shareholder influence. New CEO Cristian Barrientos Pozo was appointed in Q3 2025, and there is limited public information on his personal shareholding. The historical FCPA bribery issue (2012) is a governance stain, though well in the past. With a controlling parent, minority shareholders are price-takers on capital allocation and governance decisions.

### 8. Rational Capital Allocation History
**Verdict: YES**

Revenue has compounded from MXN 702B (FY20) to MXN 1,012B (FY25) — a ~7.5% CAGR — while ROIC simultaneously expanded. MXN 37.7B was returned to shareholders in FY25 (dividends + buybacks), representing roughly 82% of FCF, while still funding aggressive store growth. Management has avoided empire-building acquisitions and funded growth organically. The ecosystem businesses were built incrementally on the existing platform rather than through expensive M&A.

### 9. Favourable Industry Structure
**Verdict: YES**

Mexico's modern grocery retail is a scale game where the leader's advantages compound. WALMEX holds roughly 25% of modern retail market share and is 3–4x larger than the next competitor. The industry is consolidating toward larger, more efficient players. The informal/traditional retail channel provides a long secular tailwind as consumers shift to modern formats. The market is growing at mid-single digits with stable competitive dynamics.

### 10. Reasonable Valuation Relative to Quality and Growth
**Verdict: PARTIALLY**

At a trailing P/E of ~18.3x, forward P/E of ~16.6x, and EV/EBITDA of ~10.4x, WALMEX trades at a meaningful discount to Walmart US (~35x+ forward P/E). The 3% dividend yield provides a floor. However, FY25 revenue growth of 5.5% missed the 6–7% guidance, net income declined 7.3%, and Central America faces headwinds. The valuation is not demanding but requires sustained mid-to-high single-digit growth to be justified.

### 11. Resilience to Recession or Disruption
**Verdict: YES**

WALMEX's core business is selling essential goods at the lowest prices. Bodega Aurrera is explicitly designed to serve price-sensitive consumers. In downturns, trade-down benefits discount retailers — customers migrate from Soriana or Chedraui to Bodega Aurrera. During COVID-19, WALMEX grew revenue 8.5% in FY20. Bait telecom is similarly recession-resistant. The main vulnerability is peso depreciation inflating import costs.

### 12. Transparent Management Communication
**Verdict: YES**

WALMEX provides detailed quarterly releases with granular SSS data by format, eCommerce GMV breakdowns, and ecosystem business KPIs. Investor Day presentations run 100+ pages with substantive coverage. Management sets explicit targets and reports against them honestly — including acknowledging eCommerce penetration was behind its goal. The ANTAD benchmark is externally verifiable.

### Overall Summary Assessment

WALMEX is a high-quality compounder that checks nearly every box. The business dominates its market with widening competitive advantages, generates excellent returns on capital (ROIC expanding as the business doubles), and throws off substantial free cash flow allocated rationally between reinvestment and shareholder returns. The balance sheet is conservative, communication transparent, and the business naturally recession-resilient.

The two areas requiring scrutiny are governance (70% Walmart Inc. ownership is a double-edged sword) and near-term execution (FY25's miss vs. guidance and net income decline show growth is not guaranteed). The ecosystem strategy (Bait, Connect, Cashi) is the most exciting long-term element — these businesses could drive significant margin expansion and ROIC improvement over the next decade. At ~18x earnings for a business with 17–20% ROIC and a long runway, the valuation is fair to attractive, with the governance discount being the main reason it trades cheaper than its quality would otherwise warrant.

## 8. Corporate Culture

WALMEX's corporate culture is the product of a hybridization that few multinationals have managed as effectively: the grafting of Sam Walton's Arkansas-born retail philosophy onto a deeply Mexican retail institution, Grupo Cifra. When Cifra's Jeronimo Arango partnered with Walmart in 1991 and then sold majority control in 1997, the merger was not a hostile cultural takeover. Cifra was already, by temperament, a Walton-compatible organization. Bodega Aurrera — founded by the Arango brothers in the 1960s — stocked goods to the ceiling in austere warehouses and undercut competitors by as much as 50%. The EDLP philosophy did not need to be imposed; it was indigenous. What Walmart layered on top was the operational scaffolding: the logistics discipline, the data infrastructure, the formalized "Every Day Low Cost" (EDLC) culture, and a managerial vocabulary around servant leadership and associate empowerment. The blend has proven durable. At the 2024 Investor Day, then-CEO Guilherme Loureiro invoked Walton directly — "As Sam Walton used to say, take care of our associates and they will take care of your customers" — in the same breath as announcing an all-time-high associate engagement score of 90%, four percentage points above the prior year. The language is inherited; the metrics suggest it is not merely performative.

The parent-subsidiary relationship with Bentonville is the central tension of WALMEX's cultural architecture. On paper, WALMEX operates with significant local autonomy — it is publicly listed on the BMV, maintains its own executive committee of 40 members, and historically developed initiatives like BAIT telecom and Cashi financial services organically, without Walmart US equivalents. In practice, however, the leash has shortened. The Q4 2025 earnings call featured new COO Paul Lewellen — a Walmart US veteran of 35 years — stating that "Walmart has no boundaries" and describing a global technology platform (Coastal) being deployed identically across markets. The appointment of Lewellen himself, alongside a new Sam's Club CEO (Prathibha Rajashekhar, also a Walmart US import) and a new CLO and CMO, signals a tightening of Bentonville's operational grip. Cristian Barrientos, the new CEO, is an internal promotion with 26 years of Latin American retail experience, but the surrounding C-suite has been heavily internationalized. Investors should watch whether this shift toward global standardization dampens the entrepreneurial impulse that produced BAIT and Cashi — genuinely original innovations that have no direct Walmart US precedent.

WALMEX's innovation track record is more impressive than most emerging-market retailers. BAIT, its MVNO telecom service, reached 11.8 million active users by December 2023 and offers up to 7x more data per peso than the market leader. The health membership program sold 1.2 million subscriptions in its first full year. Walmart Connect (retail media) grew 34% in 2023. Cashi granted over 500,000 credits in 2023 and is building a closed-loop digital wallet. These are not vanity pilots; they are scaled businesses built on a distinctive insight — that WALMEX's 5–6 million daily store visitors represent the lowest customer-acquisition-cost channel in Mexico. However, management has been candid about failures. Loureiro acknowledged at the 2024 Investor Day that eCommerce penetration at 6.8% of GMV was behind the double-digit target set four years earlier, and that Walmart Express and 1P eCommerce remained "areas of opportunity" — a euphemism for underperformance. This willingness to name what is not working is culturally healthy and distinguishes WALMEX from companies that bury bad news.

Glassdoor data for Walmart Mexico shows a 3.9/5 rating, with 72% of employees recommending the company to a friend — meaningfully above the 55% global Walmart average. Culture and values score 4.0/5, which is 25% higher than the company-wide norm. WALMEX holds Merco's top ranking for retail employers in Mexico, ranked seventh overall in Merco's 2024 ESG assessment, and has earned 25 consecutive years of Socially Responsible Company distinction from CEMEFI. Employee turnover, which was historically high in Mexican retail, has been "brutally" reduced according to management commentary at the 2023 Investor Day, though the company does not publicly disclose the exact figure. The understaffing concerns flagged in Glassdoor reviews suggest that EDLC culture can strain frontline workers, a perennial tension in the Walmart model worldwide.

No analysis of WALMEX's cultural integrity can omit the FCPA bribery scandal, exposed by the New York Times in April 2012. The facts were damning: systematic payments to Mexican officials to expedite store permits, followed by an internal cover-up at Bentonville. The resolution in 2019 cost Walmart $282 million in combined SEC and DOJ penalties. The cultural response was substantial: compliance staff expanded by over 30%, reaching approximately 2,000 people; an escalation procedure was created linking the chief ethics office directly to the board's Audit Committee; and at least eight senior executives across Mexico and Bentonville departed. The scandal remains the most significant ethical failure in WALMEX's history and is relevant because the cultural conditions that enabled it — decentralized operations with insufficient oversight, a growth-at-all-costs mentality in the 2000s expansion era, and a reflexive instinct to handle problems locally rather than escalate — were cultural failings, not just compliance ones. The post-scandal compliance apparatus is now robust, but investors should note that the very autonomy that makes WALMEX culturally effective also created the conditions for the bribery. The current trend toward Bentonville-driven standardization partly reflects lessons learned from that era.

WALMEX demonstrated strong adaptability during COVID, accelerating its omnichannel buildout and expanding on-demand delivery to 1,200 stores. The company's format portfolio — from the no-frills Bodega Aurrera to Sam's Club — gives it structural resilience across income segments. However, two red flags merit attention. First, the CEO transition has been turbulent: Ignacio Caride resigned abruptly in August 2025 for "personal reasons" after just over a year in the role, amid a 10% profit decline. Two CEO transitions in quick succession can erode the tacit knowledge and relationship networks that sustain culture. Second, the influx of Walmart US executives raises the risk of cultural homogenization. Lewellen's framing of "no boundaries" is efficient from a technology-deployment perspective but could gradually erode the local entrepreneurialism that produced WALMEX's most distinctive innovations. If BAIT and Cashi were born from WALMEX's understanding of Mexican consumers' unmet needs, an increasingly Bentonville-centric management layer may lack the instinct to identify and back the next such bet.

WALMEX's culture is a genuine competitive advantage — the EDLP philosophy has deep roots predating the Walmart acquisition, employee engagement metrics are strong, the post-FCPA compliance infrastructure is credible, and the innovation pipeline is real. The primary cultural risk is not decay but dilution: that the tightening integration with Walmart's global operating model will trade the entrepreneurial edge that distinguished WALMEX for the operational consistency that Bentonville values.

## 9. Earnings Call Deep Dive

### Narrative Evolution: From Confident Aggression to Defensive Fundamentalism

The most striking transformation across the four quarters of FY25 is the shift in management voice and strategic framing, driven by a complete leadership change. In Q2, CEO Ignacio Caride opened with unmistakable confidence: "Let me start by highlighting our growth of 8.3%... We are building this business for the future and with our commitment of doubling the business faster than we did before." The language was expansive — doubling, accelerating, leapfrogging. Caride spoke with the authority of someone executing a known playbook.

By Q3, Caride was gone. New CEO Cristian Barrientos introduced himself from Costa Rica with carefully hedged language: "I would like to share a few reflections from my first 90 days since rejoining Walmex... Even more exciting are the opportunities that I see going forward." The word "opportunities" recurred constantly — a classic new-CEO tell that simultaneously acknowledges problems and promises improvement without committing to specifics. Barrientos repeatedly drew on his Chile experience: "It's incredible how similar the situation that we are looking today in Mexico were with the situation that I founded in 2023 when I landed in Chile because both countries were growing 0%." This Chile analogy became a narrative crutch through Q4, used to justify a posture of patience during softening growth.

By Q4, the strategic vocabulary had consolidated around "three non-negotiables" — EDLP, availability, and e-commerce acceleration. This is a notable narrowing from the Q2 era, which featured a much broader canvas: Hot Sale momentum, Cashi open-loop launch, 1,500-store expansion ambition, and labor reform technology preparation. The Q4 narrative is fundamentally about getting ready rather than winning now. Barrientos: "We have seen in other markets how relevant is... what is in our control today to be prepared when the numbers came... we can accelerate 3, 4x above the market if we are very well prepared."

### Guidance and Delivery

CFO Paulo Garcia has been remarkably consistent on margins. In Q2, he stated: "You can expect from us to see gross margin expansion in the year as we've given as a guidance, and we continue to reiterate that." He delivered — FY25 EBITDA margin came in at 10.2%, up 50 bps. The mechanism was transparent: new businesses (Bait, Walmart Connect) contributed roughly 20–30 bps to gross margin each quarter, which funded price investments in the core.

Revenue guidance told a different story. The Q2 reaffirmation of 6–7% consolidated revenue growth looked comfortable at 8.3% reported growth. By Q3, Garcia narrowed Q4 expectations to "along the lines of what we did in Q2 and Q3." The actual Q4 SSS of +3.3% was the weakest quarter, with transactions turning negative at -0.5%. Garcia's Q4 framing became openly cautious: "We are still elaborating on [2026 targets] and probably you'll hear more about that in terms of the Walmex Day." Deferring guidance to an investor day is a classic signal of uncertainty.

### Analyst Concerns

Two topics dominated analyst questioning and visibly discomforted management.

**eCommerce deceleration** was the single most persistent theme. GMV growth slid from over 20% earlier in the year to 13.3% in Q4. Analysts from Goldman Sachs, BBVA, and Barclays pressed on this. Paul Lewellen acknowledged: "E-commerce growth this quarter was below our long-term ambition." Management attributed this to One Hallway platform migration friction and weak 1P extended assortment categories (especially TVs during Buen Fin). Garcia conceded: "Our extended assortment grew much less mid-single digit, and that was impacted by 1P." The language around One Hallway shifted from Caride's Q2 enthusiasm ("I'm very happy to be one of the users already testing the friends and family") to Barrientos' Q4 hedging about "a natural learning curve as customers go through an adaptation process."

**Bait user metrics** produced the most defensive moment. In Q2, Bob Ford from Bank of America directly challenged the discrepancy between WALMEX's reported 19.8M Bait users and the telecom regulator IFT's 8.4M figure. Garcia's response was notably legalistic: "Our methodology has been consistent throughout... is audited by a Big 4 company and is audited each quarter." Caride then pivoted hard: "I'm not so interested in the final number." The promise of a press clarification "tomorrow" suggested real sensitivity. By Q4, Bait had grown to 26.4M users with MXN 11.5B in annual revenue — the metric discrepancy was never revisited.

### Key Reveals

1. **Bodega penetration in lower-income households up 300+ bps** (Q4, Lewellen): "We saw household penetration in lower income segments increased more than 300 basis points." This is the clearest evidence that Bodega is gaining wallet share in a downturn among exactly the demographic it needs.

2. **Bait customers spend 2x the average ticket** (Q4 Q&A, Garcia): "A customer that is in Byte, the average ticket is more than 2x what we see in a customer that's non-Byte." This is the strongest evidence for the ecosystem flywheel thesis — but management refuses to give a user target. Garcia explicitly said: "I'm not going to put a number in the market that holds me accountable on that."

3. **SKU rationalization of 30%+ in small formats** (Q4, Lewellen): This was large enough to draw two separate analyst questions. The magnitude is aggressive and signals genuine strategic discipline under the new regime, though it also acknowledges past assortment bloat.

4. **Hot Sale purchase intention vs. actual** (Q2, Garcia): "When we did that... the number was 45%. The real number... was actually 62%, and that compares with 52% last year." This granular consumer data reveals management confidence in their value proposition driving latent demand.

5. **Walmart Connect advertising budget pressure** (Q4, Lewellen): "We have seen some pressure on advertising budgets from suppliers in the last two quarters, given the current macro environment, which has moderated growth in the short term." Connect grew only 5% in Q4 versus 27% at mid-year. This is a canary for supplier sentiment about Mexican consumer spending.

### Q&A Dynamics

Management engaged genuinely on operational questions — availability metrics, SKU rationalization, private brand strategy, and store remodel philosophy all got detailed treatment. Garcia excels at the "let me reframe what we said" technique, often repackaging guidance in slightly different terms to manage perceptions without changing substance. The weakest engagement came on forward-looking commitments: 2026 guidance was deferred entirely, eCommerce growth targets remained aspirational rather than quantified, and Bait user trajectory was explicitly refused. The arrival of Lewellen brought a new voice — operationally fluent and Walmart-US-centric, but his repeated invocations of "global leverage" were vague on Mexico-specific execution timelines. The overall pattern reveals a management team confident in its operational playbook but unwilling to stick its neck out on when macro recovery will validate the "prepare now, harvest later" thesis.

## 10. Strategic Documents Analysis

### Strategic Priorities: A Five-Pillar Flywheel

WALMEX's strategic messaging has coalesced around five interconnected pillars: (1) omnichannel retail dominance, (2) eCommerce acceleration, (3) ecosystem businesses (Walmart Connect advertising, Bait telecom, financial services), (4) supply chain and operational efficiency, and (5) people and culture. The framing is deliberately flywheel-like — management presents these not as independent initiatives but as mutually reinforcing loops where physical store density enables last-mile eCommerce fulfillment, which generates data that powers Walmart Connect's ad targeting, which subsidizes lower prices, which drives traffic back to stores.

Between the 2023 and 2024 Investor Days, the emphasis shifted materially. In 2023, the narrative leaned heavily on store-count expansion and format optimization — Bodega Aurrera as the engine of market-share gains in Mexico's fragmented grocery market, with eCommerce positioned as an emerging complement. By 2024, management elevated the ecosystem businesses from "promising adjacencies" to core strategic pillars. Walmart Connect and financial services received dedicated stage time and explicit revenue targets. This is a meaningful evolution: it signals management recognizes that same-store sales growth alone cannot sustain the valuation premium, and that higher-margin platform-style revenues are needed to shift the earnings trajectory.

### Capital Allocation: Rhetoric Favors Reinvestment, Numbers Broadly Confirm It

Management articulates a clear hierarchy: organic investment first (new stores, remodels, eCommerce infrastructure, technology), followed by dividends, with buybacks as a residual. M&A is conspicuously absent from the stated framework — WALMEX has historically grown organically rather than through acquisition, and management shows no appetite to change this.

The numbers broadly support the rhetoric. Annual capex has been running at approximately 3.0–3.5% of revenue, directed at 100+ new store openings per year plus eCommerce fulfillment capacity. Dividend payout ratios have been steady at around 45–50% of net income, signaling commitment to shareholder returns without sacrificing reinvestment. However, one tension deserves scrutiny: the pace of investment in ecosystem businesses (Bait, financial services) has been aggressive relative to their near-term return profiles. Bait in particular has required sustained capital while generating negligible direct profitability — management frames it as a customer acquisition and retention tool, but the financial bridge between "strategic moat" and "accretive returns" remains underdisclosed.

### Long-Term Targets: Directionally Sound, Deliberately Imprecise

WALMEX has set long-term guidance around mid-to-high single-digit revenue growth, stable-to-expanding EBITDA margins, and double-digit EPS growth. The FY25 results (revenue of MXN 1,011,598M and EBITDA margin of 10.2%) are consistent with this framework. eCommerce GMV penetration reaching 9.1% shows genuine traction against what was likely a mid-teens medium-term target.

The credibility of these targets is mixed. Top-line growth is plausible given Mexico's structural tailwinds: a young, urbanizing population, retail formalization away from informal channels, and nearshoring-driven economic momentum. The EBITDA margin target is more fragile. The ecosystem businesses (Walmart Connect in particular) are high-margin and should be accretive as they scale. But eCommerce fulfillment economics remain dilutive versus in-store transactions, and management has not disclosed the margin gap transparently. If eCommerce penetration continues climbing toward 15–20%, the mix effect could pressure consolidated margins unless ad revenue and financial services scale proportionally. Management has not quantified this interplay, which is a notable omission.

### Narrative vs. Reality

Where strategy and financials align most clearly is in the core retail business. Same-store sales growth has consistently outpaced inflation and the broader retail sector, validating the market-share narrative. Store productivity metrics are strong, and the 4,265-unit footprint creates genuine density-based competitive advantages.

Where divergence appears is in the ecosystem businesses. Management presents Walmart Connect, Bait, and financial services as an integrated platform, but the financial disclosure around these segments remains thin. Walmart Connect ad revenue is growing rapidly off a small base, but its absolute contribution to EBITDA is not broken out clearly. Bait's subscriber count is reported, but unit economics are not. Financial services are at an even earlier stage. The strategic narrative has run ahead of the financial evidence, and until segment-level profitability is disclosed, investors are asked to take on faith that these investments will be accretive.

### Addressable Market: Large and Real, but Loosely Defined

Management frames TAM broadly — Mexico's USD 300B+ retail market, of which formal modern trade is perhaps 40–45%, leaving enormous runway for formalization. This is directionally correct and supported by demographic data. The eCommerce TAM framing references Mexico's low online penetration relative to Brazil and other comparable markets. However, the company is vague about realistic serviceable addressable market. Stating that Mexico's retail market is large tells you little about how much WALMEX can actually capture given competitive dynamics with FEMSA (Oxxo), Chedraui, Soriana, and Amazon Mexico.

### Risk Acknowledgement and Evolution of Messaging

WALMEX's strategic documents acknowledge macro risks (peso volatility, inflation, consumer sentiment) at a surface level. What is conspicuously underaddressed is competitive risk from Amazon Mexico's fulfillment buildout, regulatory risk around Bait's telecom operations, and execution risk on financial services. Management also says little about Central American operations, which contribute roughly 25% of stores but receive disproportionately little strategic airtime — suggesting either that the growth thesis is Mexico-centric or that Central America is underperforming expectations.

The most important shift between 2023 and 2024 is the pivot from "Mexico's best retailer" to "Mexico's retail platform." The 2023 Investor Day was anchored in operational excellence — store formats, price leadership, supply chain efficiency. The 2024 event introduced platform economics language: monetizing customer data, building advertising revenue streams, embedding financial services into the shopping journey. This is the same playbook Walmart US has executed with Walmart+, Walmart Connect, and GoLocal, adapted for the Mexican market. The strategic ambition is right, but WALMEX is earlier in the curve than the US parent, and the Mexican market's digital infrastructure and consumer behavior present different challenges. Management deserves credit for articulating a coherent multi-year vision, but the proof points remain incomplete, and investors should watch closely for segment-level disclosure improvements as the primary signal of whether the platform narrative is real or aspirational.
