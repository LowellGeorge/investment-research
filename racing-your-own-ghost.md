# Racing Your Own Ghost

## Part One: The Ghost

The summer of 1993 was hot, or I remember it that way, and I spent most of it indoors with the curtains half drawn.

Super Mario Kart had arrived in Britain that January. There was a mode on it called Time Trial, which had no opponents in it at all — no shells, no banana skins, nobody to knock you into the verge. Just you, one kart, an empty track, going round and round. On paper it is the dullest thing on the cartridge. It was the only thing I played.

Because it did have an opponent, after a fashion. The machine kept your best lap and then set it running alongside you: a pale translucent copy of your own kart, taking every corner exactly the way you had taken it. You raced that. And when you beat it, the run that beat it became the new thing to race.

Mario Circuit One, for weeks — with one pause, that June, to take note of Shane Warne's ball of the century. And I chased hundredths of a second. Not seconds — hundredths. I learned that if you clipped one particular corner a hair tighter you came out of it fractionally quicker, and that the pale kart beside you would show you on the next lap exactly where you had found the time and exactly where you had given it back.

If ever stopped at the Pearly Gates and asked where, specifically, I'd tried hardest in life — the true maximum effort of a whole life, the thing I gave the most of myself to — the honest answer is not my education, or my work, or anything I would like it to be. It is Super Mario Kart, Time Trial, Mario Circuit One — the first course of the Mushroom Cup, and the only one I ever truly went deep on — summer of 1993, against a ghost of myself.

I did not think about it again for thirty years.

## Part Two: Thirty Years Later

Then a couple of years ago I stumbled on what may be the most astonishing story I have ever read, and it sent me straight back to that bedroom.

It is in Max Bennett's book, A Brief History of Intelligence, and it goes like this. In the 1990s the neurophysiologist Wolfram Schultz put electrodes into the midbrain of monkeys, recording from the cells that release dopamine — the chemical everyone had long since filed, lazily, under pleasure. He gave the monkeys a drop of juice and watched the cells fire. Fine: reward, dopamine, as expected. But then he looked more carefully at when they fired, and the pleasure story fell apart.

Three things. When the juice arrived unannounced, the neuron fired — a clean burst. When a light reliably preceded the juice, so the animal learned to expect it, the burst walked backwards in time: it left the juice and attached itself to the light, to the earliest thing that predicted the reward, and the juice itself — now fully expected — drew no response at all. And when the light came but the promised juice did not, the neuron did something wonderful: at the exact moment the juice was due, its firing dipped below its resting rate. It went quiet in disappointment, on schedule.

Sit with that middle one. The cell is not reporting reward. A fully predicted reward earns silence. The cell is reporting the gap between what was predicted and what arrived — surprise upward, disappointment downward, and nothing at all when the world unfolds exactly as guessed. It is not a pleasure signal. It is an error signal.

Two theorists, Peter Dayan and Read Montague, saw what it was, and this is the part that raises the hair on your arms. Years earlier Richard Sutton had written down, as pure mathematics, an algorithm for learning to predict delayed rewards. Its central quantity is the moment-to-moment change in predicted reward — the temporal difference — and a learner that chases that quantity will, given time, teach itself the value of every step on a long path to a far-off payoff. Schultz's dopamine neuron was computing Sutton's term. The equation was already running, in a monkey's midbrain, tens of millions of years before anyone wrote it down. And it's there in yours right now, delivering that slight pall of disappointment caused by the fact that you don't agree — hopefully yet! — that this is the most astonishing thing you have ever read. That flatness is the very dip I described a moment ago: a predicted reward that has not arrived. Reading that line, just now, you were the disappointed monkey.

Bennett lays the machinery out cleanly. Two parts. The actor is the basal ganglia — a structure so old and so conserved that it sits, in nearly the same form, in every vertebrate that has ever lived, all the way back to the first fish, and older by far than any monkey. It spends its life inhibiting, holding down every possible action at once, and choosing simply by releasing its grip on one of them. The critic is the dopamine system, scoring each of the actor's choices not against the reward itself but against its own shifting prediction of the reward. An actor that acts and a critic that grades — and the signal the actor learns from, in Sutton's own words, is not reward but the change in predicted reward from one moment to the next. No one outside is grading the moves. The standard the actor is held to is one the brain generates for itself, and as the actor gets better the standard rises to meet it. It is the ghost, in other words, running on wet tissue a very long time before anyone thought to put one in a cartridge.

## Part Three: The Thing Bennett Walks Past

Bennett calls this a magical bootstrapping. And then he moves on — as he must, with several hundred million years of brain evolution still ahead of him, and he covers that ground miraculously well. I could not move on.

He does not miss the difficulty. He lays the circle out plainly. A position is only as good as the actor's ability to exploit it, so the critic's judgement is hostage to the actor's skill; but the actor can learn only from a critic whose judgements are sound, so its skill is hostage to the critic. Each one's competence depends on the other's.

And the critic itself is learning as it goes. Its judgement at one moment is revised by what happens next; that revision teaches the actor; the actor changes what it does; and the changed actor gives the critic a new world to judge. The yardstick and the thing being measured are being built together.

There is no teacher supplying the value of every position, no answer key for every move, no adult in the room saying this much and no more. The world contributes only an occasional verdict — the juice, the win, the lap time — and leaves actor and critic to manufacture almost everything in between. Somehow the pair climb instead of merely confirming one another's first mistakes.

Bennett's resolution is empirical: run it and see. In simulation, actor and critic sharpened one another. Then the backgammon program showed how far the trick could go. That settles whether the loop can work. It does not yet tell us what prevents a bootstrapped error from becoming the teacher of the next error. They converge names the phenomenon; it is not yet the mechanism.

I think it is genre. The book is a history, and in the history the answer genuinely was empirical: it worked in simulation, then it worked on a backgammon board, and that settled the matter for everyone involved. Bennett's own curiosity then runs off in a different direction, and a magnificent one: he wants to know whether this was a human invention or something evolution had found first, long before anybody wrote it down. That is a question about where the trick came from, not about why the trick holds.

And it is not a parlour trick tucked away in fish brains. This same humble loop, coupled to deep learning, is a large part of what has been built in the last ten years — which is why I could not let it go. The finest science storyteller of his generation calls it magic, and that is fair, because it is. But magic is only a trick you have not been shown yet. So: how does it actually work?

## Part Four: Where The Knowledge Comes From

Start with what the world withholds. A game of Go ends in one bit, win or lose — it does not tell you which of your two hundred moves was the good one. A lap ends in one number, and does not tell you which corner cost you the hundredth. A joke gets a laugh or it doesn't, and the silence never says which word was wrong.

But it is not that the world is silent. It hands over a great deal: every board you reached, every corner you took, exactly what followed each thing you did. What it never hands over is the label — this move helped, that one lost you the game. And yet people and machines both end up knowing precisely that: the decisive move, the corner where the time went, the syllable the whole thing turned on. Nobody supplied it. So where did it come from?

The loop manufactures it. It takes the coarse verdict the world hands back and breaks it, by its own effort, into the fine-grained lesson the world never gave. The tool it uses is a prediction. The verdict arrives once, at the end, but a guess about the verdict can be made at every step along the way — and a guess can be checked, not against the truth, which is not available yet, but against the next guess, made one step later with one step more information. If the estimate rises between two moments, whatever happened in between was good news. If it falls, bad. One end-of-game bit becomes a signed comment on every move that led to it.

Which is elegant, and ought to be impossible. An estimate is being checked against an estimate. Nothing in that arrangement is true. There are three things to explain, and they come in order — call them anchoring, mutual movement and ascent. Why do the guesses not simply curl round and start confirming one another's mistakes? How do two learners, each altering the problem the other is trying to solve, avoid chasing one another's tails for eternity? And even if the thing holds steady, why should it climb at all, rather than sitting there perfectly stable and getting nowhere?

Take the first, anchoring. The chain has an end. Play the game out and eventually there is no next guess left — only the result, which is not a guess at all. That last link is nailed down by what actually happened, and every replay drags the correction one step further back up the chain. The guesses are not certifying each other. They are a line of people passing a bucket, and at the far end somebody is standing in the river.

And errors shrink as they travel. A prediction is not the next prediction; it is the next prediction discounted a little, because a reward two moves away is worth marginally less than one you are holding. A mistake handed backwards arrives fainter than it left, fainter again at the next handoff, and eventually it is nothing. Truth propagates; error decays. Which is why the later guess does not need to be wiser than the earlier one. It only needs to be closer to the end.

So the system is not floating free. There is no fixed teacher. There is a fixed point. For any fixed way of playing there is exactly one set of values that fit together consistently — where every position is worth what it actually leads to — and this whole apparatus is a machine for feeling its way toward that set. Nobody supplies it. Nobody knows it. But it is there, and it is not moving.

And then the actor moves, and it is. That is mutual movement, the second problem, and the actor creates it simply by getting better. A position is not worth the same thing in every pair of hands: a board a champion can convert is worth little to a novice who cannot. So the moment the actor starts improving — which is the entire point of the exercise — the fixed point the critic was patiently approaching gets up and walks off. Two learners, each trying to learn something the other keeps altering; two people attempting to tune to one another's pitch while both keep changing pitch.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple. Separate the speeds. Let the critic update quickly enough to keep pace with how the actor is playing now, and let the actor change slowly enough that, from the critic's side, it is very nearly standing still. Each then treats the other as furniture. It is tuning a guitar to a piano rather than to another guitar being tuned. And nobody is fooled — at that ratio the approximation is honest, in the way a chemist is being honest when she solves for the motion of electrons with the atomic nuclei held fixed. The nuclei are moving. They are just so slow, in electron terms, that pretending otherwise costs nothing worth having. Konda and Tsitsiklis proved as much around the turn of the century — for a particular family of these algorithms, in an environment that behaves consistently, with the adjustments shrinking as they go. And what it settles on may be merely good rather than the best available. The point established is narrow and it is enough: two learners can change together without necessarily chasing each other into chaos.

For each way of playing there is a fixed point; the trick is to let the critic nearly reach it before the actor moves it.

Which accounts for why the thing does not fall over, and not at all for why it rises. A system can be perfectly stable and get precisely nowhere. So, ascent.

Here the answer is not a condition but a guarantee, and it is the strongest of the three — provided the critic's estimates are sound. Suppose you have a decent estimate of how good each position is, given the way you currently play. Now change your behaviour: at every point, do whatever your own estimates say leads somewhere better. The resulting way of playing is provably at least as good as the one you started with — and strictly better unless you were already perfect. So the cycle runs: judge how you play, act on the judgement, arrive at a better way of playing, judge that. Each turn of the crank is guaranteed not to go backwards. That is the ratchet with teeth in it. The climb is not luck, and it is not emergence. It is a theorem.

It is worth naming what is not on that list. Any method that learns from noisy samples needs its adjustments large enough to keep travelling and small enough to stop chasing the noise, and there is a well-known result about how to size them. But that is the entry fee for stochastic learning of every kind — it governs the training of an ordinary neural network exactly as much — and it explains nothing whatever about bootstrapping. General machinery is easy to mistake for a specific answer. The three above are specific: none of them can even be stated about a system learning from an answer key.

They are also not a licence for confidence. The failure modes are real and named: combine bootstrapping with a function approximator and off-policy data and you get what the field calls the deadly triad, which does what it sounds like. And the guarantees are cleanest in the small, exact case. Put a large neural network in the middle and several of them soften into rules of thumb that usually hold. The theory explains why the trick can work. It does not fully explain why it works as well as it does at the scale we are now running it.

Anchoring, mutual movement, ascent: between them, those three explain why the loop holds together. They do not explain why it gets rich — why a thing that started with one bit a game ends up knowing a hundred thousand things. That comes from somewhere else, and it comes from the two halves quietly setting each other's homework. A slightly better critic lets the actor tell apart two moves it had been treating as identical. Acting on that difference takes it somewhere it has never been, which the critic has never had to judge. Judging it sharpens the critic, whose new distinctions change the actor again. Neither of them is following a syllabus. Each is writing the other's.

Which is where the ghost comes in, though not in the way the shape of this essay might suggest. The ghost is not the critic. Nothing about it computes a prediction error, and it does no judging at all. Its job is narrower, and it is the one nobody else does: it keeps the next target within reach. That is the hardest practical problem in any self-improving system — finding something to measure yourself against. Too strong and you lose every time, learning nothing; too weak and you win every time, learning nothing. You want an opponent pitched just at the edge of what you can do, and a recording of your own last run is exactly that, for free. It is not better than you. It is the best you have already been: close enough to read, far enough to show the gap. Nobody has to tune it, and it re-levels itself every time you improve. That is how a summer of hundredths compounds into something absurd, and how a machine playing itself can start from nothing and finish beyond every human alive.

And underneath all of that there is one more floor, which I did not find until late. I have been saying that improving changes what counts as good. That is true — but only of one of the two things the word is doing.

The reward is fixed. Winning, the drop of juice, the number on the clock: the world sets those, and nothing you do touches them. But the value of a position is a different quantity altogether, and it is defined only relative to a way of playing. It means what you can expect from here, given that you carry on as you are. So the champion's winning board really is worth less in the novice's hands, because the novice will not convert it. Play better and every position becomes worth more than it was a moment ago. Same board. Different number.

Which sounds like vertigo and is the opposite, because there is a third level above both. There is also what a position is worth under the best possible play — the most that could ever be extracted from it by anyone. That does not move at all. It is set by the game itself, the way the true fastest lap of Mario Circuit One was fixed by the physics of the cartridge before anybody put the cartridge in.

So the whole thing comes apart into something almost tidy. Each way of playing has its own fixed point. Improving does not destroy the target; it moves you to a new one, further up. And the guarantee is that the new one is never lower than the last. The critic spends its entire life chasing something that keeps running away from it — always upward, and always toward a ceiling that was there before any of this began.

## Part Five: Two Powers

Everything so far has been one engine, and an ancient one. The ratchet has been turning in vertebrate brains for something like half a billion years — that is the whole force of Bennett's story, and it is why the machinery turns up in a fish. But the mountains that have gone up around us were not raised by it alone. What changed recently is not that anybody discovered the ratchet. It is that there was finally a second engine to couple it to.

That second engine has been standing just off the page for the whole of the last section. Every time I said, unlike a system handed an answer key, that was it, holding the door. It deserves more than a walk-on part.

It is gradient descent, and the whole of it fits in a paragraph. Imagine being dropped blindfolded onto a hillside in thick fog with no map, and told to get down. You reach out a foot; you feel whether the ground there is lower; if it is, you step to it. Then you do it again. That is the entire algorithm. It is almost insultingly simple, it requires you to understand nothing whatever about the hill, and it will reliably walk you to the bottom of whatever valley you happen to have landed in.

Now put that at the heart of a neural network. Start with a pile of data and a model that is nothing but random numbers in a mess. Define the loss — the gap between what the model produced and what was wanted — and treat that as your height on the hill. Nudge every weight slightly in whatever direction lowers it, and repeat until the electricity runs out. Backpropagation is the trick that lets you feel the slope in every direction at once rather than one foot at a time, which is the only reason any of this is feasible in a space of a billion dimensions instead of two. And that is all of it. That is what trained the attention layers; it is how a network learns language, or a face; it is the whole of modern pretraining, and everything that has come out of a chat window came out of that loop.

There is no bootstrap anywhere in it. Nothing hauls on its own laces. The ground does not move, and something walks patiently downhill toward an answer that was already there.

Which is where I did not expect to end up, because that engine was in the bedroom too. Clip one corner a hair tighter, come out fractionally quicker, keep the tighter line — nudge, keep what improves, repeat. That ceiling I mentioned a moment ago, the fastest lap the cartridge would permit, is exactly what every scraped hundredth was a step down toward, and it never moved an inch. The pale kart beside me did. A fixed floor below and a rising target behind, on the same lap, and a boy noticing neither.

They are not the same power. Descent walks toward something that was already there and does not care how the walk goes; it can be very hard to do well, and large training runs come off the rails often enough, but it cannot talk itself into a mistake. The ratchet has no valley; it lifts the floor beneath its own feet, and it is the strange and delicate one, the one that needs a thin line of outside truth and a difference in speeds even to hold together.

Deep reinforcement learning — the actor and the critic no longer looking answers up in a table but computing them with a neural network — is the two of them married. Descent supplies machinery that can judge a position it has never seen before; the bootstrap supplies the ever-rising target for it to walk toward. AlphaGo Zero runs the same principle on different parts. Its improvement comes from a search rather than a critic: the machine looks ahead through millions of continuations, guided by the network's own hunches, and arrives somewhere better than the network could have reached alone. Then the tree is discarded. Nothing is kept unless the network is trained toward what the search found — which is plain descent, target fixed, walking downhill. Improvement discovered by search, stored by descent; and the better network makes the next search stronger still. Self-play as an upward ratchet, each rung laid down by descent. The reasoning models are the same marriage carried up into language: a transformer laid down by descent over the whole written world, then lifted by a reinforcement loop that rewards the chains of reasoning which turn out to arrive somewhere checkable. Neither alone gets you here. Both together — with enough silicon to run on and enough electricity to burn — is why the Himalayas, which are only what happens when two things that were travelling separately collide, and the sole remaining direction is up.

None of this was available to a thirteen-year-old. I could not have told you what a value function was, or that there was such a thing as a moving target, or that anything at all was being manufactured. I pressed a button and watched a number. What I noticed was that the number came down when I took one particular corner differently, and that the pale kart showed me where it had come down. That is the whole of what I knew. It took thirty years and somebody else's book to tell me what I had been looking at.

What makes it worth mentioning at all is that it was a glimpse of both. Not only the ratchet, but the descent as well — the two streams that had to meet before any of the last decade could happen, both of them running in one bedroom, on the same lap, and neither of them noticed. That is not a claim to have known anything. It is a claim about how much of this is sitting in plain sight, in a toy, waiting for somebody to hand you the words.

## Part Six: It Was Always Games

One last thing, which looks like a footnote and I don't think is one.

In 1969 a programmer at Bell Labs named Ken Thompson had written a game called Space Travel — a little simulation of the solar system, in which you flew a ship about and tried to set it down on the various moons. The trouble was that every go cost something like seventy-five dollars of the lab's internal money. So he found a neglected mini-computer in a corner with a good display hanging off it, and set about moving his game onto it, which turned out to mean writing from scratch, in assembly, a floating-point package, the shapes of the characters on the screen, and a debugger — all of it prepared on another machine and walked over on paper tape. He did not build an operating system in order to play a game. But the game was what took him to that corner of the room and made him fluent in the machine sitting in it, and it was there, shortly after, that he wrote the first version of Unix. Most of the internet now runs on its descendants.

Twenty-odd years later two friends from Cambridge were making video games for a living: Demis Hassabis, who had been designing them since he was a teenager, and David Silver, who was his lead programmer. Silver left for Alberta to take a doctorate under Richard Sutton — on temporal difference learning, turned loose on the game of Go — and thanked, in his acknowledgements, both Sutton and the man who had built the backgammon program. The lineage is not a matter of ideas drifting about in the air. It is people, thanked by name, on a page. Some years later the two friends were back in the same building, in a company whose whole method was to build things that got good at games. Getting good at games turned out not to be a small thing. Watching them do it, and being frightened by it, is why much of the rest of the industry exists at all.

None of which is coincidence, and none of it is quite a joke either. Think about what this machinery actually needs in order to run at all. It needs repetition, because it climbs in tiny increments, each one measured against its own last attempt. And it needs an honest verdict from somewhere outside itself — one number nobody can argue with — or the chain of guesses curls up and begins certifying its own mistakes. Very little in ordinary life supplies both. A game supplies both by construction: a small world you can enter again, and again, and again, and a score at the end of it that will not be sweet-talked. So a game is where the ratchet — an actor getting better, a critic re-grading it, the standard rising underneath them both — has room to turn.

But that is only half of it, and the other half is why anyone was in the room to begin with. Remember what the burst did when the light came on. It moved. It left the juice and attached itself to the promise of the juice. Which is a peculiar fact about you: you are not paid when you win. You are paid at the moment winning becomes likely. And the cells doing the paying are the cells doing the teaching. Anticipation and instruction are one mechanism, felt from the inside and measured from the outside. The reason a game teaches you and the reason you cannot put it down are the same reason.

So a boy gave a whole summer to one lap of one track and never once experienced it as work, and he got better, and he never knew why either thing was happening. Both were the same small loop, doing what it does. It is still doing it — in the machines now, at a scale that has raised mountains, and in you, this second, quietly.

None of which had happened yet. That summer, at IBM, a physicist named Gerald Tesauro sat watching TD-Gammon — the backgammon program from a few paragraphs back, the one that taught itself by Sutton's method — climb to a level he had not believed a machine could reach, and nobody had told the boy. The monkeys would not give up the rest of it for another four years. AlphaGo was twenty-three summers off. That April, in a Denny's in San Jose, Jensen Huang and two friends had founded a company to build better graphics chips for video games, which, given everything above, may be the least surprising sentence in this essay. The whole internet had about fourteen million people on it, and had begun that year with fewer than a hundred websites.

And in a bedroom with the curtains still half drawn, a record bought that June was playing for the hundredth time, and the sparkling Rhodes at the top of "Blow Your Mind" came out of a small speaker while he lined up one more lap.

He had no idea. He was just going round again.
