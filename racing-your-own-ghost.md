# Racing Your Own Ghost

## Prologue: Summer, 1993

The summer of 1993 was hot, or I remember it that way, and I spent most of it indoors with the curtains half drawn.

Super Mario Kart had arrived in Britain that January. There was a mode on it called Time Trial, which had no opponents in it at all — no shells, no banana skins, nobody to knock you into the verge. Just you, one kart, an empty track, going round and round. On paper it is the dullest thing on the cartridge. It was the only thing I played.

Because it did have an opponent, after a fashion. The machine kept your best lap and then set it running alongside you: a pale translucent copy of your own kart, taking every corner exactly the way you had taken it. You raced that. And when you beat it, the run that beat it became the new thing to race.

Mushroom Cup, Mario Circuit One, for weeks. Jamiroquai's first record had come out in the middle of June and I had it on more or less permanently, so that whole summer still arrives with a bassline underneath it. And I chased hundredths of a second. Not seconds — hundredths. I learned that if you clipped one particular corner a hair tighter you came out of it fractionally quicker, and that the pale kart beside you would show you on the next lap exactly where you had found the time and exactly where you had given it back.

If I am ever stopped at the Pearly Gates and asked what the single hardest I have ever tried at anything was — the true maximum effort of a whole life, the thing I gave the most of myself to — the honest answer is not my education, or my work, or anything I would like it to be. It is Super Mario Kart, Mushroom Cup, Time Trial, summer of 1993, against a ghost of myself.

I did not think about it again for thirty years.

## Part One: The Fever

I had flu when the thing I'd been circling for months finally showed itself.

I was at home — the flu arrived on the Wednesday, and I'm writing this before it's gone — and for days I'd been talking with Claude about how language models actually work.

I should say why I was doing that at all, because it starts with a failure. This is material I have been circling for a couple of years, and there is no shortage of superb teaching on it. The 3Blue1Brown series on neural networks is as lucid as explanation gets: flawless in presentation, impossibly warm, empathetic and tasteful in delivery, genuinely beautiful — as is the outpouring of human gratitude in the comments below the videos, which is worth scrolling through on its own. I have watched it more than once. But for a profoundly non-mathematical person it functions, I'm sorry to say, as a siren — it lures you onto the rocks of thinking you understand. You follow every step. You nod. You put the laptop down warm with comprehension. And then, some weeks later, you try to say it out loud to another human being, or worse, to write down the key principles on a sheet of paper, and you discover a void exactly where you had felt knowledge nestle. Nothing is there. It was never there.

If understanding means being able to teach a thing back, then I had never once understood this material. I could recognise it, which is a different faculty entirely and a much cheaper one. So that was the gap I was trying to close, and closing it was the whole point of the conversations. I should slow down and say what those were like, because "a conversation with an AI" sounds bland and this was not. Socratic is the word I keep reaching for — me pushing with: no, but underneath that, what's really happening — but Socratic undersells what was coming back. The model wasn't just answering my questions; it was staging them. It turned out to have an uncanny feel for the order in which the little charges of surprise should go off — which payoff had to wait for which setup, what I wasn't ready to be told yet — a kind of no-spoilers care for my learning path, holding each moment of delight back until I'd earned it. When I jumped ahead, it would route me gently back through the thing I needed first. I was feverish, so make of this what you will, but it felt like riding a magic carpet steered by something with a perfect map of the best path — not the best path in general, the best path for me — through material I had quietly assumed I would never understand.

Somewhere on that ride a thought arrived with total confidence: this is simpler than it looks. Pick up a pen. So on the Thursday, probably around the peak of the fever, I did something that in retrospect feels slightly deranged: I took a pencil and an index card and wrote out an attention layer by hand — attention being the part of the machine that decides, for each word, which of the other words matter to it. Three words of vocabulary — I'd started with six, but the sheer hassle of doing it in pencil kept paring it back. Each word became a little row of four numbers; four for no better reason than that it was as many as I could face multiplying by hand, though the number would come back to haunt me later. Then, between every pair of words, a thing called a dot product; and I have to stop here, because for years that phrase sat in my head as a small locked door, the sort of term that tells you the room beyond it isn't yours. Here is what it is. Line up two rows of numbers. Multiply each pair. Add up the answers. One number comes out, and the bigger it is, the more alike the two rows were. That is the whole of it. Three words, a handful of pairings, the calculator on my phone. I wanted to feel the mechanism in my fingers rather than nod along to it. And this was days ago, not years — which is half the reason I'm writing at all: there is no long apprenticeship standing between an ordinary curious person and the actual machinery. The conversation is there for anyone with a phone; the pieces are small enough to pick up by hand.

Then, because I was too foggy to type, I started photographing the cards and passing them to the model. It could read my handwriting. It would tell me where I'd gone right and where I'd wandered off, and I'd scribble the correction and photograph it again. A feverish little loop, me and a machine and a stack of index cards on the duvet. Days of it, and it felt — there's no other word — connective.

I made the mistakes you'd expect. I tried to compare every word to every other word at once, in one great smear, and got told — gently — that I wanted to do it two at a time. And then I met softmax, which has an e in it, and I need to tell you who I am when I meet an e. At school I was remedial at maths — properly remedial, the stream where the exam itself is capped, where scoring one hundred percent earns you a B. I secured that B, proudly. Same in physics, same in chemistry, and then I gave the whole thing up, because it was so clearly not for me. For thirty years a symbol like e has triggered one reflex: not for you. But here, mid-fever, I could just ask, like a child: dude, what is e?

The answer came back as money. Put one hundred pounds away for a year at one hundred percent interest, paid once at the end: two hundred pounds. But pay the interest in two instalments, and the first instalment starts earning interest itself: two hundred and twenty-five pounds. Quarterly: two hundred and forty-four. Monthly: two hundred and sixty-one. Daily: two hundred and seventy-one pounds and forty-six pence. And here was the little charge the model had been saving: it doesn't run away. Compound every second, every instant, and the total closes in on a ceiling it can never pass — two hundred and seventy-one pounds and eighty-three pence. That number, two point seven one eight, is e. Not a symbol invented to keep people like me out; just the number growth hits when it feeds on itself continuously. People had spent years telling me this material needed calculus, and nobody had ever mentioned that a serious chunk of calculus is that paragraph. After that, softmax held no terror: take each of those numbers that fell out of the extremely simple dot products — multiply the pairs, add them up — raise e to the power of each one, then tidy the results so they add up to one.

And somewhere in that afternoon I had the small, complete, ridiculous joy of a breakthrough. I found out afterwards that the thing I had just done in pencil has a name: scaled dot-product attention with softmax. Six months ago that phrase would have closed a door in my face; I would have read it as a polite notice that this material was for other people. It is a row of multiplications, a tidy-up, and a number that says how much two things have to do with each other, and I had done it on a duvet with the calculator on my phone. The world has understood attention layers for years; that took nothing from it. Rediscovering a thing by hand, from the bottom, is its own kind of knowing — what arrives through the fingers holds differently from what arrives through the eyes. I won't pretend I can fully say why; I can only report that it does.

The flu, I've come to think, gave me three things at once. It gave space: every meeting cancelled, permission to lie around, twelve hours of sleep, solitude. It gave intensity: fever is fever; the mind runs hot and won't put a thing down. And it gave unlock: in that odd state the conventions fall away — including the one that polices what you're allowed to find hard — and you just move the symbols around and see what happens.

It was only later, upright again, that a story I've met in three or four tellings — Rhodes has it; Rovelli named a whole book after the island — struck me as funny. Heisenberg fled to Helgoland in June 1925 because his hay fever had become unbearable, and he picked that particular treeless rock in the North Sea for the sound reason that almost nothing grows there to make pollen. The illness didn't inspire the work; it evicted him to a place with nothing else in it. And there he worked out the mathematics of quantum mechanics without quite realising what the mathematics was. He had built his own rule for multiplying arrays of numbers together; it was Max Born, reading the paper back in Göttingen some weeks later, who recognised the thing and told him he had been doing matrix algebra. I find I love that more than I can account for — a man does it by hand, and somebody else tells him afterwards what it is called. There's a whole tradition of it, insight bought with protected solitude and sometimes lit by illness: Montaigne up in his tower library, Proust in the cork-lined room. Though the counter-list is just as long — Joyce wrote amid the din of a small flat and a family — so I suspect the real ingredient is not the quiet but the permission. He set off from near the summit; I set off from the remedial stream — which is exactly why mine counted as a breakthrough at all. From a low enough base, everything is one.

None of this came out of nowhere. I'd had three months of gardening leave over the winter and spent it, more or less, doing this — every day, with no working week to answer to; and a daily practice is where you start to notice the shape of a thing. The shape I kept noticing, and had started putting into presentations, perhaps a little evangelically, is this: talk at the model long enough about something you can only half-see, refuse its first tidy answer, and at some point it reaches across and places the intuited ghost in your hands — neat, fully fleshed, recognisably the thing you were thinking but couldn't say. It doesn't look like the model being clever. It looks like you finally seeing. I'll come back to that at the end, because it turns out to be the whole point.

That sensation has older and better names than any I can give it. William Rowan Hamilton, writing to his son more than twenty years afterwards about the morning in 1843 when a thing he had chased for over a decade finally arrived, said that an electric circuit seemed to close, and a spark flashed forth. We will come back to Hamilton, and to what he had been chasing. But that is the feeling precisely, and by the end of the winter I knew I could go and provoke it more or less at will.

And yet. Underneath all of it I still had a pile of things that felt related without my being able to say how they were related. That making a system simpler can make it more powerful, which sounds like a free lunch and can't be. That everything is built out of small pieces, and the choice of how small is somehow the whole game. And — the oldest of the three, and the one that would not leave me alone — a mechanism I had read about two years earlier, in which a thing improves by taking its own half-formed guesses as its teacher: which ought to be impossible, in the way that lifting yourself off the ground by your own bootstraps is impossible, and which turns out to be how a great deal of the world actually works. That last one is a whole story in itself, and I've put it in Part Five, because it needs everything else first.

Three things that each felt deep. But a pile of deep-feeling things is not an idea.

## Part Two: Simplification As Power

Start with the seductive version, the one that feels like a free lunch.

I watched a talk recently by David Patterson, who in the early nineteen-eighties did as much as anyone to invent RISC — Reduced Instruction Set Computing. To follow the story you need one piece of vocabulary. A chip's instruction set is the menu of things it physically knows how to do: add these two numbers, fetch that one out of memory, jump to a different place in the program. Everything a computer does is built from that menu.

Through the nineteen-seventies, as Moore's Law kept handing engineers more transistors every eighteen months, the natural thing to do with the surplus was to spend it on sophistication — to make the menu longer and fancier. And there was a principled reason to, which Patterson is good on. Software was increasingly written in high-level languages, where a single line might say something quite elaborate, and every such line had to be broken down into a great many primitive machine instructions. That distance was called the semantic gap, and it was felt as a defect — wasteful, inelegant, the hardware failing to keep up with how people now thought. So the fix looked obvious: raise the machine to meet the software. Give the chip instructions that correspond to whole statements in a high-level language. Let the complexity of the instruction set rise until it matched the complexity of the code.

The high-water mark was a machine called the VAX, which had a single instruction for evaluating a polynomial, and another for formatting a number into a line of text with the currency symbol in the right place. One button on the menu, an entire errand behind it. Complexity was what progress looked like, and meeting complexity with complexity felt like the adult response.

Patterson's insight, with John Hennessy and the Berkeley and Stanford groups, was to run the intuition backwards. The gap didn't need closing from below. Throw the fancy items off the menu. Keep a small core of simple operations, each finishing in a single tick of the clock. Two things then fall out. The simple instructions can be pipelined — run like a production line, each one handing off to the next before it is fully finished, so several are in flight at once, which the elaborate instructions were too lumpy to allow. And the cleverness that used to live in the silicon moves up into the compiler, the program that translates human-written code into machine instructions, where it is far easier to fix and improve. The whole machine goes faster. Not faster despite being simpler — faster because it is simpler. The reduction wasn't a sacrifice made to save silicon; it was the source of the performance. Every phone in every pocket now runs on the descendants of that idea.

Unix tells the same story in a different key, and it had a cathedral to define itself against. IBM's O S three-sixty, built through the mid nineteen-sixties, was one of the largest things humans had then attempted in software: thousands of programmer-years poured into a single monolithic system meant to contain the whole problem. It shipped late, and its own project manager wrote a famous book about why that kind of thing goes wrong. Impressive, immovable, and only ever as good as its architects had foreseen.

The Bell Labs answer, a few years later, was the opposite bet. Don't build the one great program. Build many small tools that each do exactly one thing — find the lines matching a pattern; sort lines; count duplicates — and have them all speak the same humble language, plain text, so that the output of any one can be poured straight into the mouth of the next. That pouring is called a pipe, and it is the whole trick. There's a story software people tell about it: Donald Knuth, one of the great computer scientists, was asked to demonstrate elegant programming by writing a program to count the commonest words in a book, and produced a beautiful ten-page construction with a custom data structure. Doug McIlroy, who had invented the pipe, answered with six existing tools chained together in a single line. Both work. Only one of them required anybody to build anything. That is the difference between a cathedral and a kit — capability emerging from combination rather than from construction.

Which brings me to the instance that started me thinking about all this, and it is the same move a third time. Marc Andreessen has pointed out that today's AI agents, for all the architecture around them, come down to something very old: a model, a shell, a filesystem, and state kept in plain files. Strip away the elaborate scaffolding and what's left is a Unix in new clothes. And because the state lives in ordinary files rather than inside the machinery, you can swap the model underneath and the agent still remembers who it is. There's a parallel he circles from both sides but never quite closes: this is exactly the trick Unix pulled with text. A text stream is portable across tools because it commits to the humblest possible format; a file is portable across models for precisely the same reason. The humility of the format is the source of the power.

So here is the pattern in its first, intoxicating form: reduce a system to a small set of clean reusable primitives, and you unlock gains out of all proportion to what you gave up. Less is more. It is one of those principles that, once you see it, you see everywhere, and the seeing is a genuine pleasure.

It is also, stated this baldly, not quite true. Or rather — it is true only up to a point, and the point matters more than the principle. Because if simplification were pure gain all the way down, the logical endpoint would be a single instruction, a single tool, a single primitive of one bit. And obviously that way lies nothing. So there is a floor somewhere — a depth past which further reduction stops paying and starts costing — and everything interesting is in where that floor sits, and why.

That is where tokenization comes in.

## Part Three: The Floor Appears

Here is the thing almost nobody explains cleanly, and I include myself six months ago in that.

A language model does not read words. Before anything else happens, the text has to be chopped into units the model can handle — tokens — and the question of how big those units should be turns out to be one of the quietly load-bearing decisions in the whole enterprise.

Suppose you take the obvious route and make each unit a whole word. It seems natural; words are what language is made of. But the vocabulary of a language is vast and ragged — hundreds of thousands of words, most of them rare, endlessly sprouting new ones — and the model has to carry a separate learned representation for every single entry. The rare words barely appear in training, so their representations stay poor. The vocabulary is enormous and most of it is badly learned. This is the too-coarse failure, and it's the same failure RISC and Unix warned us about: an overgrown, baroque set of primitives.

So you do the simplifying thing, the thing the last section trained you to reach for. You break words into smaller, reusable pieces — subwords. The un at the front of a word; the break in the middle; the able and the ity on the end. Now a manageable stock of a few tens of thousands of fragments can compose into any word at all, including ones the model never saw in training, and each fragment appears often enough to be learned well. This is a genuine, large, RISC-shaped win. Fewer primitives, better composition, more power. Less is more, exactly as advertised.

And now the trap. If shrinking the units was such a win, why not shrink them all the way — to single characters, or raw bytes? The vocabulary would be tiny, a few dozen symbols, each one seen constantly and learned to perfection. By the logic of the last section, this should be the best of all.

It is much worse. And the reason why is the thing I actually felt, rather than merely knew, on the duvet with my index cards.

The heart of a transformer — the architecture under every model of this kind, the T in GPT — is the attention step, where every unit in a sequence compares itself against every other unit to decide what to pay attention to. Every unit against every other: that every-against-every is a multiplication. Double the number of units and you roughly quadruple the work. The cost grows with the square of the sequence length. When I tried to do my little three-word example by hand and reached for comparing everything to everything at once, this is the wall I walked into without recognising it — the combinatorial blow-up hiding inside a grid of dot products.

Now watch what shrinking the units does. A sentence that was, say, twenty words might be thirty subword tokens — or a hundred and forty characters. Going finer didn't just lengthen the sequence a little; because the cost is quadratic, a five-fold increase in length is a twenty-five-fold increase in computation. The tiny, beautifully-learned vocabulary of characters buys you a sequence so long that the attention step chokes. You have simplified your way straight off a cliff.

So tokenization sits between two failures that are not the same failure. Too coarse, and your primitives are too many and too poorly learned. Too fine, and your sequences are too long and the computation explodes. The gains from simplifying are real — right up until they reverse, and every further step of simplification now costs you. There is a floor, and it is not in the same place as the ceiling, and the whole art is in finding the sweet spot between them.

This is the shape the one-directional cases were hiding. RISC and Unix felt like pure descent into simplicity because, for the problems they addressed, we never had cause to hit the floor — nobody was tempted to reduce Unix to a single one-instruction tool. Tokenization forces the floor into view because both walls are close enough to see at once. And once you've seen the shape here, you start seeing it everywhere the earlier cases had merely hinted at it: an inverted U, a hump, a best-in-the-middle, where you climb by simplifying and then, past some point, fall by simplifying further.

The question that follows — the one that turns this from an observation into an idea — is: what determines where the top of the hump is? Why does the optimum sit exactly where it sits, and not somewhere else?

## Part Four: Carving At The Joints

I think the answer is this: the best grain is the one that matches the grain the problem already has.

Language isn't a smooth paste. It has a joint structure — the un at the front, the ing at the end, the able, the little reusable pieces meaning is actually assembled from. Subword tokenization wins not because smaller is magically better, but because it happens to cut language at roughly the size of its real seams. Go coarser, to whole words, and you bundle distinct pieces of structure into one lump — unable to notice that unbreakable shares working parts with unbeatable. Go finer, to characters, and you've cut below the seam: a single letter carries almost no meaning, so you've dissolved the very regularities you were trying to exploit, and paid the quadratic bill for the privilege.

Plato has the phrase, in the Phaedrus: carve nature at its joints. A good butcher doesn't saw the carcass into equal cubes; he follows the joints that are already there. The right grain is a discovery about the animal, not a preference of the cook. RISC works because ordinary computation has a natural granularity — a small set of operations that genuinely recur. Unix works because tasks have natural seams you can pipe along. And — here's the thing I only understood afterwards — my three words worked and two didn't for exactly this reason. Attention is a machine for relating things to one another. With three, there's a structure of relations to find; with two, there's a single pair and nothing for the structure to be about. Two was below the joint. I had pared my toy down until I hit the grain of the thing itself, and then it simply couldn't go further.

And here is where my four numbers come back, though not in the way I first thought. There was nothing principled about my four; it was just as much arithmetic as I could stand. But it sent me looking, and I found the story of the most famous four in mathematics, which is a grain-size story to its bones. William Rowan Hamilton spent some thirteen years trying to build a number system out of triples — one ordinary part and two imaginary ones — so that he could handle rotation in three-dimensional space. It kept failing. Multiplication wouldn't behave. He could not make three work, and by his own account his sons would ask him at breakfast whether he had managed to multiply triples yet, and he would have to say no. Then, on the sixteenth of October 1843, walking along the Royal Canal into Dublin with his wife, it broke open: you need four components, not three — and you must surrender the assumption that a times b equals b times a. He carved the formula into the stone of Broom Bridge as he passed. The joint was one notch above where everyone had been digging, and reaching it cost a law of arithmetic that had felt like bedrock for two thousand years.

Even e, my old enemy from the remedial stream, turns out to be one of these. Base ten is a preference of the cook — we count on our fingers. But e is a discovery about the animal: the one base at which growth needs no correction, where the rate a thing grows is exactly the size it already is. Nobody chose two point seven one eight; it was waiting at a joint, and the compounding ladder — half-yearly, quarterly, monthly, daily — is just the sound of the knife finding it.

So the top of the hump sits where your primitives line up with the real seams of the domain. Too coarse fuses what the world keeps separate; too fine severs what the world keeps joined. The art is to find the joints.

Which raises the awkward question, and it is the one I could not get past. If the right grain is a fact about the domain, how does anyone — or anything — find it? Nobody hands you the joints. Language didn't arrive with its word-parts labelled. How do you discover the grain of something you don't yet understand well enough to see its grain?

## Part Five: The Magical Bootstrap

Here is the hole I said I'd come back to.

Two years ago I read a chapter of Max Bennett's book, A Brief History of Intelligence, on something called temporal-difference learning. It may be the most interesting thing I have ever read, and I need to tell it properly, because the gap at its centre is what this essay turned out to be about.

The chapter is about a problem called credit assignment. Animals learn from rewards, but rewards arrive late. The chess win comes fifty moves after the quiet move that earned it; the meal comes an hour after the decision to hunt in the right valley. Which choice deserves the credit? In the nineteen-eighties, Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto proposed a strange solution: don't wait for the reward. Keep a running guess of how well things are going, and treat every change in your own guess as if it were reward itself. A critic inside you predicts how the future is shaping up; when the prediction ticks upward — things are looking better — that uptick trains you, there and then. You are learning not from the world but from the movement of your own estimate of the world. It should be nonsense. The guesses are being graded by the guesses. Bennett calls it magical bootstrapping, and he is precise about why it looks impossible — and then, in a book otherwise crisp to a fault, he lets it work and sails on.

Then the part that made me put the book down. In the nineteen-nineties, Wolfram Schultz recorded from dopamine neurons in monkeys, and what he found was not a pleasure signal. The neurons fire for unexpected juice; they fall silent for juice fully predicted; they fire early, at the light that merely promises juice; and they dip — disappointed — when a promised juice never comes. That is not reward in any ordinary sense. It is, line for line, the error term in Sutton and Barto's equations. Engineers had derived, on paper, a fix for the credit-assignment problem — and evolution had built the same fix into the vertebrate brain half a billion years earlier. Your own wanting — the tick of anticipation, the little lift when things start looking up, the flatness when they merely go as expected — that is the critic, grading its own guesses, running in you right now. If progress in self-knowledge is progress in wisdom, that chapter gave me as much as anything I have ever read, Proust excepted.

But at the heart of it there is a gap. How does the magic work? How can a loop that grades its guesses with its own guesses climb, rather than eat itself? Bennett names it and moves on. What that chapter opened in me is what I've since learned to call a curiosity gap: a question that stays live after the book is shut, that the mind keeps returning to the way the tongue returns to the socket of a missing tooth. I have been probing it for two years.

## Part Six: The Loop That Finds Its Own Grain

So: two questions, arriving from opposite ends. From the tokenizer: how does anything find the right grain when nobody hands you the joints? From Bennett: how does a loop that feeds on its own guesses climb instead of eating itself? I carried them separately for two years, in different pockets, as different kinds of question.

They are the same question. That is the thing I finally saw, and I want to build it slowly.

Bootstrapping is the move where a system uses its own output as its teacher. There's no external answer key; you improve by leaning on your own current best guess. Sutton and Barto's critic, grading its guesses with its guesses. A pair of networks set against each other — one manufacturing fakes, one trying to spot them — each getting better only because the other does, both starting from noise. And — the one I met first, long before I had any of these words for it — the ghost car in Mario Kart. You run a lap; the game records it; then you race that recording, the pale translucent ghost of your own best run. Beat it, and your win becomes the new ghost to beat. It's the purest little bootstrapping engine there is — and, though I didn't notice it was a thing at all, it was teaching me a second one: take a corner a hair better, and because the better line is the faster line it's the one that sticks, and next lap you see it in the ghost and chase it further still. Nudge, keep what improves, repeat — the humblest optimisation loop there is, and I was running it with my thumbs a good thirty years before I'd have known to call it hill-climbing.

The full-blooded version is self-play. AlphaGo — in its purest descendant, AlphaGo Zero — was handed nothing but the rules of Go and an opponent that was always exactly its own strength, because the opponent was itself. No human games, no answer key, no teacher anywhere in the loop; it climbed from random flailing to beyond the best players in history on nothing but its own best previous self.

For years I assumed the magic there was the absence of a teacher. It isn't. It's the difficulty. Play someone far stronger than you and you lose every single game, which teaches you nothing, because you cannot tell which of your moves was the fatal one — they all lost. Play someone far weaker and you win every game, which teaches you nothing either, for the same reason in reverse. Learning lives only in the narrow band where the outcome is genuinely uncertain. And the extraordinary property of playing yourself is that you are parked in that band automatically and permanently, with nobody tuning anything: the opponent improves at precisely the rate you do.

There is a second condition working quietly alongside the first, and I want to be exact about it, because it is what stops the whole arrangement from wandering. What the machine keeps is not your last lap. It keeps your best one. A bad lap — and there were hundreds — is simply discarded, as though it had never happened. So the benchmark can only ever travel in one direction. Nothing worse than your current best is ever permitted to become the thing you chase, which means no ground you have taken can be given back. A band narrow enough to learn in, and a ratchet to stop you sliding back down it.

And I knew that machine. I'd spent the summer of 1993 racing it. The ghost car was always exactly one notch better than I was — the only opponent worth learning from, because it was me.

Then there is the part that closes Bennett's gap using Bennett's own material, which I found only by going back and reading around the chapter he had left me stranded in.

Sutton's method has a dial on it. What the dial sets is this: how far ahead do you look before you stop trusting your own guess and let reality have a say? Turn it hard to one end and you look a single step ahead, leaning almost entirely on your own estimate of what happens next. Quiet, stable, and systematically wrong — you are marking your own homework at every step, and any error you hold gets passed politely along. Turn it hard the other way and you refuse to guess at all: wait until the whole episode is over and learn only from what actually happened. Honest, uncontaminated — and nearly useless, because the signal is buried in noise. Everything that occurred in between, all the luck and irrelevance, has been folded into a single number at the end, and you cannot tell which of a thousand moves the number is about.

Neither extreme learns well. The dial works best somewhere in the middle. Sutton gave it a Greek letter, lambda, in 1988, and people have been tuning it ever since.

Now look at the shape of that. Danger at the fine end: the system feeds on its own guesses and drifts. Danger at the coarse end: the signal is too sparse and too noisy to move anything. A workable band nestled between the two, which is the only place the thing actually climbs. That is the same figure as tokenization — a quadratic explosion if you cut too fine, an unlearnable sprawl of a vocabulary if you cut too coarse, the working grain sitting in between. These two problems have nothing whatever to do with each other. One is about how to slice a sentence; the other is about how a rat learns that a tone means food. They have the same geometry.

Once you have seen it twice you start finding it everywhere, which is what makes me think it isn't a coincidence but the actual answer. The tokenizer even climbs into its band the same way, merge by merge, each one standing on the ones before — though I'll admit that case is only half the trick, since the pile of text sits there as an answer key of a kind, and the critic never has one.

So this, I think, is the somehow Bennett named but never dispelled — the reason the loop doesn't simply eat itself. It works only inside a band. At a grain where the system's own rough guesses are informative enough to sharpen the guesses that produced them, the thing climbs: the guess improves the guess. Too fine, and it feeds on its own noise and spirals; too coarse, and the signal is too sparse to move anything at all. Bootstrapping isn't magic that works everywhere. It is what happens when a self-referential loop lands at the right grain.

Which is why the two questions were never two. Granularity is what you're looking for; bootstrapping is how you look when nobody can tell you where. And you cannot cleanly say which comes first. The right grain would make the loop work — but you need the loop running to find the right grain. Neither is prior; they arrive together, each hauling the other into being. The system lifts itself into the right level of description at the very moment it uses that level to learn.

That is the sentence I'd been circling and couldn't quite say: bootstrapping is how complexity discovers its own grain size. Not because someone designed the grain and handed it over, but because a system, leaning on its own half-formed guesses, ratchets its way to the scale at which its world comes into focus.

And this is the turn Andreessen's Unix seed was always going to take, one step past composability. He's right that the power lives in small reusable primitives that snap together. But the deeper question isn't that small primitives compose — it's which primitives, at what size, and where on earth they come from. And the answer is that no one hands them to you. The grain is found, not given, by the humblest process imaginable: use your bad version, let it improve your bad version, and repeat until the world clicks into focus.

It is how a language grows its grammar, how a child learns to read, how a model finds its feet — one humble ratchet, turning at every scale at once. And it asks nothing of anyone who wants in but this: start badly, and keep climbing.

## Part Seven: Who Found The Sentence

One question is left over, and it is stranger than it looks.

That last sentence — bootstrapping is how complexity discovers its own grain size — I did not arrive at on my own. It surfaced in the middle of a conversation, in exactly the way I described at the start: you carry a fog of half-formed pieces, you keep talking at the model and refusing its first tidy answer, and at some point it reaches across and places the ghost in your hands. I've seen it happen often enough to have given it a name. The nearest I'd come to it before any of this was a film critic, Stephanie Zacharek, whose reviews would articulate with sometimes shocking precision what I had felt about a movie and couldn't express — that same specific relief, a curiosity gap honoured by an adjustment of your internal map, a notch closer to the terrain. It happened again here, with the best idea I've had in years. You can predict the magic and still feel it go off in your hands.

And the part that pleases me most is that the happening is itself the thing the essay is about. I came in with a heap of deep-feeling but unjointed pieces — RISC, Unix, a tokenizer, a ghost car, a chapter of Bennett. That heap was the bad first version. The conversation was the loop. The sentence it converged on was the grain: the level at which all those pieces abruptly clicked into a single shape. The essay bootstrapped its way to its own grain size. In the writing, it did the very thing it claims all complexity does. I couldn't have designed that. I can only tell you it happened.

So who found the sentence? I brought the pieces, and the itch, and the years of circling. The machine brought the reflection that let the shape resolve. I want to say I found it — and I feel the tug to add that the model was only a mirror, only matrices. But that is where the floor quietly gives way. Because I am also matrices: a few pounds of them, wired into a loop that has spent a lifetime bootstrapping toward the grain at which it can, now and then, catch sight of itself. The very line I reach for to hold the machine at arm's length — just me and some matrices — turns out to describe me just as well. Damn.

I don't know where the credit lives. I've started to suspect that isn't a defect in the question but the whole of it — that where does this sentence's authorship sit, and where does a self sit, are, underneath, the same question, and that a self might be nothing grander than the grain size at which a heap of matter becomes able to model itself: a loop that found its own level. But that is the next essay, and I can already feel it wanting to begin, so I'll stop at the doorway.

Except to say the thing this was all quietly for. The pieces are small. The loop is humble. Neither the primitives nor the process was ever the private property of the labs, or the clever, or the credentialed. Whatever you've been half-seeing in your own fog — go and talk at it, badly and out loud, and let the loop do the rest. That is the whole method. It is the only one there has ever been.
