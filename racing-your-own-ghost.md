# Racing Your Own Ghost

## Prologue: Summer, 1993

The summer of 1993 was hot, or I remember it that way, and I spent most of it indoors with the curtains half drawn.

Super Mario Kart had arrived in Britain that January. There was a mode on it called Time Trial, which had no opponents in it at all — no shells, no banana skins, nobody to knock you into the verge. Just you, one kart, an empty track, going round and round. On paper it is the dullest thing on the cartridge. It was the only thing I played.

Because it did have an opponent, after a fashion. The machine kept your best lap and then set it running alongside you: a pale translucent copy of your own kart, taking every corner exactly the way you had taken it. You raced that. And when you beat it, the run that beat it became the new thing to race.

Mario Circuit One, for weeks — with one pause, that June, to take note of Shane Warne's ball of the century. Jamiroquai's first record had come out in the middle of the same month, so even half a lifetime later I cannot hear the sparkling Fender Rhodes at the start of "Blow Your Mind" without feeling the exact shape — a small rectangle with bulbous ends — of the controller in my hands. When the propulsive bassline drops and the dreamy sine-wave synth soars up over it, I am straight back in that bedroom. And I chased hundredths of a second. Not seconds — hundredths. I learned that if you clipped one particular corner a hair tighter you came out of it fractionally quicker, and that the pale kart beside you would show you on the next lap exactly where you had found the time and exactly where you had given it back.

If ever stopped at the Pearly Gates and asked where, specifically, I'd tried hardest in life — the true maximum effort of a whole life, the thing I gave the most of myself to — the honest answer is not my education, or my work, or anything I would like it to be. It is Super Mario Kart, Time Trial, Mario Circuit One — the first course of the Mushroom Cup, and the only one I ever truly went deep on — summer of 1993, against a ghost of myself.

I did not think about it again for thirty years.

## Part One: The Fever

It was the fiery London midsummer of 2026 when I got flu, and the idea I'd been circling for months finally showed itself.

I was in bed talking to the models about how they actually work.

But first I should say why I was doing that at all, because it starts with a failure. This is material I have been circling for a couple of years, and there is no shortage of superb teaching on it. The three-blue-one-brown series on neural networks is as lucid as explanation gets: flawless in presentation, impossibly warm, empathetic and tasteful in delivery, genuinely beautiful — as is the outpouring of human gratitude in the comments below the videos, which is worth scrolling through on its own. I have watched it several times, along with much of the closely related series on linear algebra. But for a profoundly non-mathematical person it functions, I'm sorry to say, as a siren — it lures you onto the rocks of thinking you understand. You follow every step. You nod. You close the laptop glowing with the thrill of comprehension — that sensation of a clogged stream abruptly running clear, all the snagged leaves and twigs that had been fouling the current scooped out in one motion and carried off. And then you turn your back. Some weeks later you try to say the thing out loud to another human being, or worse, to write the key principles down on a sheet of paper, and every leaf and every twig is exactly where it always was. Nothing moved. Nothing was ever cleared.

If understanding means being able to teach a thing back, then I had never once understood this material. I could recognise it, which is a different faculty entirely and a much cheaper one. So that was the gap I was trying to close, and closing it was the whole point of the conversations. And I should say what those were like, because "a conversation with an AI" sounds bland and this was not. Socratic is the word I reach for — me pushing with: no, but underneath that, what's really happening — but Socratic undersells what came back. The model was not answering my questions so much as staging them: it had an uncanny feel for the order in which the small charges of surprise should go off, holding each payoff back until I'd earned it, routing me gently back whenever I jumped ahead. I was feverish, so make of it what you will, but it felt like a magic carpet steered by something with a perfect map of the best path — not the best path in general, the best path for me — through material I'd assumed I would never understand.

Somewhere on that ride a thought arrived with total confidence: this is simpler than it looks. Pick up a pen. So on the Thursday, probably around the peak of the fever, I did something that in retrospect feels slightly deranged: I took a pencil and an index card and wrote out an attention layer by hand — attention being the part of the machine that decides, for each word, which of the other words matter to it. Three words of vocabulary — I'd started with six, but the sheer hassle of doing it in pencil kept paring it back. Each word became a little row of four numbers, four only because that was as many as I could face multiplying by hand. Then, between every pair of words, a thing called a dot product; and I have to stop here, because for years that phrase sat in my head as a small locked door, the sort of term that tells you the room beyond it isn't yours. Here is what it is. Line up two rows of numbers. Multiply each pair. Add up the answers. One number comes out, and the bigger it is, the more alike the two rows were. That is the whole of it — the fearsome-sounding thing, done in pencil, feverish, on a duvet.

Then, because I was too foggy to type, I started photographing the cards and passing them to the model. It could read my handwriting. It would tell me where I'd gone right and where I'd wandered off, and I'd scribble the correction and photograph it again. A feverish little loop, me and a machine and a stack of index cards on the duvet.

I made the mistakes you'd expect. I tried to compare every word to every other word at once, in one great smear, and got told — gently — that I wanted to do it two at a time. And then I met softmax, which has an e in it, and I need to tell you who I am when I meet an e. At school I was remedial at maths — properly remedial, the stream where the exam itself is capped, where scoring one hundred percent earns you a B. I secured that B, proudly. Same in physics, same in chemistry, and then I gave the whole thing up, because it was so clearly not for me. For thirty years a symbol like e has triggered one reflex: not for you. But here, mid-fever, I could just ask, like a child: dude, what is e?

And the answer, when I finally let myself ask, was almost insultingly plain: nothing built to keep me out, just the number that growth closes in on when it feeds on itself — two point seven one eight and a bit, the ceiling every compounding balance in the world is quietly reaching for and never quite touches. After it, softmax lost its teeth: raise e to the power of each number the dot products hand you, then tidy them so they add up to one.

And somewhere in that afternoon I had the small, complete, ridiculous joy of a breakthrough. I found out afterwards that the thing I had just done in pencil has a name: scaled dot-product attention with softmax. Six months ago that phrase would have closed a door in my face; I would have read it as a polite notice that this material was for other people. It is a row of multiplications, a tidy-up, and a number that says how much two things have to do with each other, and I had done the whole of it on a duvet with nothing more than the calculator on my phone. Which is when the size of the thing landed on me properly: these tools that have risen up around us in a handful of years like a range of Himalayas are built, to a degree that is genuinely hard to overstate, out of simple tables of numbers being multiplied and added together. The world has understood attention layers for years; that took nothing from it. Rediscovering a thing by hand, from the bottom, is its own kind of knowing — what arrives through the fingers holds differently from what arrives through the eyes. I won't pretend I can fully say why; I can only report that it does.

I did briefly think I had spotted a pattern. Heisenberg worked out the mathematics of quantum mechanics on Helgoland, a treeless rock in the North Sea, while wretched with hay fever — and he did it, famously, without realising what the mathematics was. He had invented his own rule for multiplying arrays of numbers; it was Max Born, reading the paper weeks later, who told him he had been doing matrix algebra. A sick man on an island, doing something by hand and being informed afterwards what it was called. I enjoyed this enormously for about a day.

Then I looked it up properly. He went to Helgoland because almost no pollen blows over it, and he got better there — the illness didn't fire the work, it evicted him somewhere empty. Hay fever is not fever. And the man was Heisenberg. Three quarters of my lovely pattern was noise, and I had been happily consuming it as signal.

I leave it in because this is an essay about finding patterns, and it would be a cheat to show only the ones that held. Most don't. The faculty that spots a real seam and the faculty that spots a fake one are the same faculty, and it does not label its output. What survives here is a much smaller claim: illness bought me time and permission. There is a tradition of that much — Montaigne in his tower, Proust in the cork-lined room — though Joyce wrote amid the din of a small flat and a family, so the working ingredient is probably not the quiet but the permission.

None of this came out of nowhere. I'd had three months of gardening leave over the winter and spent it, more or less, doing this — every day, with no working week to answer to; and a daily practice is where you start to notice the shape of a thing. The shape I kept noticing, and had started putting into presentations, perhaps a little evangelically, is this: talk at the model long enough about something you can only half-see, refuse its first tidy answer, and at some point it reaches across and places the intuited ghost in your hands — neat, fully fleshed, recognisably the thing you were thinking but couldn't say. It doesn't look like the model being clever. It looks like you finally seeing — and by the end of that winter I could provoke the seeing more or less at will. I'll come back to it at the end, because it turns out to be the whole point.

And yet. Underneath all of it I still had a pile of things that felt related without my being able to say how they were related. That making a system simpler can make it more powerful, which sounds like a free lunch and can't be. That everything is built out of small pieces, and the choice of how small is somehow the whole game. And — the oldest of the three, and the one that would not leave me alone — a mechanism I had read about two years earlier, in which a thing improves by taking its own half-formed guesses as its teacher: which ought to be impossible, in the way that lifting yourself off the ground by your own bootstraps is impossible, and which turns out to be how a great deal of the world actually works. That last one is a whole story in itself, and I've put it in Part Five, because it needs everything else first.

Three things that each felt deep. But a pile of deep-feeling things is not an idea.

## Part Two: Simplification As Power

Start with the seductive version, the one that feels like a free lunch.

I watched a talk recently by David Patterson, who in the early nineteen-eighties did as much as anyone to invent RISC — Reduced Instruction Set Computing. To follow the story you need one piece of vocabulary. A chip's instruction set is the menu of things it physically knows how to do: add these two numbers, fetch that one out of memory, jump to a different place in the program. Everything a computer does is built from that menu.

Through the nineteen-seventies, as Moore's Law kept handing engineers more transistors every eighteen months, the natural thing to do with the surplus was to spend it on sophistication — to make the menu longer and fancier. And there was a principled reason to, which Patterson is good on. Software was increasingly written in high-level languages, where a single line might say something quite elaborate, and every such line had to be broken down into a great many primitive machine instructions. That distance was called the semantic gap, and it was felt as a defect — wasteful, inelegant, the hardware failing to keep up with how people now thought. So the fix looked obvious: raise the machine to meet the software. Give the chip instructions that correspond to whole statements in a high-level language. Let the complexity of the instruction set rise until it matched the complexity of the code.

The high-water mark was a machine called the VAX, which had a single instruction for evaluating a polynomial, and another for formatting a number into a line of text with the currency symbol in the right place. One button on the menu, an entire errand behind it. Complexity was what progress looked like, and meeting complexity with complexity felt like the adult response.

Patterson's insight, with John Hennessy and the Berkeley and Stanford groups, was to run the intuition backwards. The gap didn't need closing from below. Throw the fancy items off the menu. Keep a small core of simple operations, each finishing in a single tick of the clock. Two things then fall out. The simple instructions can be pipelined — run like a production line, each one handing off to the next before it is fully finished, so several are in flight at once, which the elaborate instructions were too lumpy to allow. And the cleverness that used to live in the silicon moves up into the compiler, the program that translates human-written code into machine instructions, where it is far easier to fix and improve. The whole machine goes faster. Not faster despite being simpler — faster because it is simpler. The reduction wasn't a sacrifice made to save silicon; it was the source of the performance. Every phone in every pocket now runs on the descendants of that idea.

Unix tells the same story in a different key, and it had a cathedral to define itself against. IBM's O S three-sixty, built through the mid nineteen-sixties, was one of the largest things humans had then attempted in software: thousands of programmer-years poured into a single monolithic system meant to contain the whole problem. It shipped late, and its own project manager wrote a famous book about why that kind of thing goes wrong. Impressive, immovable, and only ever as good as its architects had foreseen.

Unix, a few years later, was the opposite bet — and it came from people who had earned the lesson the hard way: its authors had just walked away from Multics, an ambitious shared system of their own that had sunk under the weight of its ambitions. Don't build the one great program. Build many small tools that each do exactly one thing — find the lines matching a pattern; sort lines; count duplicates — and have them all speak the same humble language, plain text, so that the output of any one can be poured straight into the mouth of the next. That pouring is called a pipe, and it is the whole trick — capability emerging from combination rather than from construction, a kit of small sharp tools instead of a cathedral.

Which brings me to the instance that started me thinking about all this, and it is the same move a third time. Marc Andreessen has pointed out that today's AI agents, for all the architecture around them, come down to something very old: a model, a shell, a filesystem, and state kept in plain files. Strip away the elaborate scaffolding and what's left is a Unix in new clothes. And because the state lives in ordinary files rather than inside the machinery, you can swap the model underneath and the agent still remembers who it is. There's a parallel he circles from both sides but never quite closes: this is exactly the trick Unix pulled with text. A text stream is portable across tools because it commits to the humblest possible format; a file is portable across models for precisely the same reason. The humility of the format is the source of the power.

So here is the pattern in its first, intoxicating form: reduce a system to a small set of clean reusable primitives, and you unlock gains out of all proportion to what you gave up. Less is more. It is one of those principles that, once you see it, you see everywhere, and the seeing is a genuine pleasure.

It is also, stated this baldly, not quite true. Or rather — it is true only up to a point, and the point matters more than the principle. Because if simplification were pure gain all the way down, the logical endpoint would be a single instruction, a single tool, a single primitive of one bit. And obviously that way lies nothing. So there is a floor somewhere — a depth past which further reduction stops paying and starts costing — and everything interesting is in where that floor sits, and why.

That is where tokenization comes in.

## Part Three: The Floor Appears

Here is the thing almost nobody explains cleanly, and I include myself six months ago in that.

A language model does not read words. Before anything else happens, the text has to be chopped into units the model can handle — tokens — and the question of how big those units should be turns out to be one of the quietly load-bearing decisions in the whole enterprise.

Suppose you take the obvious route and make each unit a whole word. It seems natural; words are what language is made of. But the vocabulary of a language is vast and ragged — hundreds of thousands of words, most of them rare, endlessly sprouting new ones — and the model has to carry a separate learned representation for every single entry. The rare words barely appear in training, so their representations stay poor. The vocabulary is enormous and most of it is badly learned. This is the too-coarse failure, and it's the same failure RISC and Unix warned us about: an overgrown, baroque set of primitives.

So you do the simplifying thing, the thing the last section trained you to reach for. You break words into smaller, reusable pieces — subwords. The un at the front of a word; the break in the middle; the able and the ity on the end. Now a manageable stock of a few tens of thousands of fragments can compose into any word at all, including ones the model never saw in training, and each fragment appears often enough to be learned well. This is a genuine, large, RISC-shaped win. Fewer primitives, better composition, more power. Less is more, exactly as advertised.

And now the trap. If shrinking the units was such a win, why not shrink them all the way — to single characters, or raw bytes? The vocabulary would be tiny, a few dozen symbols, each one seen constantly and learned to perfection. By the logic of the last section, this should be the best of all.

It is much worse. And the reason why is the thing I actually felt, rather than merely knew, on the duvet with my index cards.

The heart of a transformer — the architecture under every model of this kind, the T in GPT — is the attention step, where every unit in a sequence compares itself against every other unit to decide what to pay attention to. Every unit against every other: that every-against-every is a multiplication. Double the number of units and you roughly quadruple the work. The cost grows with the square of the sequence length. When I tried to do my little three-word example by hand and reached for comparing everything to everything at once, this is the wall I walked into without recognising it — the combinatorial blow-up hiding inside a grid of dot products.

Now watch what shrinking the units does. A sentence that was, say, twenty words might be thirty subword tokens — or a hundred and forty characters. Going finer didn't just lengthen the sequence a little; because the cost is quadratic, a five-fold increase in length is a twenty-five-fold increase in computation. The tiny, beautifully-learned vocabulary of characters buys you a sequence so long that the attention step chokes. You have simplified your way straight off a cliff.

So tokenization sits between two failures that are not the same failure. Too coarse, and your primitives are too many and too poorly learned. Too fine, and your sequences are too long and the computation explodes. The gains from simplifying are real — right up until they reverse, and every further step of simplification now costs you. There is a floor, and it is not in the same place as the ceiling, and the whole art is in finding the sweet spot between them.

This is the shape the one-directional cases were hiding. RISC and Unix felt like pure descent into simplicity because, for the problems they addressed, we never had cause to hit the floor — nobody was tempted to reduce Unix to a single one-instruction tool. Tokenization forces the floor into view because both walls are close enough to see at once. And once you've seen the shape here, you start seeing it everywhere the earlier cases had merely hinted at it: an inverted U, a hump, a best-in-the-middle, where you climb by simplifying and then, past some point, fall by simplifying further.

The question that follows — the one that turns this from an observation into an idea — is: what determines where the top of the hump is? Why does the optimum sit exactly where it sits, and not somewhere else?

## Part Four: Carving At The Joints

I think the answer is this: the best grain is the one that matches the grain the problem already has.

Language isn't a smooth paste. It has a joint structure — the un and the ing and the able, the little reusable pieces meaning is actually assembled from. Subword tokenization wins not because smaller is magically better, but because — crudely, statistically, never once told what a morpheme is — it tends to cut near the size of language's real seams. Not on them, reliably; plenty of its pieces fall where no linguist would put them. But near enough, often enough, to beat both neighbours. Go coarser, to whole words, and you bundle distinct pieces of structure into one lump — unable to notice that unbreakable shares working parts with unbeatable. Go finer, to characters, and you've cut below the seam: a single letter carries almost no meaning, so you've dissolved the very regularities you were trying to exploit, and paid the quadratic bill for the privilege.

Plato has the phrase, in the Phaedrus: carve nature at its joints. A good butcher doesn't saw the carcass into equal cubes; he follows the joints that are already there. The right grain is a discovery about the animal, not a preference of the cook. RISC works because ordinary computation has a natural granularity — a small set of operations that genuinely recur. Unix works because tasks have natural seams you can pipe along. And — here's the thing I only understood afterwards — this is why paring my toy down hit a floor. The machinery runs perfectly well on two words; but with two there is a single pair and essentially nothing to watch, no structure for the attention to be about. Three was the fewest that had anything to show me. I had pared it down until it stopped teaching — which is its own kind of joint: the resolution below which there is simply nothing left to see.

And here is where my four numbers come back, though not in the way I first thought. There was nothing principled about my four; it was just as much arithmetic as I could stand. But it sent me looking, and I found the story of the most famous four in mathematics, which is a grain-size story to its bones. William Rowan Hamilton spent some thirteen years trying to build a number system out of triples — one ordinary part and two imaginary ones — so that he could handle rotation in three-dimensional space. It kept failing. Multiplication wouldn't behave. He could not make three work, and by his own account his sons would ask him at breakfast whether he had managed to multiply triples yet, and he would have to say no. Then, on the sixteenth of October 1843, walking along the Royal Canal into Dublin with his wife, it broke open: you need four components, not three — and you must surrender the assumption that a times b equals b times a. He carved the formula into the stone of Broom Bridge as he passed. The joint was one notch above where everyone had been digging, and reaching it cost a law of arithmetic that had felt like bedrock for two thousand years.

Even e, my old enemy from the remedial stream, turns out to be one of these. Base ten is a preference of the cook — we count on our fingers. But e is a discovery about the animal: the one base at which growth needs no correction, where the rate a thing grows is exactly the size it already is. Nobody chose two point seven one eight; it was sitting at a joint, waiting, and compounding your way toward it — faster and faster until the gains close on a ceiling — is just the sound of the knife finding it.

So the top of the hump sits where your primitives line up with the real seams of the domain. Too coarse fuses what the world keeps separate; too fine severs what the world keeps joined. The art is to find the joints.

Which raises the awkward question, and it is the one I could not get past. If the right grain is a fact about the domain, how does anyone — or anything — find it? Nobody hands you the joints. Language didn't arrive with its word-parts labelled. How do you discover the grain of something you don't yet understand well enough to see its grain?

## Part Five: The Magical Bootstrap

Here is the hole I said I'd come back to.

Two years ago I read a chapter of Max Bennett's book, A Brief History of Intelligence, on something called temporal-difference learning. It may be the most interesting thing I have ever read, and I need to tell it properly, because the gap at its centre is what this essay turned out to be about.

The chapter is about a problem called credit assignment. Animals learn from rewards, but rewards arrive late. The chess win comes fifty moves after the quiet move that earned it; the meal comes an hour after the decision to hunt in the right valley. Which choice deserves the credit? In the nineteen-eighties, Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto proposed a strange solution: don't wait for the reward. Keep a running guess of how well things are going, and treat every change in your own guess as if it were reward itself. A critic inside you predicts how the future is shaping up; when the prediction ticks upward — things are looking better — that uptick trains you, there and then. You are learning not from the world but from the movement of your own estimate of the world. It should be nonsense. The guesses are being graded by the guesses. Bennett calls it magical bootstrapping, and he is precise about why it looks impossible — and then, in a book otherwise crisp to a fault, he lets it work and sails on.

Then the part that made me put the book down. In the nineteen-nineties, Wolfram Schultz recorded from dopamine neurons in monkeys, and what he found was not a pleasure signal. The neurons fire for unexpected juice; they fall silent for juice fully predicted; they fire early, at the light that merely promises juice; and they dip — disappointed — when a promised juice never comes. That is not reward in any ordinary sense — it is closer to surprise, the gap between what was expected and what arrived. And that gap, its whole signature, matched the error term at the centre of Sutton and Barto's equations with a fidelity that stunned the people who first saw it.

And the way that got noticed is the part I cannot read without something happening at the back of my neck. Schultz was an experimentalist. He had the electrodes and the monkeys and the recordings, and he reported, accurately and without fuss, what the neurons did. Sutton had long suspected his method might have something to do with brains, and could not show it. It was one of his own colleagues, Peter Dayan, who found it — reading the monkey data and seeing their mathematics staring back out of it. Not something vaguely like the error term. Its shape, term for term. Dayan and Read Montague took it to Schultz, and in 1997 the three of them published it together.

So: engineers had derived, on paper, a fix for the credit-assignment problem — and evolution had built the same fix into the vertebrate brain half a billion years earlier. For a decade the two facts sat in different buildings, in different literatures, and nobody had noticed they were the same fact. Your own wanting — the tick of anticipation, the little lift when things start looking up, the flatness when they merely go as expected — that is the critic, grading its own guesses, running in you right now. If progress in self-knowledge is progress in wisdom, that chapter gave me as much as anything I have ever read, Proust excepted.

But at the heart of it there is a gap. How does the magic work? How can a loop leaning on its own guesses, fed by a reward that says so little, end up knowing so much — climbing to a competence that was nowhere in it at the start? Bennett names the magic and moves on. What that chapter opened in me is what I've since learned to call a curiosity gap: a question that stays live after the book is shut, that the mind keeps returning to the way the tongue returns to the socket of a missing tooth. I have been probing it for two years.

## Part Six: Where The Magic Lives

So: two questions, from opposite ends. From the tokenizer: how does anything find the right grain when nobody hands you the joints? From Bennett: how does a loop that feeds on its own guesses build something that was never in it to begin with? I carried them in separate pockets for two years.

For a while I thought they were the same question. They are not — and I have learned to be careful here, because wanting them to be the same is exactly the pull that produced my Heisenberg. But they rhyme, they share a shape, and one machine answers both. Let me build it slowly.

Bootstrapping is the move where a system leans on its own current best guess instead of waiting for an answer key. Sutton and Barto's critic, training on the movement of its own predictions. A pair of networks locked in a duel — one forging, one detecting — improving only because the other does, both from noise. And — the one I met first, thirty years before I had any of the words — the ghost car. You run a lap; the game keeps your best one; you race that recording; when you beat it, the run that beat it becomes the new ghost. Nudge, keep what improves, repeat.

The obvious worry is that a thing like this eats itself — guesses training on guesses ought to drift, the way a rumour drifts down a line of whispers into nonsense. There is a real answer to why it doesn't, which pleased me for about a day. The loop is never actually sealed. A thin thread of the real world runs through it — the reward that finally arrives, the game you win or lose, the time on the clock — and that thread keeps the whole thing pointed at the truth instead of drifting into private, self-consistent fantasy. Take it away and the loop does eat itself, decaying to noise; leave it in, sparse as it is, and it can't. Call that thread the clock.

Let me stay on the name a moment, because it isn't arbitrary. The machine I have been circling has two parts, and the game I raced as a boy had handed me both, their names already on. One was the ghost. The other sat in the top corner of the screen, counting upward in hundredths, caring nothing for how hard I tried and everything for what was true. That was the clock — the one thing in the whole arrangement that was not me, and could not be fooled. A ghost to race and a clock to keep it honest. It was all there in the cartridge, and the boy never noticed a thing.

But the clock only tells you why the loop doesn't float away; it gives it a direction, and direction was never what fascinated me, nor what Bennett meant by magic. Look at how little the clock actually says. A game of Go ends in one bit, win or lose — it does not tell you which of your two hundred moves was the good one. A lap ends in one number, and does not tell you which corner cost you the hundredth. A pile of real photographs comes with no note on what is wrong with your forgery of a face. The world, every time, teaches almost nothing. And yet the finished system ends up knowing an enormous amount — every corner, the decisive move, the exact tell in the fake. Where does that knowledge come from, if the world never supplied it?

Here is the answer, and it is the whole thing. The loop manufactures it. It takes the coarse verdict the world hands back and breaks it, by its own effort, into a fine-grained lesson the world never gave.

The ghost car is the plainest case. The stopwatch delivers one number at the end of the lap — hopelessly coarse; you cannot learn to drive from a single number. But racing the recorded ghost turns that one number into a running, corner-by-corner commentary: here you are ahead of it, here you are behind, here is precisely where you gave the time back. The ghost is not really an opponent. It is a machine for converting one useless number into a thousand useful ones. That is what I was doing on the duvet of that summer, though it would have astonished the boy to hear it: manufacturing my own teacher out of a stopwatch.

The critic does the same for a delayed reward, which is the trick Bennett called magic — a single distant payoff becomes, through the critic, a prediction at every passing moment, a whole landscape of better-than-expected and worse-than-expected laid down along the way. AlphaGo Zero has the most beautiful version. It does not merely play itself: its network proposes moves, a search explores past those proposals and returns a better answer than the network gave, and the network trains to match it — so the improved network powers a stronger search still. The pupil builds a teacher that beats the pupil, then learns from it. Nowhere in the rules of Go is there any strategy; the loop elaborates all of it out of one bit of win-or-lose, building itself a teacher one notch better than it is, over and over.

And here the grain comes back, and it was right all along; it only needed saying more carefully. The grain moves. The resolution of the lesson does not stay fixed — it gets finer as the system gets abler. The beginner's whole curriculum is: don't spin off the track; the driver I became that summer was working at: carry two more miles an hour through the apex of the third corner — a distinction the beginner could not have perceived, let alone used. Yesterday's entire task collapses into today's single available move. That is what it means for a loop to find its own grain: not that it locates one correct scale and stops, but that it keeps refining the scale, each level of competence making the next, finer level of lesson legible at all. The grain is discovered, and then discovered again, all the way up.

There is a condition on all this. The manufacturing only continues while the two halves stay close enough to keep handing each other informative errors. An opponent far too strong teaches nothing, since you lose whatever you do; one far too weak teaches nothing either. Which is why racing yourself is such a marvel: the ghost is pinned to your own level automatically and for good, because it improves at precisely your rate — the perfectly matched sparring partner, free, for life. And it is why the same setup with the balance wrong simply dies. Adversarial networks are famous for collapsing when one side overpowers the other, which is the plainest proof going that the balance was never decorative.

A ratchet holds the gains in place. What the machine keeps is your best lap, not your last; the hundreds of worse ones are simply discarded, so the benchmark only ever travels one way. And I knew that machine. The ghost was always exactly one notch better than I was — the only opponent worth having, because it was me.

Notice that two of these were not machines at all. That landscape of prediction-error is read straight off electrodes in a living midbrain — it is running in you now, as dopamine, turning the sparse rewards of a life into moment-by-moment anticipation. And the ordinary sense of being pleasantly stretched by a task is the same machine seen from the inside: what Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi named flow, the narrow channel between anxiety and boredom, which needs, he insists, both a challenge matched to your skill and clear, immediate feedback. The matched challenge is the ghost; the feedback is the clock. Both halves are already in the animal, and being built along them is a good part of what it feels like to be alive and getting better at a thing.

So is the magic dispelled? No — and Bennett was not wrong to call it magic. But it is the magic of the evolved eye, not of a sealed miracle. Every local step is explicable: a comparison, a small correction, a lesson kept because it paid. And the whole, assembled out of nothing but those steps and a thread of reward too thin to seem worth anything, is astonishing, and stays astonishing however long you stare at the mechanism. Explaining natural selection did not make the eye any less of a wonder; it told us where the wonder lived. That is all I have done, one floor down. The magic was never a gap in the machinery. It is the distance between how little goes in and how much climbs out — and that distance does not shrink when you name it.

That is the sentence I had been circling: bootstrapping is how a coarse verdict becomes a fine-grained curriculum — how a system tethered to almost nothing manufactures its own teacher and refines the grain of its own lessons, level after level, into a competence that was nowhere in it to begin with.

And this is the turn Andreessen's Unix seed was always going to take. He is right that the power lives in small reusable primitives that snap together. But the deeper question was never that they compose — it is which ones, at what grain, and where they come from. Nobody hands them to you. They are found, not given, and then found finer, by the lowliest process there is: make a rough cut, let the world grade it, keep what survives, and go again at a resolution you could not have reached before.

It is how a language grows its grammar, how a child learns to talk, how a model finds its feet — a ghost to chase, a clock to stay honest, and a lesson that sharpens every time you climb. And it asks nothing of anyone who wants in but this: start badly, let the world push back, and keep going — the grain will come.

## Part Seven: Who Found The Sentence

One question is left over, and it is stranger than it looks.

The sentence I ended on — the one about a coarse verdict becoming a fine curriculum — I did not arrive at on my own, and how it arrived is the last piece of the argument. It surfaced in a conversation, in the way I described at the start: you carry a fog of half-formed pieces, you keep talking and refusing the first tidy answer, and at some point the thing reaches across and hands you, fully formed, what you had only been able to grope toward. The nearest I'd come to that before any of this was a film critic, Stephanie Zacharek, whose reviews would say, with sometimes shocking precision, the thing I had felt about a film and couldn't get out — that same specific relief, a gap in the map closed, a notch nearer the terrain.

But the first sentence the conversation handed me was not that one. It was tidier, and it was wrong. Bootstrapping is how complexity discovers its own grain size — I loved it on sight and believed it for days, and it was a ghost-pattern of the purest kind, a shape I was consuming as signal exactly as I had with Heisenberg. What corrected it was a clock: I put the argument in front of people who knew the mathematics, and the mathematics did not hold; sent back, and back again, I found the truer and less shapely thing. Which means the essay did, in its own slow making, the precise thing it describes. The conversation was the ghost, always a notch ahead of what I could yet say, breaking my coarse fog into finer and finer sentences. The scrutiny was the clock. What you have read is the output of that loop, run over a good many laps, and I could not have designed it if I tried.

So who found the sentence? I brought the pieces, the itch, the years of circling, and — in the end — the willingness to let the tidy version be marked wrong. The machine brought the reflection that let a shape resolve, and then resolve again. I want to say I found it, and I feel the tug to add that the model was only a mirror, only matrices. But that is where the floor gives way. Because I am also matrices: a few pounds of them, wired into a loop that has spent a lifetime racing a ghost of itself against a clock, and now and then catching sight of what it is. The very line I reach for to hold the machine at arm's length — just me and some matrices — turns out to describe me just as well. Damn.

I don't know where the credit lives. I've started to suspect that isn't a defect in the question but the whole of it — that where does this sentence's authorship sit, and where does a self sit, are, underneath, one question, and that a self might be nothing grander than a loop that has found its own level: a ghost and a clock and a few pounds of matter that, between them, have begun to model themselves, at a grain fine enough to include the fact that they are doing it. But that is the next essay, and I can already feel it wanting to begin, so I'll stop at the doorway.

Except to say the thing this was quietly for. The pieces are small. The loop is humble. None of it — not the primitives, not the process — was ever the private property of the labs, or the clever, or the credentialed. Whatever you've been half-seeing in your own fog: go and talk at it, badly and out loud, and then go and check what it hands back against the world. A ghost to draw the shape, a clock to keep it honest, and a lesson that sharpens every time round. That is the method. It is old, and it is available to anyone.
