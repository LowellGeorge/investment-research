# The Ghost Car

## Part One: The Ghost

The summer of 1993 was hot, or I remember it that way, and I spent most of it indoors with the curtains half drawn.

Super Mario Kart had arrived in Britain that January. There was a mode on it called Time Trial, with the shells and the banana skins and the other eleven drivers all stripped out of it. Just you, one kart, an empty track, going round and round. On paper it is the dullest thing on the cartridge. It was the only thing I played.

Because it did have an opponent, after a fashion. The machine kept your best lap and set it running alongside you: a pale translucent copy of your own kart, taking every corner exactly the way you had taken it. You raced that. And when you beat it, the run that beat it quietly became the new thing to race. Nobody asked me. It simply moved up.

Only for as long as the power was on, mind. The cartridge had room for a single ghost in the whole game, and saving one — hold L and R and Y, press X, wait for the coin sound — wiped whatever was in there before, from whatever track. Which suited me. There was only ever one track.

Mushroom Cup, Mario Circuit One, for weeks — with one pause, on the fourth of June, for the first ball Shane Warne ever bowled in an Ashes match. And I chased hundredths of a second. Not seconds — hundredths. I learned that if you clipped one particular corner a hair tighter you came out of it fractionally quicker, and that the pale kart beside you would show you on the next lap exactly where you had found the time and exactly where you had given it back.

If I were ever stopped at the Pearly Gates and asked where, specifically, I had tried hardest in life — the thing I gave the most of myself to — the honest answer is not my education, or my work, or anything I would like it to be. It is Super Mario Kart, Time Trial, Mario Circuit One, the first course of the Mushroom Cup, summer of 1993, against a ghost of myself. And not only where I tried hardest. It is the closest I have ever come to the ceiling of a thing — the nearest I have been, before or since, to the limit of what my own hands could do.

I did not think about it again for thirty years.

## Part Two: Thirty Years Later

Then a couple of years ago I stumbled on what may be the most astonishing story I have ever read, and it sent me straight back to that bedroom.

It is in Max Bennett's book A Brief History of Intelligence, and it goes like this. Through the late 1980s and early 1990s the neurophysiologist Wolfram Schultz put electrodes into the midbrain of monkeys, recording from the cells that release dopamine — the chemical everyone had long since filed, lazily, under pleasure. He gave the monkeys a drop of juice and watched the cells fire. Fine: reward, dopamine, as expected. But then he looked more carefully at when they fired, and the pleasure story fell apart.

Three things. When the juice arrived unannounced, the neuron fired — a clean burst. When a light reliably preceded the juice, so the animal learned to expect it, the burst walked backwards in time: it left the juice and attached itself to the light, to the earliest thing that predicted the reward, and the juice itself — now fully expected — drew no response at all. And when the light came but the promised juice did not, at the exact moment the juice was due, the firing dipped below its resting rate. It went quiet in disappointment, on schedule.

Sit with those last two for a moment. The cell is not reporting reward. A fully predicted reward earns silence. The cell is reporting the gap between what was predicted and what arrived — surprise upward, disappointment downward, silence when the world unfolds exactly as guessed. It is not a pleasure signal. It is an error signal.

Read Montague, Peter Dayan and Terrence Sejnowski saw what it was. Years earlier Richard Sutton — one of the founders of reinforcement learning — had written down, as pure mathematics, an algorithm for learning to predict delayed rewards. Its central quantity is the moment-to-moment change in predicted reward — the temporal difference — and a learner that chases that quantity will, given time, teach itself the value of every step on a long path to a far-off payoff. Schultz's dopamine neuron was computing Sutton's term. The equation was already running, in a monkey's midbrain, hundreds of millions of years before anyone wrote it down. And it's there in yours right now, delivering that slight pall of disappointment caused by the fact that you don't agree, hopefully yet, that this is the most astonishing thing you have ever read. That flatness is the very dip I described a moment ago: a predicted reward that has not arrived. Reading that line, just now, you were the disappointed monkey.

Bennett lays the machinery out cleanly. Two parts. The actor is the basal ganglia — striatum, pallidum and the dopamine supply that feeds them, present and recognisable in the lamprey, which parted company from us something like 560 million years ago, and older by far than any monkey. It spends its life inhibiting, holding down every possible action at once, and choosing by releasing its grip on one of them. The critic is the dopamine system, scoring each of the actor's choices against its own shifting prediction of the reward. An actor that acts and a critic that grades — and the signal the actor learns from is the change in predicted reward from one moment to the next. No one outside is grading the moves. The standard the actor is held to is one the brain raises for itself, and as the actor gets better the standard rises to meet it — on wet tissue, a very long time before anybody thought to put one in a game.

## Part Three: The Thing Bennett Walks Past

Bennett calls this a magical bootstrapping. And then he moves on — as he must, with several hundred million years of brain evolution still ahead of him, and he covers that ground well. I could not move on.

He lays the difficulty out plainly. A position is only as good as the actor's ability to exploit it, so the critic's judgement is hostage to the actor's skill; but the actor can learn only from a critic whose judgements are sound, so its skill is hostage to the critic. Each one's competence depends on the other's.

Nobody supplies the value of every position. There is no answer key, and no adult in the room saying this much and no more. The world contributes an occasional verdict — the juice, the win, the lap time — and leaves actor and critic to manufacture almost everything in between. Somehow the pair climb instead of merely confirming one another's first mistakes.

That it works is settled — in simulation, and then on a backgammon board, where a program that taught itself by Sutton's method came within a single point of a world champion over forty games, and rewrote the opening theory of something people had been studying for centuries. But that it works is not why. Naming the convergence is not the same as showing what stops a bootstrapped error from becoming the teacher of the next one.

And this same humble loop is a large part of how the machines learned backgammon, then Go, and then almost everything else. Magic is a fair word for it. But magic is only a trick you have not been shown yet. So: how does it actually work?

## Part Four: Inside the Loop

There are three things that ought to stop this working, and a thirteen-year-old solved all three without noticing. If it starts to feel like machinery, remember that every piece of it is something you have already done.

First problem. If I only ever correct my guesses against my own next guess, I am marking my own homework — I could settle into a set of beliefs that all agree with each other and are quietly, completely wrong. So why don't I?

Because the lap ends. However I kid myself about how it is going, the clock eventually stops on a real number, and that number does not care what I believed. It corrects the guess I made just before it. Next lap, the guess I make one corner earlier is checked against that now-corrected one — so a little truth has moved a corner back up the track. The lap after, another corner. Nothing runs backwards in time; I just drive the lap again and again, and the one hard fact waiting at the finish line seeps one corner further back each time, like a stain wicking up a rope. Race a track enough and the truth of the finish has reached all the way to the start. My guesses only ever check against each other — which sounds sealed shut, until you follow the chain to its end and find the last link nailed to what actually happened.

The machinery, for anyone who wants it: the guesses live in a table that survives between laps — one number per corner, your running estimate of the time still to come. The finish is fixed and real; each lap drags the numbers a little further into line with it, end first. That the table persists between laps is the whole trick. But you don't need the table. You need the thumbs.

There is no fixed teacher. There is a fixed point. Nobody stands over the track holding the right answer. And yet a right answer exists — one set of corner values that all square with each other and with the finish — and the whole apparatus is feeling its way towards it in the dark.

Second problem, and this is the subtle one. That right answer is only right for the driver I am. The values I so carefully settle are the truth about a clumsy boy — and the entire point is that I am becoming a less clumsy one. So the moment I improve, everything I learned is a shade stale: it describes a driver who no longer exists. The target got up and walked off just as I reached it. Now two things move at once, my skill and my read on my skill, each chasing the other, with no clear reason they ever meet.

They meet because one of them moves slowly. I do not turn into a different driver each lap; I improve by fractions, while my sense of the track updates constantly. So my read is fast and my skill is slow, and across the many laps it takes me to get meaningfully better, that read has revised itself over and over and very nearly caught up. From its point of view I am almost standing still — slow enough to be measured. It is the honest move a chemist makes when she solves for the fast electrons with the slow nucleus held fixed. The nucleus is moving. It is just so slow, beside an electron, that pretending otherwise costs nothing. Konda and Tsitsiklis proved the careful version around the turn of the century, and what they proved is modest: hold the two speeds far enough apart and the thing stops thrashing — it keeps returning to the neighbourhood of a decent answer. No promise that it settles there, and none at all that the answer is the best available.

Third problem. Grant both of those — the guesses are anchored, the two speeds don't spiral. I still have not shown the boy gets better. He could be perfectly steady and perfectly stuck.

He climbs because of one guarantee. At each corner I have a rough sense of which line is worth more, tight or wide, and the rule is simply: take the better one. The guarantee — the single hard-edged fact in all of this — is that a driver who switches to the better-rated line at every corner cannot come out slower than before, and comes out faster unless he was already perfect. So it ratchets. Drive, see which lines paid, favour those, drive again. Each pass leaves me at least as good and usually better, and the gain never has to be given back. A theorem, running in a thirteen-year-old's thumbs.

An anchor, a gap between two speeds, a ratchet. Between them they explain why the loop holds together. They do not explain why it gets rich — why a thing that started with one bit a game ends up knowing a hundred thousand things. That comes from the two halves quietly setting each other's homework. A slightly better critic lets the actor tell apart two moves it had been treating as identical. Acting on that difference takes it somewhere it has never been, which the critic has never had to judge. Judging it sharpens the critic, whose new distinctions change the actor again. Neither of them is following a syllabus. Each is writing the other's.

Which is where the ghost comes in, and not where the shape of this essay has been implying. The ghost is no part of the critic. It computes nothing, and it does no judging at all. Its job is narrower, and it is the one nobody else does: it keeps the next target within reach. That is the hardest practical problem in any self-improving system — finding something to measure yourself against. Too strong and you lose every time, learning nothing; too weak and you win every time, learning nothing. You want an opponent pitched just at the edge of what you can do, and a recording of your own last run is exactly that, for free. It is the best you have already been: close enough to read, far enough to show the gap. Nobody has to calibrate it, and it re-levels itself the instant you improve. That is how a summer of hundredths compounds into something absurd, and how a machine playing itself can start from nothing and finish beyond every human alive.

None of the three guarantees is a licence for confidence. They are cleanest in the small, exact case; put a large neural network in the middle and several of them soften into rules of thumb that usually hold. The theory explains why the trick can work, and leaves unexplained why it works as well as it does at the scale we run it now.

One last distinction, because it keeps the movement straight. The reward is fixed. Whatever the world pays out at the end, it pays the same amount to a novice and to a champion. The value of a position moves — it means what you can expect from here given the way you currently play, so the champion's winning board really is worth less in the novice's hands, and playing better makes every position on the board worth more than it was. But above both sits what a position is worth under the best possible play, and that does not move at all. It was set by the game itself, the way the fastest possible lap of Mario Circuit One was already fixed in the code Nintendo shipped, before anybody had driven one. So the critic spends its life chasing something that keeps running away from it — always upward, and always toward a ceiling that was there before any of this began.

## Part Five: Two Powers

Everything so far has been one engine, and an ancient one. The ratchet has been turning in vertebrate brains for something like half a billion years — that is the whole force of Bennett's story, and it is why the machinery turns up in a lamprey. What has been built in the last ten years was not built by it alone. Nobody recently discovered the ratchet. What changed is that there was finally a second engine to couple it to.

That second engine has been standing just off the page for the whole of the last section. Every time I said, unlike a system handed an answer key, that was it, holding the door.

It is gradient descent, and the whole of it fits in a paragraph. Imagine being set down blindfolded on a hillside with no map and told to get to the bottom. You reach out a foot; you feel whether the ground there is lower; if it is, you step to it. Then again. That is the entire algorithm. It requires you to understand nothing whatever about the hill, and it will reliably walk you to the bottom of whatever valley you happen to have landed in.

Now put that at the heart of a neural network. Start with a pile of data and a model that is nothing but random numbers in a mess. Define the loss — the gap between what the model produced and what was wanted — and treat that as your height on the hill. Nudge every weight slightly in whatever direction lowers it, and repeat until the electricity runs out. Backpropagation is the trick that lets you feel the slope in every direction at once instead of one foot at a time, which is the only reason any of this is feasible in a space of a billion dimensions. And that is all of it. That is what trained the attention layers; it is how a network learns language, or a face; it is the whole of modern pretraining, and everything that has come out of a chat window came out of that loop.

There is no bootstrap anywhere in it. Nothing hauls on its own laces. The ground holds still, and something walks patiently downhill toward an answer that was already there.

And the ground was holding still in the bedroom too. That ceiling I mentioned a moment ago, the fastest lap the code would permit, was fixed the day the cartridge was pressed, and every scraped hundredth was a step down toward it. What I was doing was the blindfolded version, precisely: try a variation, keep it if the number falls, feel for the next one. One foot, one corner, one lap. I had no gradient and no way of getting one — I could not feel the slope in every direction at once, which is the whole distance between a boy on a carpet and a training run. It is the same instinct, though, and descent is what that instinct becomes once somebody works out how to give it a billion feet.

So both engines were in the room, and neither was doing much. A fixed floor being felt for one step at a time, and a pale kart that raised its own bar every time I chose to save it. On the same lap, and a boy noticing neither.

They are not the same power. Descent walks toward something that was already there and does not care how the walk goes; it can be very hard to do well, and large training runs come off the rails often enough, but it cannot talk itself into a mistake. The ratchet has no valley; it lifts the floor beneath its own feet, and it is the delicate one, the one that needs a thin line of outside truth and a difference in speeds even to hold together.

Deep reinforcement learning — actor and critic no longer looking answers up in a table, but computing them with a neural network — is the two of them married. Descent supplies machinery that can judge a position it has never seen before; the bootstrap supplies the ever-rising target for it to walk toward. AlphaGo Zero runs the same marriage with the parts arranged differently. Its improvement comes from search: the machine looks ahead through millions of continuations, steered at every branch by the network's own hunches about which moves are worth trying and how good the resulting positions look, and arrives somewhere better than the network could have reached in a single glance. Then the tree is discarded. Nothing is kept unless the network is trained toward what the search found — which is plain descent, target fixed, walking downhill. Improvement discovered by search, stored by descent; and the better network makes the next search stronger still. Self-play as an upward ratchet, each rung laid down by descent. The reasoning models are the same marriage carried up into language: a transformer laid down by descent over the whole written world, then lifted by a reinforcement loop that rewards the chains of reasoning which turn out to arrive somewhere checkable.

Neither engine alone gets you here. Both together — with enough silicon to run on and enough electricity to burn — are why the ground has come up the way it has. The Himalayas are only what happens when two things that were travelling separately collide, and the sole remaining direction is up.

I could not have told you any of this at thirteen. I could not have told you what a value function was, or that there was such a thing as a moving target, or that anything at all was being manufactured. I pressed a button and watched a number. What I noticed was that the number came down when I took one particular corner differently, and that the pale kart showed me where it had come down. That is the whole of what I knew. It took thirty years and somebody else's book to tell me what I had been looking at.

Which is a claim about how much of this sits in plain sight, in a toy, waiting for somebody to hand you the words.

## Part Six: It Was Always Games

One last thing.

By 1969 a programmer at Bell Labs named Ken Thompson had written a game called Space Travel — a little simulation of the solar system, in which you flew a ship about and tried to set it down on the various moons. The trouble was that every go cost something like seventy-five dollars of the lab's internal money. So he found a neglected PDP-7 in a corner with a good display hanging off it, and set about moving his game onto it, which turned out to mean writing from scratch a floating-point package, the shapes of the characters on the screen, and a debugger — all of it prepared on another machine and walked over on paper tape. The game took him to that corner of the room and made him fluent in the machine sitting in it, and it was there, a few months later, that he wrote the first version of Unix. Most of the internet now runs on its descendants.

Thirty years on, two friends from Cambridge were making video games for a living: Demis Hassabis, who had been designing them since he was a teenager, and David Silver, who was his lead programmer. Silver left for Alberta to take a doctorate under Richard Sutton — on temporal difference learning, turned loose on the game of Go. The acknowledgements read, in part: Rich Sutton has been a constant source of inspiration and wisdom. And, a little later: I'd also like to thank Gerry Tesauro for his keen insights and many constructive suggestions. The lineage is people, thanked by name, on a page. Some years later the two friends were back in the same building, in a company whose whole method was to build things that got good at games. Getting good at games turned out to be the whole of it. Watching them do it, and being frightened by it, is why much of the rest of the industry exists at all.

Which is neither coincidence nor quite a joke. Think about what this machinery actually needs in order to run. It needs repetition, because it climbs in tiny increments, each one measured against its own last attempt. And it needs an honest verdict from somewhere outside itself — one number nobody can argue with — or the chain of guesses curls up and begins certifying its own mistakes. Very little in ordinary life supplies both. A game supplies both by construction: a small world you can enter again, and again, and again, and a score at the end of it that will not be sweet-talked. So a game is where the ratchet — an actor getting better, a critic re-grading it, the standard rising underneath them both — has room to turn.

That is half of it. The other half is why anyone was in the room to begin with. Remember what the burst did when the light came on. It moved. It left the juice and attached itself to the promise of the juice. Which is a peculiar fact about you: you are not paid when you win. You are paid at the moment winning becomes likely. And the cells doing the paying are the cells doing the teaching. Anticipation and instruction are one mechanism, felt from the inside and measured from the outside. The reason a game teaches you and the reason you cannot put it down are the same reason — which is how a boy could give a whole summer to one lap of one track, get steadily better at it, and never once experience either thing as work.

That summer, none of the rest of it had happened. At IBM a physicist named Gerald Tesauro was watching TD-Gammon climb to a level he had not believed a machine could reach, and would take it to Bill Robertie, twice a world champion, and lose by one point across forty games. The monkeys had just given up the middle finding — Schultz's paper showing the burst walking backwards onto the light appeared that spring; the dip below the resting rate took another five years. AlphaGo was twenty-three summers off. A few weeks earlier, in a Denny's in San Jose, Jensen Huang and two friends had founded a company to build better graphics chips for video games, which, given everything above, may be the least surprising sentence in this essay. The whole internet had about fourteen million people on it, and had begun that year with fewer than a hundred websites.

And in a bedroom with the curtains still half drawn, a record bought that June was playing for the hundredth time, and the sparkling electric piano at the top of Blow Your Mind came out of a small speaker while he lined up one more lap.

He had no idea. He was just going round again.

## A Footnote About the Record

Blow Your Mind runs to eight and a half minutes, which in 1993 might as well have been half an hour, and which is about six complete Time Trials of Mario Circuit One. The record did not so much accompany that summer as measure it out. I would like to report that I noticed the bassline climbing under the chorus, or the synth line above it — thin, exact, barely there, and somehow summer itself, as everyone from Kool and the Gang to Jazzy Jeff has known. I noticed none of it. I was looking at a number in the corner of a screen.
